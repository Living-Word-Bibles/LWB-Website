Common Christian Prayers

The Lord’s Prayer

Our Father, who art in heaven, hallowed be thy name; thy kingdom come; thy will be done; on earth as it is in heaven. Give us this day our daily bread. And forgive us our trespasses, as we forgive those who trespass against us. And lead us not into temptation; but deliver us from evil. For thine is the kingdom, the power and the glory, for ever and ever. Amen.

About The Lord’s Prayer

The Lord’s Prayer stands at the center of Christian prayer because the Gospels present it as a prayer taught by Jesus himself. Matthew places the prayer within the Sermon on the Mount, where Jesus warns against prayer performed for public admiration and against empty multiplication of words. Luke gives a shorter form after a disciple asks Jesus, “Lord, teach us to pray.” These two settings show both sides of the prayer’s importance: it is a model for the inner disposition of prayer and a concrete form Christians can actually pray together.

The opening words, “Our Father,” locate Christian prayer within relationship rather than mere religious technique. The prayer is communal—“our,” not simply “my”—and it addresses God with reverence and trust. “Hallowed be thy name” asks that God’s holiness be recognized; “thy kingdom come” and “thy will be done” place God’s purposes ahead of the worshiper’s preferences. The first movement of the prayer therefore turns the heart toward God before turning to human need.

The petitions that follow are deliberately ordinary and comprehensive. “Daily bread” gathers up dependence on God for the necessities of life while also reminding Christians that material provision is received, not possessed absolutely. The request for forgiveness is inseparable from the command to forgive others. In Matthew, Jesus immediately returns to this point after the prayer, emphasizing that reconciliation is not an optional addition to discipleship. The petitions concerning temptation and evil acknowledge moral vulnerability and ask for God’s protection in the struggle to remain faithful.

The familiar concluding doxology—“For thine is the kingdom, the power and the glory”—has an important textual history. It is absent from the earliest recoverable text of Matthew in many modern critical editions, yet it entered Christian worship very early. The Didache, an early Christian church manual, already preserves a doxological conclusion to the prayer. For many Protestant Christians the doxology is an ordinary part of the spoken Lord’s Prayer; Roman Catholic liturgy places a related doxology after an embolism following the prayer. The difference is liturgical and textual, not a disagreement about God’s sovereignty.

Across Christian history the Lord’s Prayer has served as catechism, liturgy, private devotion, and theological outline. Church fathers such as Tertullian, Cyprian, Origen, Augustine, and John Chrysostom expounded it. Reformers including Martin Luther and John Calvin did the same. Its endurance comes partly from its balance: adoration and petition, heaven and earth, present need and future kingdom, forgiveness received and forgiveness given.

Its communal grammar also matters. Even when one person prays alone, the words remain plural: our Father, give us, forgive us, deliver us. The prayer resists a spirituality concerned only with private needs. It teaches believers to approach God as members of a people whose hunger, failures, forgiveness, temptations, and hope are shared. That feature helps explain why the prayer has been so durable in public worship across Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican, Lutheran, Reformed, Methodist, Baptist, and other Christian traditions.

For Bible study, the prayer should be read in context, especially Matthew 6:5–15 and Luke 11:1–13. It is not merely a set of lines to recite quickly. Each petition can become a doorway into Scripture: God’s fatherhood, holiness, kingdom, providence, forgiveness, spiritual testing, and deliverance. Prayed slowly, the Lord’s Prayer teaches Christians not only what to ask for, but what kind of people prayer is meant to form.

Bibliography & Further Reading

  • The Holy Bible, Matthew 6:5–15 and Luke 11:1–13. The two canonical Gospel settings for the prayer and the primary basis for its interpretation.
  • Catechism of the Catholic Church, Part Four, Section Two: “The Lord’s Prayer.” A detailed theological exposition of the prayer’s address and petitions.
  • United States Conference of Catholic Bishops, “Evening Prayer (Vespers).” Describes the Lord’s Prayer in the daily liturgical life of the Church.
  • “Lord’s Prayer, The,” New Catholic Encyclopedia / Encyclopedia.com. A reference treatment comparing the Matthean and Lukan forms and their liturgical history.
  • Raymond E. Brown, “The Pater Noster as an Eschatological Prayer,” Theological Studies 22 (1961). A classic scholarly study of the prayer’s kingdom-oriented and eschatological dimensions.
