Song of Solomon

Overview and Place in Scripture

The Song of Solomon, also called the Song of Songs or Canticles, is a collection of some of the Bible’s most vivid love poetry. Its Hebrew title, Shir ha-Shirim, is a superlative meaning “the greatest song” or “song of songs.” Unlike most biblical books, it contains no sustained narrative, law code, historical chronicle, or explicit sermon. Instead, lovers speak, seek, praise, remember, and delight in one another through gardens, vineyards, spices, animals, jewelry, landscapes, and the rhythms of spring. The woman’s voice is especially prominent, an unusual feature within the biblical canon. In the Hebrew Bible the Song belongs to the Writings and is associated liturgically with Passover. Christian Bibles traditionally place it among the poetic or wisdom books. Its presence in Scripture has generated centuries of reflection on human love, marriage, desire, covenant, and the relationship between literal and spiritual interpretation.

Solomon, Authorship, and Composition

The opening line associates the Song with Solomon, but the precise force of the Hebrew phrase can be read in more than one way: a song “of,” “for,” or “concerning” Solomon. Traditional Jewish and Christian interpretation commonly attributed the book to Solomon, whose biblical reputation includes wisdom, poetry, wealth, and many marriages. Modern scholars usually treat the collection as anonymous and debate whether Solomon functions as author, dedicatee, character, or literary symbol. The poems contain features that have led many interpreters to propose a date after Solomon, though precise dating remains uncertain because poetry can preserve archaic forms and borrow vocabulary across regions. The book may also have developed as a collection rather than as one composition written at a single moment. What matters for interpretation is that the final form is carefully arranged: repeated refrains, recurring speakers, garden imagery, search scenes, and descriptions of the beloved create coherence even where a strict plot is difficult to reconstruct.

Poetic Form, Speakers, and Structure

The Song moves among a woman, a man, and groups such as the “daughters of Jerusalem.” Modern translations often identify speakers typographically, but the Hebrew text itself does not provide modern stage directions, so some assignments remain debated. The book includes wasf poems—extended descriptions of a beloved’s body—as well as invitation songs, search scenes, recollections, and celebrations of mutual desire. Refrains such as “do not stir up or awaken love until it pleases” mark transitions and give the collection a musical quality. Attempts to turn the Song into a complete drama with a fixed cast have rarely won broad consensus, because the sequence works more naturally as lyric poetry. Images reappear in transformed ways: vineyards can be literal and symbolic, gardens evoke fertility and delight, and the enclosed garden of chapter 4 joins intimacy with exclusiveness. Readers should therefore allow metaphor to work imaginatively rather than forcing every comparison into a literal visual diagram.

Desire, Mutuality, and the Woman’s Voice

One of the Song’s most striking features is its frank celebration of mutual attraction. The woman is not merely an object described by a male speaker; she desires, initiates, searches, invites, praises, and speaks extensively in her own voice. “My beloved is mine and I am his” expresses reciprocity rather than possession in only one direction. The poetry delights in bodies without reducing them to commodities. Beauty is described through images drawn from the land—doves, goats, pomegranates, palms, spices, mountains, and pools—so erotic love is woven into the goodness of creation. At the same time, modern readers should approach ancient beauty language critically and avoid turning culturally specific metaphors into universal standards of appearance. The Song’s strongest ethical contribution lies in mutual delight, consent-like reciprocity, longing without coercion, and the refusal to portray desire itself as shameful. Within Scripture, embodied love is treated as worthy of poetry.

Love, Marriage, and Interpretive Caution

The Song is often read in wedding settings, and many Christian traditions understandably connect its celebration of exclusive love with marriage. Yet the book itself does not give a modern marriage ceremony, household code, or explicit theology of matrimony. Responsible interpretation should distinguish what the poetry directly says from later applications. The lovers appear deeply committed and repeatedly seek one another, but the Song’s literary world is intentionally lyrical rather than legal. This caution matters because readers sometimes recruit the text to support claims it was not written to settle. Its enduring value for marriage lies less in proof-texts than in the portrayal of attentive speech, admiration, mutual longing, delight, absence, reunion, and the extraordinary strength of love. The climactic declaration in 8:6–7 compares love to death in its power and says floods cannot extinguish it. Love here is not trivial romance; it is a force that commands reverence and cannot be purchased.

Allegorical and Spiritual Readings

For much of Jewish and Christian history, the Song was read primarily or extensively as an allegory of divine love. Jewish interpreters associated the lovers with God and Israel, often connecting images to exodus, Sinai, temple, and covenant. Christian interpreters read the bridegroom as Christ and the bride as the Church, the soul, or both. Origen’s commentary and homilies helped establish a rich Christian tradition in which the Song became a major text for mystical theology. Medieval writers, including Bernard of Clairvaux, preached long series of sermons on only the opening chapters. These readings should not simply be mocked as premodern mistakes; they testify to how communities received the book canonically. Still, spiritual interpretation is strongest when it does not erase the poem’s literal celebration of human love. A canonical reading can recognize that embodied covenant love may itself become an analogy for divine-human communion without pretending that every pomegranate or gazelle encodes a secret doctrine.

Connections with Genesis and Wisdom

The Song contains no explicit retelling of Genesis, yet many readers hear echoes of Eden in its gardens, fruit, naked delight, animals, flowing water, and lovers who encounter one another without the shame that dominates the post-fall story. Whether or not every echo was consciously intended, the canonical juxtaposition is suggestive: the Bible that tells of fractured desire also preserves poetry in which desire can be joyful, mutual, and life-giving. The Song also shares the wisdom tradition’s interest in skilled living, disciplined speech, timing, and the power of passions. Its repeated warning not to awaken love prematurely treats desire as a good force that nonetheless possesses its own season and intensity. The book therefore belongs beside Proverbs and Ecclesiastes in a broad biblical wisdom conversation, but it contributes through lyric imagination rather than instruction. It teaches not by rules alone but by training readers to perceive beauty, longing, fidelity, and the danger of trying to buy what can only be given.

Why the Song Matters

The Song of Songs matters because it refuses the idea that holiness requires contempt for the body. It gives biblical space to attraction, beauty, longing, touch, absence, and reunion while portraying love as something stronger than wealth and not reducible to acquisition. It also gives unusual prominence to a woman’s desire and speech, making mutuality central to the book’s emotional world. For modern Christian readers, the Song can correct both prudishness and consumerist sexuality. Desire is not treated as dirty, but neither is it casual or purchasable. Love has timing, exclusiveness, vulnerability, and enormous power. The book also invites believers to consider why Scripture preserves poetry that does not constantly explain itself theologically. Beauty can instruct. Metaphor can awaken gratitude. Human love can be honored as part of creation and, within the wider canon, can become a window through which communities contemplate the faithful love of God without dissolving the human poem that made the analogy possible.

Annotated Bibliography (Chicago Style)

  • Pope, Marvin H. Song of Songs: A New Translation with Introduction and Commentary. Anchor Bible 7C. Garden City, NY: Doubleday, 1977. Monumental philological and comparative study of the Song’s language, imagery, ancient context, and reception.
  • Exum, J. Cheryl. Song of Songs: A Commentary. Old Testament Library. Louisville: Westminster John Knox Press, 2005. Major literary and gender-aware commentary emphasizing voice, desire, poetry, and the woman’s prominence.
  • Longman III, Tremper. Song of Songs. New International Commentary on the Old Testament. Grand Rapids: Eerdmans, 2001. Evangelical commentary on poetic structure, human love, theology, and canonical interpretation.
  • Murphy, Roland E. The Song of Songs: A Commentary on the Book of Canticles or The Song of Songs. Hermeneia. Minneapolis: Fortress Press, 1990. Critical commentary on text, genre, structure, love poetry, and interpretation history.
  • Keel, Othmar. The Song of Songs: A Continental Commentary. Minneapolis: Fortress Press, 1994. Rich treatment of ancient Near Eastern iconography, landscape, body imagery, and poetic symbolism.
  • Garrett, Duane, and Paul R. House. Song of Songs/Lamentations. Word Biblical Commentary 23B. Nashville: Thomas Nelson, 2004. Detailed exegetical treatment of the Song alongside Lamentations, with literary and theological attention.
  • Yale Bible Study. “Song of Songs, Part 1 – Study Guide.” yalebiblestudy.org. Accessed August 28, 2026. Academic introduction to genre, speakers, Solomon, and the book’s literary character.
  • Yale Bible Study. “Song of Songs, Part 2 – Study Guide.” yalebiblestudy.org. Accessed August 28, 2026. Discussion of beauty, metaphor, sensory imagery, bodies, and interpretive questions.
