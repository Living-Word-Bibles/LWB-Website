1 Samuel

Read 1 Samuel Now

Overview and Place in Scripture

First Samuel tells the story of Israel’s transition from the era of the judges to monarchy. In the Jewish canon Samuel was originally one book among the Former Prophets; the division into two books came later. The book begins with Hannah’s prayer for a child and the birth of Samuel, moves through the decline of Eli’s priestly house, the rise and rejection of Saul, and the emergence of David, and ends with Saul’s death on Mount Gilboa. Philistine pressure is strong, tribal leadership is fragmented, and Israel debates what kind of king it should have. First Samuel is therefore not a simple celebration of monarchy. It asks whether royal power can coexist with covenant obedience and whether leaders will listen to prophetic correction.

Literary Structure

The book can be read in four major movements. Chapters 1–7 focus on Samuel: his birth, call, prophetic authority, the corruption of Eli’s sons, the capture and return of the ark, and Israel’s deliverance from the Philistines. Chapters 8–15 narrate the rise of Saul, including the people’s demand for a king, Saul’s anointing, early victories, and the failures that lead Samuel to declare his dynasty rejected. Chapters 16–27 introduce David, whose rise increasingly contrasts with Saul’s decline. Chapters 28–31 describe Saul’s final crisis, the medium at Endor, and defeat by the Philistines. The narrative repeatedly contrasts leaders so that power is evaluated through obedience, humility, and response to God’s word.

Authorship, Date, and Composition

First Samuel does not name its final author. Jewish tradition has associated Samuel, Nathan, and Gad with traditions behind the books, partly on the basis of 1 Chronicles 29:29, but Samuel dies in 1 Samuel 25 and cannot be the author of the complete work. Modern scholarship commonly reads Samuel within the Deuteronomistic History, which interprets monarchy through themes related to Deuteronomy. At the same time, the book appears to preserve older materials of different kinds: ark traditions, stories of Saul, accounts of David’s rise, poems, battle narratives, genealogical notices, and court memories. The final narrative often allows different viewpoints to stand in tension rather than forcing a simple judgment.

Hannah, Samuel, and the Reversal of Power

The book opens not with kings but with Hannah, a woman enduring infertility and household rivalry. Her prayer at Shiloh is initially misunderstood by Eli, but she is eventually blessed and gives birth to Samuel. Hannah then dedicates the child to the Lord. Her song in 1 Samuel 2 praises God for reversing human fortunes and raising the lowly. The song becomes a theological overture for the entire book. Saul will rise and fall; David will rise from obscurity; established priestly and royal houses will be judged. Christian readers hear echoes of Hannah’s song in Mary’s Magnificat in Luke 1. Samuel himself grows into prophet, judge, and kingmaker, embodying a form of authority that stands outside monarchy and can call kings to account.

The Ark and the Holiness of God

First Samuel 4–7 tells how Israel carries the ark of the covenant into battle as though sacred presence could guarantee military success. The Philistines capture it, Eli’s sons die, and the news leads to Eli’s death. Yet the Philistines discover that possessing the ark is no victory: their god Dagon falls before it, and calamity afflicts Philistine cities until the ark is returned. The episode rejects treating holy objects as magical tools. Neither Israel nor the Philistines can control God through ritual or possession. Sacred presence is powerful, but it demands reverence and cannot substitute for covenant faithfulness.

“Give Us a King”

In 1 Samuel 8 the elders ask Samuel to appoint a king “like all the nations.” The request receives a deliberately ambivalent response. On one hand, monarchy can provide military leadership and political continuity. On the other, Samuel warns that kings “will take”: sons for armies, daughters for service, fields, vineyards, servants, livestock, and produce. God tells Samuel that the people have rejected divine kingship, yet the request is granted. Saul is genuinely chosen and empowered by God, but monarchy is never absolute. The king remains subordinate to prophetic word and covenant obligation. First Samuel therefore reflects searchingly on political power: a necessary institution can still become dangerous when authority ceases to listen.

Saul: Promise, Failure, and Tragedy

Saul is introduced as physically impressive and initially modest. He receives divine Spirit, leads Israel to victory, and begins with real promise. His downfall is gradual and tragic. In 1 Samuel 13 he offers sacrifice when Samuel is delayed; in chapter 15 he fails to carry out the command concerning Amalek and then attempts to justify his actions. Samuel’s response—“to obey is better than sacrifice”—becomes the theological verdict. Saul’s rejection also raises moral questions because the commands concerning Amalek involve severe violence. Within the narrative, however, the issue is royal autonomy: Saul repeatedly acts as though kingship permits him to redefine divine instruction. Saul becomes fearful, jealous, and unstable, yet the book preserves sympathy for his tragedy.

The Rise of David

David enters in chapter 16 through anointing at Bethlehem, but First Samuel preserves more than one introduction to him. He appears as the youngest son chosen over older brothers, as a musician in Saul’s court, and as the youth who defeats Goliath. David’s victory over Goliath establishes courage and trust, while his friendship with Jonathan creates one of Scripture’s most moving bonds of loyalty. As David’s popularity grows, Saul sees him as a threat and repeatedly attempts to kill him. David becomes a fugitive, gathers followers, spares Saul when able to take revenge, and demonstrates political skill. Yet the narrative does not make him flawless: his years among the Philistines show ambiguity, deception, and survival strategy as he moves toward the throne.

Jonathan, Abigail, and Moral Restraint

Several supporting characters reveal what faithful restraint looks like amid escalating conflict. Jonathan recognizes David’s future and repeatedly protects him despite being Saul’s son and natural heir. Abigail intervenes when David intends to massacre Nabal’s household after an insult, persuading him not to shed blood in personal vengeance. These episodes ask what kind of conduct should mark a future ruler. David’s refusal to kill Saul, described as “the LORD’S anointed,” similarly delays the seizure of power. The narrative idealizes patience more than ambition: legitimate kingship is to be received rather than grasped through murder.

Saul’s Final Night and Death

Near the end of the book Saul faces a Philistine army without Samuel, without clear divine guidance, and without confidence. Although he had expelled mediums and necromancers, he disguises himself and consults a woman at Endor, asking her to summon Samuel. Samuel’s message offers no escape: Saul and his sons will die. The king who once stood tall before Israel now spends the night terrified and exhausted. Chapter 31 then records Saul’s death with his sons on Mount Gilboa. His enemies display his body, but men of Jabesh-gilead recover and bury the remains, remembering Saul’s earlier rescue of their city.

Why First Samuel Matters

First Samuel is one of the Bible’s deepest studies of leadership. Priests, prophets, kings, warriors, women, and household figures are all measured by how they use power and respond to God. The book neither romanticizes monarchy nor rejects political order. Instead, it insists that rulers remain accountable, worship cannot replace obedience, and charisma does not guarantee character. It also shows that God’s purposes may advance through unexpected people: Hannah, the overlooked David, Jonathan, Abigail, and others who act faithfully outside obvious centers of authority. For Christians, David’s rise matters for later messianic expectation, but the book should not be reduced to a prelude to Jesus. In its own right it is a account of prayer, vocation, friendship, jealousy, restraint, failure, and the transition from one form of leadership to another.

Annotated Bibliography (Chicago Style)

  • Tsumura, David Toshio. The First Book of Samuel. New International Commentary on the Old Testament. Grand Rapids: Eerdmans, 2007. Major commentary with special strength in Hebrew philology, textual criticism, Philistine and Canaanite background, literary structure, and theology.
  • McCarter, P. Kyle, Jr. I Samuel: A New Translation with Introduction, Notes and Commentary. Anchor Bible 8. Garden City, NY: Doubleday, 1980. A classic critical commentary on the complex Hebrew text, source traditions, monarchy narratives, and historical background.
  • Brueggemann, Walter. First and Second Samuel. Interpretation. Louisville: John Knox Press, 1990. A theological and literary commentary focusing on kingship, prophetic authority, political power, David, Saul, and the canonical significance of the Samuel narratives.
  • Alter, Robert. The David Story: A Translation with Commentary of 1 and 2 Samuel. New York: W. W. Norton, 1999. Especially valuable for the narrative art, characterization, dialogue, irony, ambiguity, and literary realism of Samuel.
  • Bergen, Robert D. 1, 2 Samuel. New American Commentary 7. Nashville: Broadman & Holman, 1996. An evangelical exegetical commentary covering historical setting, Hebrew details, theology, leadership, and canonical interpretation.
  • Gordon, Robert P. I & II Samuel: A Commentary. Library of Biblical Interpretation. Grand Rapids: Zondervan, 1986. A compact scholarly commentary that treats textual, historical, literary, and theological issues across both books.
  • Yale Bible Study. “The Books of Samuel.” Yale Divinity School. yalebiblestudy.org/courses/the-books-of-samuel/. Accessed August 28, 2026. Free academic lessons on Samuel, the origins of kingship, Saul, David’s rise, prophetic authority, and the literary complexity of the narratives.
  • Collins, John J. Introduction to the Hebrew Bible. 3rd ed. Minneapolis: Fortress Press, 2018. Places Samuel within the Deuteronomistic History and provides broader context for monarchy, historical reconstruction, literary sources, and theology.
