Common Christian Prayers

The Glory Be

Glory be to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit. As it was in the beginning, is now, and ever shall be, world without end. Amen.

About The Glory Be

The Glory Be, also known by its Latin name Gloria Patri, is a short Christian doxology—a formula of praise directed to God. Unlike prayers that ask for provision, forgiveness, healing, or guidance, the Glory Be is almost entirely adoration. It names the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit and declares that divine glory belongs to God from the beginning, in the present, and forever.

The word doxology comes from Greek terms associated with “glory” and “speech” or “saying.” Scripture contains many doxological passages. Paul repeatedly closes arguments or sections of letters with bursts of praise, as in Romans 11:36 and Ephesians 3:21. Revelation is filled with worship offered to God. Christian doxologies therefore did not arise in isolation; they grew naturally from biblical patterns of concluding prayer, proclamation, and thanksgiving with praise.

The Glory Be’s explicitly Trinitarian form also belongs to the worship life of the early Church. Matthew 28:19 places Father, Son, and Holy Spirit together in the baptismal command. Early Christian communities increasingly used similar threefold formulas in worship. During the fourth-century controversies over the identity of the Son and the Holy Spirit, Trinitarian doxologies took on added doctrinal significance. Praising the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit together expressed the conviction that Christian worship is directed to the one God known in three persons.

The precise wording developed over time and differs somewhat between Eastern and Western traditions. Western Christians commonly know the form beginning “Glory be to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit,” followed by “as it was in the beginning, is now, and ever shall be.” Eastern liturgies use related formulas that emphasize “now and ever and unto ages of ages.” These are not competing prayers so much as members of the same ancient family of Christian praise.

In Roman Catholic practice the Glory Be appears frequently in the Liturgy of the Hours and in popular devotion, especially the Rosary, where it concludes each decade. Anglicans and other liturgical Protestants also use forms of the Gloria Patri with psalms and canticles. Its placement after psalms is significant: Christians receive the prayer book of Israel and then explicitly offer Trinitarian praise, reading the Psalms within Christian worship.

The phrase “world without end” can sound unusual in contemporary English. It does not mean that the present fallen order will continue unchanged forever. It is a traditional English way of expressing unending ages—“forever and ever.” Modern versions often render the idea more directly. The theological point is God’s eternal glory, not a claim about the permanence of present historical conditions.

For personal prayer, the Glory Be can act as a corrective to a purely request-driven spiritual life. Christians often come to prayer carrying urgent needs, and Scripture encourages petition. Yet worship also means simply recognizing who God is. The Glory Be trains that movement of the heart. Its brevity makes it suitable as a conclusion to Bible reading, a pause during the day, or a response after answered prayer.

Studied carefully, this small prayer carries a remarkably large theology: the Trinity, eternity, worship, biblical doxology, and the continuity of Christian praise across centuries. Its purpose is simple—to return glory to God.

Bibliography & Further Reading

  • United States Conference of Catholic Bishops, “Glory Be (Doxology).” Provides the standard contemporary Catholic English form of the prayer.
  • Compendium of the Catechism of the Catholic Church, Appendix A: Common Prayers. Places the Gloria Patri among established common prayers in both English and Latin.
  • “Doxology,” Catholic Encyclopedia. A historical account of Christian doxologies, including development of the Gloria Patri and its Trinitarian form.
  • The Holy Bible, Matthew 28:19; Romans 11:36; Ephesians 3:21; Revelation 4–5. Biblical foundations for Trinitarian confession and doxological praise.
  • Robert F. Taft, The Liturgy of the Hours in East and West, 2nd ed. (Liturgical Press, 1993). A major study of daily Christian prayer traditions in which psalmody and doxology developed.
