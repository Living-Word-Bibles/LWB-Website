Bible Study: Matthew 6:33

Matthew 6:33 gives one of Jesus’ clearest statements about the ordering of Christian priorities: “But seek ye first the kingdom of God, and his righteousness; and all these things shall be added unto you.” The verse is often quoted as a promise that spiritual devotion will lead automatically to material success. In the Sermon on the Mount, however, Jesus is addressing anxiety about food, drink, clothing, wealth, and the divided loyalties created by possessions. The promise is about trust under God’s reign, not a prosperity formula.

The immediate context begins in Matthew 6:19 with Jesus warning against storing up treasures on earth. He speaks of the eye as the lamp of the body and then says no one can serve both God and mammon. Verse 25 begins, “Therefore,” linking the teaching about worry directly to the problem of competing masters. Anxiety about possessions can become spiritually powerful because fear of not having enough may govern decisions as effectively as greed.

Jesus then points to birds and lilies. Birds do not sow, reap, or store in barns, yet God feeds them. Lilies do not manufacture clothing, yet their beauty exceeds royal splendor. These images are not instructions against work. Elsewhere Scripture values labor, planning, and provision. The point is that creation witnesses to God’s care and challenges the assumption that survival depends entirely on anxious control.

Working Preacher notes that verse 33 marks a shift from Jesus’ critique of wealth and worry toward a positive description of serving God. Instead of making acquisition the organizing purpose of life, disciples are to seek God’s rule and righteousness. The contrast is not between responsible work and religious passivity but between two ultimate orientations.

“The kingdom of God” in Matthew refers to God’s reign or rule rather than merely to a place people enter after death. Jesus announces that the kingdom has drawn near and teaches disciples to pray, “Thy kingdom come. Thy will be done in earth, as it is in heaven.” Seeking the kingdom means ordering life toward what God desires in the present as well as hoping for its future fulfillment.

“His righteousness” is equally important. In Matthew, righteousness includes faithful conduct aligned with God’s will. The Sermon on the Mount has already connected righteousness with reconciliation, truthfulness, mercy, enemy-love, generosity, prayer, and integrity. Seeking righteousness therefore cannot be reduced to private religious feelings.

This gives the verse a social dimension. Working Preacher observes that seeking God’s rule and righteousness can stand in tension with the obsessive accumulation of goods. If disciples seek a world shaped by God’s justice, they must consider how their economic choices affect neighbors, workers, the poor, and the vulnerable.

“All these things shall be added unto you” refers in context to necessities such as food, drink, and clothing. Jesus is not promising every luxury, ambition, promotion, or desired possession. The Father “knoweth that ye have need of all these things.” The assurance is grounded in God’s knowledge and care.

That promise should also not be used to blame people who experience poverty or hunger. Scripture never teaches that every person lacking food simply failed to seek the kingdom first. The Bible repeatedly commands communities to feed the hungry, protect the poor, and practice economic justice. One way God provides may be through the generosity and responsibility of other people.

The verse therefore challenges churches as well as individuals. A congregation can say it seeks God’s kingdom while making institutional survival, property, prestige, or financial expansion its actual first priority. Matthew asks what the community’s budgets and practices reveal about its true master.

For individuals, “seek first” does not mean every day must begin with a particular devotional routine before any other task can be valid. It means God’s reign has first claim on the ordering of life. Work, family, money, politics, rest, and relationships are placed beneath that priority.

This can also reduce anxiety without pretending anxiety vanishes instantly. A person can make wise plans while refusing to treat planning as salvation. The future remains partly outside human control. Seeking the kingdom re-centers life around what can be pursued faithfully today.

The command invites practical questions: Does this choice serve truth and mercy? Does this career decision require abandoning integrity? Does this purchase reflect stewardship? Does this relationship help me live under Christ’s teaching? Does my use of money serve only security for myself or also the good of others?

Matthew 6:33 therefore gives disciples a hierarchy of desire. Food and clothing matter; Jesus does not deny bodily need. But they cannot become the highest good. The kingdom and righteousness come first because they describe life under the Father’s rule.

The promise is liberating precisely because it transfers ultimate responsibility away from anxious self-sufficiency. Christians still work, plan, save, give, and provide, but they do so as people whose lives are not finally secured by possessions. They seek God’s kingdom first and trust the Father with the needs they cannot completely control.

  • Working Preacher, “Commentary on Matthew 6:24–34”. Places Matthew 6:33 within Jesus’ teaching on wealth, possessions, necessities, righteousness, and God’s rule.
