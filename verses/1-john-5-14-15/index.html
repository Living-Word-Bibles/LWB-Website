Bible Study: 1 John 5:14-15

First John 5:14–15 stands near the conclusion of a letter written to strengthen Christian assurance. Immediately before these verses, the author says that believers may “know that ye have eternal life” because they believe in the name of the Son of God. The movement from assurance of life to confidence in prayer is deliberate. Prayer is not presented as a technique for forcing an uncertain deity to act. It grows from a relationship already grounded in God’s gift of life in Christ. The word translated “confidence” carries the sense of boldness or freedom of approach. Christians may come before God openly because their standing rests in the Son rather than in their own spiritual performance.

The qualifying phrase “according to his will” is essential. John does not offer a blank check in which any strongly desired request is guaranteed. Prayer remains prayer to God, not control over God. The believer asks as a creature before the Creator and as a disciple whose desires are being formed by Christ. Elsewhere in 1 John, obedience, love of the brethren, confession of sin, and abiding in God are marks of life with Him. Asking according to God’s will therefore involves more than adding a phrase at the end of a request. It means learning to desire what accords with God’s character, commands, purposes, and wisdom.

“He heareth us” does not imply that God is sometimes unaware of a prayer. In biblical language, divine “hearing” often means favorable attention: God receives the petition and responds as the wise and faithful Lord. Verse 15 develops the assurance further: if believers know that God hears requests made according to His will, they may know that they “have the petitions” they asked of Him. John speaks with the confidence of faith, even when the timing or form of the answer is not immediately visible. The promise concerns God’s faithful response, not the believer’s ability to dictate the response.

This distinction protects the passage from two opposite errors. One error turns prayer into wish fulfillment: if a person believes hard enough, God must grant the desired outcome. That reading ignores “according to his will” and can burden suffering people with the false conclusion that an unanswered request proves deficient faith. The opposite error drains prayer of expectation and treats it as little more than private reflection. John permits neither. Christian prayer is genuinely petitionary. Believers really ask, and they are encouraged to ask with confidence because God truly hears.

The next verse shows that John has intercession in view as well. He moves from general confidence in prayer to the case of seeing a brother or sister sin. Whatever difficulties surround the phrase “sin unto death,” the transition makes one point clear: prayer is not merely about the individual’s private needs. Love for fellow believers expresses itself by bringing them before God. In the larger letter, love and prayer belong to the same life of communion with God.

These verses also invite careful reflection on how Christians discern God’s will. Scripture gives direct guidance about many things: believers may pray for wisdom, holiness, forgiveness, perseverance, justice, daily provision, the spread of the gospel, and love for neighbor. Other requests concern matters where Scripture does not reveal a specific outcome—a job, a move, healing from a particular illness, the resolution of a relationship, or a hoped-for opportunity. In those situations, asking according to God’s will means asking honestly while entrusting the result to God’s wiser judgment.

Jesus models this posture in Gethsemane: He makes a real request and yet submits Himself to the Father’s will. Christian submission is therefore not passivity or emotional dishonesty. A person may plead intensely, repeat a request, lament, and ask others to pray. What changes is the final ground of confidence. The Christian trusts not in the intensity of the asking but in the goodness of the One who hears.

For daily discipleship, 1 John 5:14–15 encourages a mature confidence. Believers need not approach God as strangers who must persuade Him to care. Nor should they imagine that prayer makes them sovereign. They pray as children who know the Father through the Son. The passage therefore joins boldness and humility: boldness because God hears, humility because His will governs the answer. In that balance, prayer becomes both an act of trust and a school in which Christian desires are continually reshaped toward the will of God.

John’s confidence also keeps prayer anchored in communion rather than transaction. The letter repeatedly speaks of abiding in God, keeping His commandments, and loving fellow believers. Prayer belongs inside that whole life. As believers learn Scripture, worship with the church, confess sin, and practice love, their requests are increasingly shaped by the priorities of the kingdom. This does not make prayer predictable, but it makes it relational. The deepest assurance of the passage is not that Christians can foresee every answer; it is that they can know the character of the God to whom they pray and therefore continue asking, trusting, and obeying even when an answer unfolds differently than expected.
