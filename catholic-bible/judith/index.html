The Holy Bible: Douay-Rheims — Judith

Summary, History, Theology & Annotated Bibliography

Read Judith 1:1

Overview

Judith is a dramatic deuterocanonical narrative about the deliverance of Israel through an unexpected champion: a devout widow whose courage, intelligence, prayer, and daring overturn the military power threatening her people. The United States Conference of Catholic Bishops identifies the book’s repeated formula—deliverance “by the hand of a woman”—as a deliberate theological motif recalling the saving “hand” of God in the Exodus. Judith becomes the human instrument through whom divine power defeats an enemy that appears overwhelmingly stronger. [1]

The Assyrian Threat

The story begins with a deliberately compressed imperial setting. Nebuchadnezzar is presented as king of the Assyrians at Nineveh and sends Holofernes westward to punish resisting nations. Holofernes advances toward Judea, while Israel prepares the mountain passes, protects Jerusalem, fasts, prays, and appeals to God.

The Ammonite Achior warns Holofernes that Israel’s strength does not finally depend on armies: if the people remain faithful to God, they will be difficult to defeat. Holofernes rejects the warning and sends Achior to Bethulia, framing the conflict as imperial arrogance against faith in Israel’s God.

The Siege of Bethulia

Holofernes surrounds Bethulia and cuts off its water. As thirst worsens, the population demands surrender. The town’s leader, Uzziah, asks the people to wait five more days for God to act. At this point Judith enters the story. She is introduced as a wealthy, respected, beautiful, and intensely observant widow who has remained unmarried since her husband’s death.

Judith rebukes the leaders for setting a deadline for God. Human beings, she insists, cannot test divine providence. Israel must remain faithful rather than dictate the timing of deliverance: crisis calls for trust and obedience, not bargaining with God.

Judith’s Prayer and Plan

Before acting, Judith prays. In chapter 9 she remembers earlier acts of divine deliverance and contrasts Assyria’s confidence in weapons, horses, and numbers with the strength of God, who protects the lowly and those without hope. She asks that enemy pride be crushed through her own hand. The USCCB notes that her prayer moves from remembrance of God’s past saving deeds to an appeal for the same power in the present. [4]

Judith then lays aside her widow’s clothing, dresses magnificently, and leaves Bethulia with her maid. She deliberately uses beauty, speech, ambiguity, and the enemy’s assumptions about her to gain access to Holofernes. Deborah Levine Gera’s modern commentary examines the book’s sophisticated use of biblical traditions, Greek literary influences, irony, characterization, and the moral questions created by Judith’s strategy. [6]

Judith and Holofernes

In the Assyrian camp Judith speaks so persuasively that Holofernes misunderstands her intentions. Captivated by her beauty and intelligence, he thinks she will help him conquer Judea. The irony is complete: he imagines drawing Judith into his power while she draws him toward destruction.

After several days Holofernes invites Judith to a private banquet. He drinks excessively and eventually collapses on his bed. Left alone with him, Judith prays, takes his own sword, strikes him twice, and cuts off his head. Her maid places the head in the food bag, and the two women walk out of the camp as they had on previous nights. They return safely to Bethulia and display the head to the astonished people.

Victory “By the Hand of a Woman”

Judith tells the Israelites to hang Holofernes’ head on the city wall and launch an attack at dawn. When the Assyrians discover their commander dead, panic spreads through the army and Israel drives the invaders away. Achior, after seeing what has happened, accepts Israel’s God and joins the people. Judith’s victory is therefore portrayed not as personal revenge but as a public act of deliverance.

The book places Judith alongside earlier biblical women. The USCCB compares her to Jael, who killed Sisera, and Deborah, who led Israel in military crisis. Judith’s name means “Jewish woman,” making her both heroine and literary embodiment of faithful Israel. [1]

Thanksgiving and Judith’s Final Years

Victory culminates not in Judith taking political power but in worship. She sings a long hymn celebrating the God who “crushes wars” and recalls how a mighty foreign commander was defeated not by warriors or giants but by a woman. The people celebrate in Jerusalem, offer gifts, and rejoice. Judith returns to her estate, remains a widow, and lives to an advanced age. The narrative closes by describing a long period in which no one again terrorized Israel during Judith’s lifetime and for many years afterward.

Date, Historicity, and Literary Character

Judith presents itself against an ancient imperial background, but its chronology and geography deliberately combine eras. The USCCB notes that Nebuchadnezzar actually ruled Babylon rather than Assyria, Nineveh had fallen before his reign, the rebuilt Temple presupposes the postexilic period, and names such as Holofernes and Bagoas also evoke later Persian history. The introduction concludes that the simplest explanation is historical fiction written to proclaim God as Israel’s deliverer, possibly around 100 B.C. [1]

Carey A. Moore’s Anchor Yale commentary likewise addresses the central question of historicity and the author’s literary purpose, while Deborah Levine Gera’s Oxford treatment surveys the book’s story, date, structure, biblical influences, original-language debate, and reception history. [5] [7]

Language and Textual History

The oldest complete witnesses to Judith are Greek. The USCCB identifies four Greek recensions associated with major Septuagint codices, along with ancient Old Latin, Syriac, Sahidic, and Ethiopic translations. Although Jerome said that he translated Judith from an Aramaic text, no ancient Hebrew or Aramaic manuscript of the book has yet been recovered. Later Hebrew versions survive, but they appear to depend on medieval or Christian textual traditions rather than preserving an independently discovered ancient Hebrew original. [1]

The original language remains debated. Gera examines arguments for Greek composition and a Semitic background, along with the book’s biblical language and engagement with Greek literature. Judith clearly belongs to the multilingual literary world of Hellenistic Judaism. [6]

Canon and Christian Reception

Judith is not part of the Jewish biblical canon and was consequently excluded from the Protestant Old Testament canon, though it has often appeared in Protestant Apocrypha. The early Church, however, used the book extensively. The Catholic Encyclopedia notes citations and praise of Judith among early Christian writers, while the USCCB points to Clement of Rome and Jerome as important witnesses to its Christian reception. [2]

The Council of Trent formally included Judith in its canonical list in 1546, confirming its place among the Catholic deuterocanonical books. [3] It is likewise received as Scripture in Eastern Orthodox and other ancient Christian traditions.

Lasting Importance

Judith endures because it turns the expected logic of power upside down. Holofernes possesses armies, weapons, political authority, and overwhelming force; Judith possesses faith, discipline, intelligence, courage, and a willingness to act. Her victory dramatizes a biblical pattern in which God chooses an unlikely instrument to humble the proud. The book also refuses to separate courage from prayer: Judith’s decisive action comes after fasting, reflection, and direct appeal to God. Its combination of suspense, irony, theological conviction, female heroism, and liturgical thanksgiving made Judith one of the most influential deuterocanonical stories in Christian art and literature. At its center stands a simple claim: deliverance belongs to God, and divine strength can be revealed through the person whom worldly power is most likely to underestimate.
