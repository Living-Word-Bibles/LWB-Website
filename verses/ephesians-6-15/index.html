Bible Study: Ephesians 6:15

Ephesians 6:15 describes the footwear of God’s armor: “your feet shod with the preparation of the gospel of peace.” The image is striking because it places “peace” inside a military metaphor. That tension is intentional. Ephesians takes imagery associated with battle and transforms it around the gospel. The church is prepared not by eagerness for violence but by readiness shaped by the peace Christ has made.

The phrase likely echoes Isaiah 52:7: “How beautiful upon the mountains are the feet of him that bringeth good tidings, that publisheth peace.” In Isaiah, the messenger announces God’s reign and restoration. Ephesians adapts this biblical messenger imagery so that believers stand ready in the good news of peace.

Peace is one of Ephesians’ central theological themes. Chapter 2 says Christ “is our peace.” Through the cross, He has broken down the dividing wall of hostility and created one new humanity from Jews and Gentiles. He came and “preached peace” to those far and near. The gospel of peace in chapter 6 is therefore not vague inner calm. It is the announcement and embodiment of reconciliation accomplished in Christ.

This helps explain how peace functions as armor. Communities are vulnerable when hostility, suspicion, and division control them. The peace of Christ protects the church by giving it a deeper identity than the conflicts that might tear it apart. A reconciled people can resist pressures that depend on turning neighbors into enemies.

The word translated “preparation” can mean readiness, preparedness, or a firm footing. The imagery may emphasize stability—feet equipped to stand—or readiness to move with the gospel. Both ideas fit the letter. Christians stand firm in what Christ has accomplished and remain ready to carry that peace outward.

This verse strongly cautions against reading spiritual warfare as a mandate for aggression. The church’s “battle shoe” is peace. Whatever resistance to evil looks like, it cannot contradict the gospel’s reconciling character. Christians may confront injustice, protect vulnerable people, and oppose destructive systems, but they must resist the temptation to make hatred itself a weapon.

Peace does not mean avoiding conflict at any cost. Ephesians later insists on truth, righteousness, and resistance. Sometimes peace requires confronting wrongdoing because superficial quiet can hide deep harm. Biblical peace, often associated with shalom, concerns wholeness, justice, restored relationship, and flourishing. The gospel of peace may therefore disturb a false peace maintained by fear or domination.

The missionary dimension is equally important. Christians are prepared to explain and embody good news. Their witness should make the character of the message visible. A gospel announced with contempt, coercion, or unnecessary hostility contradicts the peace it claims to proclaim. Evangelism in Ephesians belongs to the same reconciliatory mission that formed the church.

Read this way, the footwear prepares Christians for both stability and movement: stable enough not to be swept into hostility, mobile enough to carry the message of reconciliation beyond familiar boundaries. The church’s readiness is tested not only by what it says about peace but by whether divided people can actually find a reconciled life together under Christ.

For discipleship, Ephesians 6:15 presents a paradox at the heart of Christian spiritual warfare: the church stands against evil by being equipped with peace. Its readiness comes from the gospel that reconciles enemies to God and to one another. Believers do not prepare for battle by becoming more hostile. They prepare by becoming people whose very footing is the peace of Christ.

This passage also belongs to the larger argument of the book, so faithful interpretation should keep the immediate verse connected to the gospel, the community, and the surrounding commands. Reading it in context protects the text from becoming a slogan and helps its theological force shape ordinary Christian practice.

Bibliography & Further Reading

  • The Holy Bible, King James Version — Ephesians 6:10–20. Primary text and complete armor-of-God context.
  • Yale Bible Study, “A Militant Metaphor”. Yale Divinity School discussion of Ephesians 6:10–20, cosmology, martial imagery, incarceration, and power.
  • Working Preacher, “Commentary on Ephesians 6:10–20”. Explores the armor as the church’s shared practice of truth, righteousness, peace, faith, and resistance to evil.
  • Andrew T. Lincoln, Ephesians, Word Biblical Commentary 42 (Word, 1990). Major exegetical commentary on Ephesians, spiritual powers, the armor imagery, and ecclesial ethics.
  • Frank Thielman, Ephesians, Baker Exegetical Commentary on the New Testament (Baker Academic, 2010). Detailed historical and theological commentary on Ephesians 6 and the letter’s theology of power and unity.
