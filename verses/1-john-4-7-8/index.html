Bible Study: 1 John 4:7-8

First John 4:7–8 contains one of Scripture’s most profound statements about God: “God is love.” The phrase is short enough to become a slogan, but First John gives it carefully defined Christian content. The writer does not say that every experience called love is automatically divine, nor does he reverse the sentence into “love is God.” He identifies love as intrinsic to the character of the living God revealed through Jesus Christ. Because love comes from God, those who are born of God are called to love one another.

The passage begins with affection and command: “Beloved, let us love one another.” First John repeatedly addresses its audience as beloved children within a community that appears to have experienced conflict and separation. Love is therefore not discussed in a peaceful vacuum. It is summoned in a setting where theological disagreement and communal fracture have made relationships difficult. The repeated call to love is part of the writer’s answer to division.

“Love is of God” identifies the source. Christian love is not merely a natural personality trait possessed by kind people and lacking in difficult people. It participates in God’s own action. Verse 7 connects love with being “born of God” and knowing God. First John often uses birth language to describe the new identity created by God. The claim is ethical as well as theological: knowledge of God that produces no love is exposed as false or incomplete.

Verse 8 sharpens the point negatively: “He that loveth not knoweth not God.” The writer is not claiming that Christians achieve perfect affection at every moment. The letter’s exhortations assume continuing struggle. Rather, a settled refusal of love contradicts a claim to know the God whose character is love. Christian orthodoxy and Christian ethics are therefore inseparable in First John. Confession about Christ must become a way of treating people.

The words “God is love” are explained by the next verses, which describe God sending the Son into the world. This prevents readers from defining divine love primarily through sentiment. God’s love is self-giving, life-producing, and directed toward reconciliation with sinners. The cross and mission of Christ give the phrase its shape. Christians are not free to call possessiveness, exploitation, manipulation, or indifference “love” merely because strong feelings accompany them.

This also means love and truth are not enemies. First John cares deeply about truthful confession, moral obedience, and discernment. Love does not require pretending doctrine or conduct are irrelevant. At the same time, truth cannot be wielded in a way that contradicts the God whose truth is revealed in self-giving love. Christian communities are called to hold conviction and charity together rather than choosing one against the other.

The passage has broad implications for spiritual formation. If knowing God and loving others belong together, practices such as prayer, Bible study, worship, and theology should make believers more capable of patient and truthful love. Increased religious knowledge that produces contempt is a warning sign. First Corinthians makes a similar point when Paul says knowledge can puff up while love builds up. First John tests spiritual maturity relationally.

The command to love one another first addresses the believing community, but the character of divine love cannot be confined to a closed circle. The New Testament consistently extends love toward neighbor, stranger, enemy, and those in need. First John’s immediate concern is fellowship among believers, yet the God proclaimed as love is the God who sent the Son “into the world.” Christian communities are therefore formed by a love that should overflow beyond themselves.

For personal application, these verses ask more than “Do I feel loving?” They ask whether one’s life participates in God’s pattern of love. Do I seek another person’s good? Can I tell truth without delighting in harm? Do I receive people as gifts rather than competitors? Do I make room for forgiveness, generosity, patience, and service? Such actions do not earn new birth; they reveal its direction.

First John 4:7–8 finally draws theology and discipleship into a single confession. God is love; therefore God’s children learn love. Love comes from God; therefore no believer can boast as though love were self-created. Knowing God becomes visible in relationships. The Christian life begins with being loved by God and matures as that received love is embodied toward others.

The statement also gives Christian mission its proper tone. If God is love, witness cannot be reduced to winning arguments or increasing institutional strength. Truth matters deeply, but truth about this God should be communicated through conduct that seeks life, dignity, repentance, reconciliation, and the genuine good of the neighbor.

Bibliography & Further Reading

  • The Holy Bible, King James Version — 1 John 4:7–21. Primary biblical context for the statement “God is love.”
  • Working Preacher, Janette H. Ok, “Commentary on 1 John 4:7–21”. Explains the passage in three movements: the source, experience, and confidence of perfect love.
  • Working Preacher, Alicia D. Myers, “Commentary on 1 John 4:7–21”. Examines the literary structure of verses 7–11 and the centrality of agapē in the letter.
  • Raymond E. Brown, The Epistles of John, Anchor Bible 30 (Doubleday, 1982). Classic commentary on Johannine theology and community history.
  • Judith M. Lieu, I, II, & III John: A Commentary, New Testament Library (Westminster John Knox, 2008). Detailed interpretation of love, knowledge of God, identity, and ethics in the Johannine epistles.
