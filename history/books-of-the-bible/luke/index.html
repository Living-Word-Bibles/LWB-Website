Luke

Overview and the Two-Volume Story

The Gospel according to Luke is the first volume of a two-part work completed by the Acts of the Apostles. Together Luke and Acts trace a sweeping story from Israel’s scriptural hopes through the ministry, death, and resurrection of Jesus and then outward through the Spirit-empowered mission of the early church. Luke is an accomplished narrator. He frames events with formal prefaces, synchronizes the story with rulers and public history, composes memorable songs and speeches, and gives special attention to journeys, meals, prayer, reversal, forgiveness, and the work of the Holy Spirit. Many of Christianity’s best-known parables and scenes appear only here: the Good Samaritan, the Prodigal Son, Zacchaeus, the repentant criminal at the cross, and the road to Emmaus. Luke’s Jesus brings good news especially to the poor, excluded, and morally compromised while calling all people to repentance and faithful discipleship.

Authorship, Sources, and Date

Luke 1:1–4 states that the author was not the first to write about Jesus. He knows earlier accounts and traditions handed down by eyewitnesses and presents his own work as an orderly narrative for a reader named Theophilus. The Gospel itself does not name its author. Later Christian tradition identified him with Luke, the “beloved physician” mentioned in Colossians and a companion associated with Paul, though the precise historical relationship between the author and Paul remains debated. Most scholars date Luke to the late first century, commonly around 80–90 CE, after Mark, which Luke appears to use extensively. Where Luke shares sayings with Matthew that are absent from Mark, scholars propose various source theories, including the hypothetical sayings source known as Q. Luke also preserves extensive unique material, showing that the evangelist drew from multiple streams of tradition and shaped them into a coherent theological narrative.

Israel’s Scriptures and the Birth Narratives

Luke begins not by leaving Israel behind but by immersing readers in Israel’s Scriptures and worship. Zechariah serves in the temple, Elizabeth and Mary receive miraculous promises, John the Baptist is interpreted through prophetic expectation, and the songs traditionally called the Magnificat, Benedictus, Gloria, and Nunc Dimittis echo the language of the Psalms and prophets. Jesus is circumcised, presented in the temple, and recognized by Simeon and Anna. The infancy narratives therefore present the Christian story as fulfillment within Israel’s covenant history. At the same time, Luke widens the horizon: Simeon speaks of light for the Gentiles as well as glory for Israel. The familiar census narrative anchors Jesus’ birth in the reign of Augustus and the administration of Quirinius, though its chronology is historically debated. Luke’s theological point is unmistakable: God’s saving action enters the world amid imperial claims yet is recognized by humble, faithful witnesses.

The Spirit, Prayer, and Prophetic Mission

The Holy Spirit is prominent from Luke’s opening chapters. John is filled with the Spirit, Mary conceives through divine power, Simeon is guided by the Spirit, and Jesus begins his public ministry “full of the Holy Spirit.” In Nazareth Jesus reads Isaiah and announces good news to the poor, release to captives, sight to the blind, and freedom for the oppressed. That programmatic scene shapes Luke’s portrait of mission. Prayer accompanies decisive moments: Jesus prays at baptism, before choosing the Twelve, at the transfiguration, and in Gethsemane; he teaches disciples to pray and tells parables about persistence. Luke’s combination of Spirit and prayer prepares for Acts, where the same Spirit forms and directs the church. Christian mission is therefore not merely institutional expansion. It is participation in God’s promised renewal, sustained through prayer and empowered by the Spirit.

Meals, Outsiders, Women, and Reversal

Luke repeatedly places theology at the table. Jesus eats with tax collectors and sinners, accepts invitations from Pharisees, teaches about banquets, feeds crowds, and is recognized in the breaking of bread at Emmaus. Meals become settings where status is reordered and grace is made visible. Luke also foregrounds figures often marginalized in ancient social structures: women accompany Jesus and support the ministry; widows become examples of faith; Samaritans act as neighbors and recipients of mercy; tax collectors repent; the poor are blessed while the rich are warned. These emphases should not be romanticized, since Luke also reflects the patriarchal structures of the ancient world, but the Gospel consistently dramatizes reversal. Mary sings that the mighty are brought down and the lowly lifted up. The rich man and Lazarus, the Pharisee and tax collector, and Zacchaeus all show that God’s kingdom challenges ordinary rankings of honor, wealth, and righteousness.

The Journey to Jerusalem and the Parables of Discipleship

At Luke 9:51 Jesus “sets his face” toward Jerusalem, beginning a long travel section that structures much of the Gospel. The geographical journey is also theological preparation for the cross. Along the way Jesus teaches about prayer, possessions, hospitality, repentance, vigilance, love of neighbor, and the cost of discipleship. Luke places many distinctive parables here. The Good Samaritan asks not who qualifies as neighbor but what it means to become one through mercy. The Prodigal Son portrays extravagant restoration while exposing resentment in the elder brother. The Rich Fool and the Rich Man and Lazarus warn against security built on possessions. The Persistent Widow and the Pharisee and Tax Collector teach perseverance and humility. These stories resist reduction to sentimental morals. They confront listeners with the disruptive generosity of God’s reign and the demanding social shape of repentance.

Passion, Resurrection, and Emmaus

Luke follows the broad passion sequence shared with Mark and Matthew but gives it distinctive accents. Jesus tells the disciples that greatness means service, heals the servant’s ear at the arrest, speaks to the women of Jerusalem, prays for forgiveness from the cross in many manuscripts, and promises paradise to the repentant criminal. Pilate repeatedly declares that he finds no basis for the charges, a feature that contributes to Luke’s interest in presenting the Christian movement as not inherently seditious. At the empty tomb, women become the first witnesses to the resurrection announcement, though the apostles initially dismiss their report. The Emmaus story then interprets the resurrection through Scripture and table fellowship: the risen Christ explains Moses and the Prophets and is recognized in the breaking of bread. The Gospel closes with blessing, worship, and joyful return to Jerusalem, exactly where Acts will begin.

Historical Questions and Responsible Reading

Luke explicitly signals concern with reliable narration, yet the Gospel is ancient theological historiography rather than a modern documentary transcript. Historians compare Luke with Mark, Josephus, Paul’s letters, inscriptions, Roman chronology, and other sources. Some details fit known first-century settings well; other matters, especially the census associated with Quirinius, remain debated. Acts also sometimes presents Paul differently from Paul’s own letters. Responsible Christian reading need not force every ancient source into artificial agreement. Luke’s stated purpose is theological as well as historical: to give assurance about the events and teaching received by the church. His selection, arrangement, speeches, and scriptural echoes interpret those events. Historical study can clarify what Luke is doing without reducing the Gospel either to modern reportage or to fiction. The text claims that God acted in history, while telling that history through the literary conventions and theological convictions of its time.

Reception and Why Luke Matters

Luke has profoundly shaped Christian worship, art, preaching, and social imagination. Christmas liturgy draws heavily from its infancy narratives and songs. The Good Samaritan has become a cultural symbol of compassion, while the Prodigal Son has inspired paintings, music, and countless sermons on reconciliation. Luke’s emphasis on the poor has energized charitable ministry, monastic movements, liberation theology, and Christian reflection on wealth. Its attention to women and outsiders has invited both appreciation and critical study of inclusion in early Christianity. Above all, Luke insists that salvation is both spiritually profound and socially visible. Forgiveness creates restored lives; prayer produces mission; table fellowship crosses boundaries; wealth is judged by its use; and resurrection opens the Scriptures. Read with Acts, Luke presents Christianity as a movement rooted in Israel’s story and carried by the Spirit toward all nations.

Annotated Bibliography (Chicago Style)

  • Green, Joel B. The Gospel of Luke. New International Commentary on the New Testament. Grand Rapids: Eerdmans, 1997. Major literary-theological commentary emphasizing reversal, salvation, social relationships, and the journey to Jerusalem.
  • Fitzmyer, Joseph A. The Gospel According to Luke I–IX. Anchor Bible 28. Garden City, NY: Doubleday, 1981. Detailed critical commentary on authorship, sources, infancy narratives, and early ministry.
  • Fitzmyer, Joseph A. The Gospel According to Luke X–XXIV. Anchor Bible 28A. Garden City, NY: Doubleday, 1985. Extensive treatment of Luke’s travel narrative, passion, resurrection, and theology.
  • Bock, Darrell L. Luke. 2 vols. Baker Exegetical Commentary on the New Testament. Grand Rapids: Baker Academic, 1994–1996. Comprehensive evangelical exegesis with historical and theological discussion.
  • Carroll, John T. Luke: A Commentary. New Testament Library. Louisville: Westminster John Knox Press, 2012. Narrative and theological commentary with attention to empire, wealth, marginality, and discipleship.
  • Johnson, Luke Timothy. The Gospel of Luke. Sacra Pagina 3. Collegeville, MN: Liturgical Press, 1991. Concise scholarly commentary integrating literary, historical, and theological analysis.
  • Yale Bible Study. “The Gospel of Luke.” yalebiblestudy.org. Accessed August 28, 2026. Academic course on Luke’s narrative craft, Israel’s story, compassion, parables, passion, and relationship to Acts.
  • Parsons, Mikeal C. Luke. Paideia. Grand Rapids: Baker Academic, 2015. Literary and rhetorical interpretation attentive to ancient education, characterization, and theological themes.
