Bible Study: Lamentations 3:22-23

Lamentations 3:22–23 contains one of Scripture’s most beloved affirmations of hope: “It is of the LORD’S mercies that we are not consumed, because his compassions fail not. They are new every morning: great is thy faithfulness.” These words have inspired the hymn “Great Is Thy Faithfulness” and countless devotional reflections. Yet their power becomes clearer, not weaker, when readers remember that they arise from a book of catastrophic grief.

Lamentations responds poetically to the destruction of Jerusalem and its temple by Babylon in the early sixth century BCE. Yale Bible Study describes the book as an attempt to give language to the incomprehensibility of communal trauma: loss of city, land, temple, security, and the felt presence of God.

The first four chapters use elaborate acrostic patterns. Chapter 3 is especially intricate, with groups of lines beginning with successive Hebrew letters. The disciplined literary form stands in tension with the chaos the poetry describes. Grief is being given structure without pretending it has been solved.

The speaker of Lamentations 3 spends the first twenty verses describing darkness, affliction, blocked paths, brokenness, bitterness, and apparent divine hostility. Verse 21 marks a turn: “This I recall to my mind, therefore have I hope.” Verses 22–23 then name the ground of that hope.

The key Hebrew word behind “mercies” or “steadfast love” is hesed, a rich covenant term associated with loyal, enduring love. Working Preacher notes that the plural can evoke concrete acts of faithful devotion rather than a vague feeling. Hope depends on God’s character.

“His compassions fail not” uses language related to deep mercy. Some scholars note its connection with the Hebrew word for womb, giving the image an embodied tenderness. The poet reaches for language of intimate compassion in the middle of national devastation.

“They are new every morning” is particularly striking because the circumstances have not suddenly become easy. Jerusalem is still ruined. Loss is still real. Morning mercy does not mean the night never happened. It means despair does not exhaust the possibilities of God’s faithfulness.

Working Preacher also warns against reading these verses as though they cancel the rest of Lamentations. The book returns again and again to anguish, and it ends with unresolved questions about whether God has rejected the people. Hope and lament coexist.

This is pastorally important. Christians sometimes quote Lamentations 3:22–23 too quickly in the presence of grief, using it to shut down anger or sorrow. The book itself does the opposite. It gives extensive space to trauma before, during, and after this confession of faith.

Hope here is not emotional positivity. The speaker “calls to mind” what is known about God. Memory becomes a discipline when present experience seems to contradict hope. Faith draws on the character of God previously known even while current circumstances remain painful.

The next verse says, “The LORD is my portion … therefore will I hope in him.” In a setting where land, possessions, institutions, and security have collapsed, God Himself becomes the speaker’s portion. This does not trivialize material loss; it locates hope beneath it.

The passage should also not be used to claim that every tragedy is directly inflicted by God for a simple lesson. Lamentations contains multiple voices wrestling with divine responsibility and human suffering. It is a book of theological struggle, not a tidy explanation.

For people living through trauma, the text offers permission to move between lament and hope. One day may be dominated by grief; another may allow a confession of faith; later grief may return. Lamentations does not demand a straight line toward emotional recovery.

For churches, these verses call for communities that can remember God’s faithfulness without silencing suffering. They can sing “Great Is Thy Faithfulness” while also making space for people who can barely sing.

The phrase “new every morning” can shape daily discipleship. Christians do not need to possess tomorrow’s strength today. Mercy is received again. Prayer, Scripture, worship, food, rest, friendship, and ordinary acts of care can become signs of renewed compassion.

At the same time, the communal setting means hope should lead to solidarity. Lamentations bears witness to a whole city’s suffering. Remembering mercy should make believers more attentive to people whose grief is overlooked.

Lamentations 3:22–23 therefore offers mature hope. It does not stand outside catastrophe but in its center. The poet remembers covenant love while surrounded by evidence of loss. Great faithfulness is confessed not because nothing terrible happened, but because terrible events have not completely erased the possibility of relationship with God.

The verses endure because they refuse two easy answers. They do not surrender to hopelessness, and they do not deny grief. Mercy can be new every morning while lament continues. Biblical hope is strong enough to tell the truth about both.

Bibliography & Further Reading

  • The Holy Bible, King James Version — Lamentations 3:1–33. Primary context of anguish, recalled hope, steadfast love, compassion, and waiting.
  • Yale Bible Study, “Lamentations, Part 1 — Study Guide”. Academic treatment of Jerusalem’s destruction, lament, trauma, and the book’s acrostic structure.
  • Working Preacher, “Commentary on Lamentations 3:22–33”. Discusses hesed, compassion, Jerusalem’s devastation, and the confession of renewed mercy.
  • Adele Berlin, Lamentations: A Commentary, Old Testament Library (Westminster John Knox, 2002). Major literary commentary on the voices, poetry, acrostics, trauma, and theology of Lamentations.
  • Kathleen M. O’Connor, Lamentations and the Tears of the World (Orbis, 2002). Theological and pastoral study of Lamentations as communal trauma, grief, protest, and hope.
