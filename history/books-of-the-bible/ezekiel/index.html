Ezekiel

Read Ezekiel Now

Overview and Exilic Setting

Ezekiel is the great prophetic book of the early Babylonian exile. The prophet is introduced as a priest among Judean deportees by the river Chebar, where in 593 BCE he receives an overwhelming vision of God’s glory. Unlike Jeremiah, who spends much of his ministry in Judah, Ezekiel speaks from exile while Jerusalem still stands and then interprets its destruction after the news arrives. The book is highly structured: chapters 1–24 largely announce judgment on Jerusalem, chapters 25–32 contain oracles against foreign nations, and chapters 33–48 turn increasingly toward restoration. Ezekiel’s language can be visionary, symbolic, priestly, severe, and at times shocking. He performs signs, sees heavenly beings and wheels, measures an ideal temple, addresses individual responsibility, and imagines a valley of dry bones returning to life. The central theological issue is how Israel’s God can remain holy, sovereign, and present when the temple has been destroyed and the people are scattered.

Ezekiel the Priest-Prophet

Ezekiel’s priestly background shapes the book’s concern with holiness, purity, sanctuary, boundaries, and divine presence. His call vision does not occur in the Jerusalem temple but in Babylon, a theological surprise of enormous significance. The living creatures, wheels, expanse, throne, and radiant humanlike form communicate that God’s glory is mobile and cannot be confined to one building or land. Ezekiel falls on his face and is commissioned as a watchman for a rebellious house. The prophet repeatedly receives and eats the divine word, underscoring that proclamation must become internal before it is spoken. Priestly categories are transformed for a displaced community: holiness is not abandoned with the loss of the temple. Instead, the prophet announces that God can accompany exiles, judge a polluted sanctuary, and eventually restore sacred space. Ezekiel’s ministry thus reimagines priestly identity after catastrophe.

Symbolic Actions and Prophetic Performance

Ezekiel does not only preach; he turns his body and household into prophetic signs. He builds a model siege against Jerusalem, lies on his side for extended periods, eats rationed food, cuts and divides his hair, packs baggage as though going into exile, and refrains from conventional mourning after the death of his wife. These actions are difficult, and some details have prompted questions about whether they were literally performed, visionary, stylized, or literarily shaped. Their purpose is clear: the coming disaster must be made visible to an audience that does not want to believe it. Prophetic performance collapses the distance between message and messenger. Ezekiel’s life becomes part of the sign. Modern readers should resist romanticizing this cost; the text depicts a vocation that can be psychologically and ethically disturbing. Yet the signs also demonstrate the seriousness of communication in a crisis where ordinary sermons have failed to persuade.

The Departure of the Glory

Chapters 8–11 contain a visionary tour of Jerusalem’s temple in which Ezekiel sees practices portrayed as abominations. The most important movement, however, is the gradual departure of the divine glory from the sanctuary. The glory moves from the inner temple to the threshold, then toward the east, dramatizing the loss of the presence that made the temple holy. This vision explains destruction without implying that Babylon’s gods defeated Israel’s God. YHWH leaves a defiled sanctuary and remains sovereign over the catastrophe. At the same time, the departure is not final. In the restoration vision of chapters 40–48, the glory returns from the east and fills a renewed temple. The book therefore frames exile through presence, departure, and return. For communities that had identified God’s presence strongly with Jerusalem, Ezekiel offers a difficult but hopeful theology: sacred institutions can be judged, yet divine presence can accompany displaced people and return in a transformed future.

Individual Responsibility and Moral Agency

Ezekiel 18 challenges a proverb that blamed children’s suffering on their parents’ behavior: “The parents have eaten sour grapes, and the children’s teeth are set on edge.” The prophet insists that each person is accountable for his or her own conduct and that repentance can change the moral future. This does not eliminate biblical ideas of corporate identity or intergenerational consequence, but it sharply rejects fatalism. Exiles cannot excuse present behavior by saying they are trapped entirely by ancestral guilt. The righteous person who turns to wickedness is accountable, and the wicked person who turns toward justice can live. Ezekiel 33 repeats this emphasis after Jerusalem falls. The watchman’s task is to warn; the hearer’s task is to respond. This theology is pastorally important in a displaced community: catastrophe has happened, but moral agency remains. People are not merely prisoners of history. They can turn, choose, and live before God.

Dry Bones, New Heart, and Restoration

After Jerusalem’s fall, Ezekiel’s message turns toward restoration. Chapter 36 promises cleansing, a new heart, and a new spirit so that the people can live faithfully. Chapter 37 presents the famous valley of dry bones. The bones explicitly represent the house of Israel saying, “Our hope is lost.” At God’s command, they come together, receive flesh, and finally receive breath. The vision is first a corporate metaphor for national restoration from exile, not originally a diagram of individual resurrection, although later Jewish and Christian readers naturally connected it with resurrection hope. The chapter then joins two sticks representing Judah and Joseph, envisioning a reunited people under one shepherd. Restoration is therefore political, spiritual, communal, and embodied. God’s action addresses both external displacement and internal transformation. The vision’s enduring power comes from its refusal to accept apparent finality: even a landscape of death can become the setting for divine re-creation.

Gog, Temple, and the River of Life

Ezekiel 38–39 depicts the mysterious enemy Gog of Magog in an apocalyptic-style invasion that ends through divine intervention. The imagery influenced later Jewish apocalyptic expectation and is reused in Revelation, but attempts to identify Gog directly with a modern nation usually ignore the symbolic and literary character of the text. Chapters 40–48 then present an immense vision of a restored temple, land, priesthood, and tribal arrangement. The measurements express order and holiness rather than simply architectural planning. Most strikingly, water flows from the temple and becomes a river deep enough to swim in, bringing life to the desert and healing the Dead Sea. Trees on its banks bear fruit and leaves for healing. Revelation later reworks this imagery in its vision of the new Jerusalem. Ezekiel ends with a transformed city whose name is “The LORD is there,” bringing the book’s concern with divine presence to completion.

Why Ezekiel Matters

Ezekiel speaks to communities whose religious world has collapsed. It insists that God is not defeated by the destruction of familiar institutions and is not geographically trapped. The divine glory can appear in exile, depart from a corrupt sanctuary, and return in renewal. The book also joins accountability with hope: people are responsible for their choices, yet restoration requires God to give a new heart and spirit. Its visions have inspired Jewish mysticism, Christian art, spirituals, apocalyptic imagination, and modern preaching. At the same time, its violent metaphors demand ethical caution. Reading Ezekiel well requires both wonder and honesty. The prophet’s central question remains urgent: how can a devastated people imagine life with God after judgment, displacement, and institutional failure? Ezekiel’s answer is not nostalgia. It is re-creation—new breath, renewed community, ordered holiness, living water, and finally the assurance that God is there.

Annotated Bibliography (Chicago Style)

  • Greenberg, Moshe. Ezekiel 1–20 and Ezekiel 21–37. Anchor Bible 22, 22A. Garden City/New York: Doubleday, 1983–1997. Foundational commentary on priestly theology, prophetic symbolism, language, and exilic context.
  • Block, Daniel I. The Book of Ezekiel. 2 vols. New International Commentary on the Old Testament. Grand Rapids: Eerdmans, 1997–1998. Extensive evangelical commentary on text, structure, theology, visions, and restoration.
  • Zimmerli, Walther. Ezekiel. 2 vols. Hermeneia. Philadelphia: Fortress Press, 1979–1983. Landmark historical-critical commentary on the formation of Ezekiel’s prophecies and their exilic setting.
  • Allen, Leslie C. Ezekiel. 2 vols. Word Biblical Commentary 28–29. Dallas: Word Books, 1990. Detailed exegetical treatment of Hebrew text, literary structure, theology, and prophetic rhetoric.
  • Joyce, Paul M. Ezekiel: A Commentary. Library of Hebrew Bible/Old Testament Studies. New York: T&T Clark, 2007. Theological and literary study with particular attention to individual responsibility and exile.
  • Mein, Andrew. Ezekiel and the Ethics of Exile. Oxford Theological Monographs. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2001. Focused study of moral agency, priestly ethics, exile, and the social world of Ezekiel.
  • Yale Bible Study. “Jeremiah and Ezekiel.” yalebiblestudy.org. Accessed August 28, 2026. Academic course on Ezekiel’s exilic setting, judgment, restoration, and prophetic development.
  • Hayes, Christine. “Literary Prophecy: Perspectives on the Exile.” Open Yale Courses, RLST 145, Lecture 19. oyc.yale.edu. Accessed August 28, 2026. Overview of Ezekiel’s visions, individual responsibility, divine presence, and restoration.
