Joshua

Read Joshua Now

Overview and Place in Scripture

Joshua begins where Deuteronomy ends. Moses has died, Joshua has been commissioned as his successor, and Israel stands east of the Jordan preparing to enter the land promised to the ancestors. In the Jewish canon Joshua opens the Former Prophets; in Christian Bibles it opens the historical books. Its central narrative follows Israel’s entry into Canaan, the campaigns associated with Jericho and Ai, the southern and northern coalitions, the distribution of land among the tribes, and Joshua’s final covenant speeches. The book presents these events as fulfillment of divine promise and repeatedly emphasizes courage, obedience to Torah, and exclusive loyalty to the Lord. Its conquest narratives also contain some difficult commands of destruction and demand careful historical and moral interpretation.

Literary Structure

Joshua is arranged in four broad movements. Chapters 1–5 describe preparation and entry: Joshua receives his commission, spies encounter Rahab, Israel crosses the Jordan, memorial stones are erected, and covenant signs are renewed. Chapters 6–12 narrate conquest stories, beginning with Jericho and Ai and moving through battles with regional coalitions. Chapters 13–21 shift from war to land distribution, tribal boundaries, Levitical cities, and cities of refuge. Chapters 22–24 turn toward covenant maintenance: a dispute over an altar east of the Jordan is resolved, Joshua gives farewell speeches, and the tribes renew covenant at Shechem. The land is portrayed not merely as territory but as an inheritance tied to covenant responsibility. The closing challenge—“choose you this day whom ye will serve”—makes loyalty, not conquest, the book’s final emphasis.

Authorship, Date, and the Deuteronomistic History

Traditional interpretation associates the book with Joshua and notes that Joshua 24:26 says he wrote words in the book of the law. The final book, however, refers to Joshua’s death and repeatedly looks back on events from a later perspective. Modern scholarship commonly understands Joshua as part of the Deuteronomistic History, a large theological narrative extending through Judges, Samuel, and Kings and shaped by ideas closely related to Deuteronomy. Scholars often place major stages of this editing in the late monarchic and exilic periods, while recognizing that the book may preserve older local traditions, boundary lists, etiological stories, and memories of conflict. What is clear is that Joshua has been edited to interpret possession of the land through covenant categories: obedience brings success, disobedience endangers the community, and Israel’s future depends on fidelity to the God who gave the land.

Crossing the Jordan and the New Moses

Joshua is deliberately portrayed in continuity with Moses. God promises to be with him as he was with Moses; the people cross water on dry ground; Joshua encounters a divine commander; and covenant rites mark the beginning of a new era. The Jordan crossing echoes the sea crossing in Exodus and confirms Joshua’s leadership. Twelve stones are set up as a memorial so that future children will ask what they mean, turning geography into catechesis. The book’s emphasis on memory reflects a major biblical theme: acts of deliverance must be retold if a community is to understand its identity. Joshua is not simply a military hero. He is repeatedly commanded to meditate on and obey the Torah, and his authority is measured by faithfulness to the instruction received through Moses.

Jericho, Rahab, and the Meaning of Faithfulness

The capture of Jericho is the book’s most famous conquest story. Before the city falls, however, the narrative introduces Rahab, a Canaanite woman who shelters Israelite spies and confesses that the Lord has given Israel the land. She and her household are spared when Jericho is placed under herem, a ban that devotes the city and its goods to destruction or sacred claim. Rahab complicates a simple ethnic reading of the book because the outsider who acknowledges Israel’s God is incorporated into the community, while Achan, an Israelite who violates the ban, brings disaster on Israel at Ai. The paired stories suggest that covenant response matters more than ancestry alone. Christian tradition later remembers Rahab in Matthew, Hebrews, and James, though Joshua should first be read in its own setting.

Conquest, Archaeology, and Historical Questions

The historical relationship between Joshua’s conquest account and archaeology is complex. Some sites named in the book, including Hazor, show major destruction in the Late Bronze Age, but identifying the agents of destruction is difficult. At Jericho, archaeological dating and interpretation have been debated for more than a century, and the remains do not provide a simple confirmation of the biblical sequence. Ai presents additional problems because the site commonly identified with it appears to have been unoccupied during the period when a straightforward thirteenth-century conquest model would place Joshua. Judges itself also portrays Israel’s emergence as gradual and incomplete, with many Canaanite populations remaining in the land. For these reasons many historians describe Israel’s origins through mixed processes such as migration, local social transformation, conflict, and settlement.

The Moral Problem of Conquest

Commands to destroy entire populations are morally disturbing, and responsible interpretation should say so plainly. Ancient Near Eastern royal inscriptions often used sweeping conquest rhetoric, including claims of total annihilation that coexist with evidence that populations survived. This literary background may help explain Joshua’s language, especially because later biblical texts still describe Canaanites living among Israel. Even so, the theological presentation of divinely authorized violence remains difficult. These texts have been misused in colonial, racial, and nationalist projects, including attempts to cast modern peoples as “Canaanites” who may be displaced. Such applications must be rejected. Christian readers interpret Joshua within the whole canon and through the teaching and example of Jesus, whose kingdom is not advanced by holy war. Historical context does not erase the ethical problem, but it prevents these texts from becoming warrants for modern violence.

Land, Inheritance, and Covenant Responsibility

The long land-allotment sections can seem tedious, but they are central to the book’s theology. The promise to Abraham becomes geographically concrete as tribes receive inheritance. Land is gift, identity, livelihood, and responsibility. Cities of refuge establish protected places for persons involved in accidental killing, distinguishing such cases from intentional murder and restraining cycles of blood revenge. The Levites receive cities rather than one continuous territorial allotment, reflecting their special religious role. At the same time, the book repeatedly acknowledges that possession is incomplete. Certain peoples remain, boundaries are contested, and some tribes must still act. Joshua thus holds together fulfillment and unfinished task.

Covenant at Shechem

Joshua 24 gathers the book’s theology into a final covenant ceremony. Joshua retells Israel’s story from the ancestors beyond the Euphrates through Abraham, Egypt, the Exodus, wilderness, and settlement. The emphasis falls on what God has done before Israel is called to respond. Joshua then demands a choice between the Lord and other gods. The famous line “as for me and my house, we will serve the LORD” belongs to this larger public act of covenant commitment, not merely private household devotion. The chapter makes clear that Israel’s deepest threat is not military weakness but divided worship. The conquest narrative therefore ends with a question of allegiance.

Why Joshua Matters

Joshua is a book of promise fulfilled, leadership transferred, memory established, land received, and covenant renewed. It also forces readers to confront the relationship between sacred texts and violence. For Jewish and Christian traditions, the book contributes language of courage, obedience, inheritance, and faithfulness; the New Testament later uses “rest” and inheritance imagery in transformed ways. Yet its hardest passages should never be detached from their ancient context or used to sanctify modern conquest. Read carefully, Joshua presents both confidence and warning: God is faithful to promise, but possession of gifts does not excuse disobedience, injustice, or idolatry. Its final demand is enduring—whom will the community serve, and how will memory of God’s acts shape its life?

Annotated Bibliography (Chicago Style)

  • Nelson, Richard D. Joshua: A Commentary. Old Testament Library. Louisville: Westminster John Knox Press, 1997. A major critical commentary on literary structure, Deuteronomistic editing, conquest traditions, land allotments, and theology.
  • Butler, Trent C. Joshua. Word Biblical Commentary 7. Waco, TX: Word Books, 1983. A detailed technical commentary with extensive treatment of textual criticism, geography, historical questions, source traditions, and literary structure.
  • Hess, Richard S. Joshua: An Introduction and Commentary. Tyndale Old Testament Commentaries. Downers Grove, IL: IVP Academic, 2008. An accessible evangelical commentary emphasizing historical setting, ancient Near Eastern context, literary design, covenant, and theology.
  • Hawk, L. Daniel. Joshua. Berit Olam: Studies in Hebrew Narrative and Poetry. Collegeville, MN: Liturgical Press, 2000. A literary reading attentive to characterization, narrative tension, land, identity, obedience, and the book’s theological ambiguities.
  • Younger, K. Lawson, Jr. Ancient Conquest Accounts: A Study in Ancient Near Eastern and Biblical History Writing. Sheffield: JSOT Press, 1990. Important comparative study of conquest rhetoric in Joshua and neighboring ancient Near Eastern royal literature.
  • Yale Bible Study. “Joshua and Judges.” Yale Divinity School. yalebiblestudy.org/courses/joshua-and-judges/. Accessed August 28, 2026. Free scholarly materials addressing the historical and moral problems of conquest, Jericho, Achan, Deuteronomistic theology, and Israel’s emergence in Canaan.
  • Bible Odyssey. “Hazor Map.” Society of Biblical Literature. bibleodyssey.org/map-gallery/hazor-map. Accessed August 28, 2026. Concise archaeological orientation to Hazor, its strategic importance, Late Bronze destruction, and the uncertainty over who destroyed the city.
  • Collins, John J. Introduction to the Hebrew Bible. 3rd ed. Minneapolis: Fortress Press, 2018. Provides broader historical and literary context for Joshua, the Deuteronomistic History, conquest traditions, archaeology, and biblical theology.
