Bible Study: Mark 10:27

Mark 10:27 says, “With men it is impossible, but not with God: for with God all things are possible.” The statement is frequently quoted as encouragement for ambitious goals, difficult projects, athletic achievements, or personal dreams. In Mark’s Gospel, however, Jesus is answering a very specific question: if even a wealthy, morally serious person cannot secure eternal life through human achievement, “Who then can be saved?”

The episode begins when a man runs to Jesus, kneels, and asks, “Good Master, what shall I do that I may inherit eternal life?” Mark does not initially call him a ruler or even identify him as wealthy. The detail of his great possessions emerges only after Jesus challenges him.

Jesus first cites commandments dealing with human relationships: do not murder, commit adultery, steal, bear false witness, or defraud; honor father and mother. The man says he has kept these from his youth.

Then Mark gives an important detail: “Jesus beholding him loved him.” The difficult command that follows is spoken within love. Jesus tells him he lacks one thing: sell what he has, give to the poor, and follow Jesus.

The man goes away grieving because he has many possessions. The story is not simply about someone who refuses an arbitrary test. Wealth has become a barrier to following Jesus freely.

Jesus then says it is difficult for the rich to enter the kingdom of God and uses the shocking image of a camel passing through the eye of a needle. Attempts to soften this image by proposing a small Jerusalem gate called “the Needle’s Eye” lack convincing historical basis. The point is deliberate impossibility.

The disciples are astonished. In many ancient contexts wealth could be interpreted as evidence of blessing and social importance. If a rich, commandment-keeping person faces such difficulty, they ask, who can be saved?

That is the question verse 27 answers. “With men it is impossible.” Salvation is not the final product of human moral performance, social advantage, wealth, or religious seriousness. Humans cannot manufacture entry into God’s kingdom.

“But not with God” redirects the entire discussion toward grace. God can accomplish what people cannot. Working Preacher emphasizes that the story exposes the impossibility of earning salvation and the need for God’s action.

This means “all things are possible with God” should not be turned into a slogan promising that every desired outcome is available if a believer thinks positively enough. The “impossible” thing in context is salvation and the transformation necessary for discipleship.

Yet grace does not mean the man’s relationship with wealth no longer matters. Jesus’ command is still real. Working Preacher explicitly warns against using divine possibility as cheap grace that leaves everything unchanged. God’s saving work transforms loyalties.

The story therefore holds grace and discipleship together. No one earns salvation by giving away enough money. Yet encountering Jesus can expose the ways possessions control the heart and prevent freedom to follow.

The passage also asks economically comfortable Christians to hear the warning rather than assume it applies only to millionaires. Wealth is relative, and many readers of Mark in affluent societies possess levels of security unimaginable to much of the world. The text asks what possessions do to trust, generosity, and solidarity with the poor.

Peter responds that the disciples have left everything and followed Jesus. Jesus promises a new community of houses, siblings, mothers, children, and fields—but also “persecutions.” The new household of God is abundant, but it is not a prosperity guarantee.

For discipleship, Mark 10:27 first destroys despair. Salvation does not depend on achieving spiritual impossibility through human effort. God is able to save.

Then it destroys complacency. God’s grace can free people from attachments they thought impossible to surrender. For one person that may be wealth; for another, status, resentment, control, reputation, or another form of security.

The verse also gives hope for transformation. A believer may say, “I cannot imagine becoming generous,” “I cannot imagine forgiving,” or “I cannot imagine being free from what controls me.” The context allows confidence that God can accomplish changes human strength alone cannot.

Mark 10:27 therefore is not a generic promise that every human possibility can be achieved. It is better news. Human beings cannot save themselves, but God can. The kingdom is gift before it becomes achievement, and the God who saves is able to loosen even the attachments that make following Jesus seem impossible.

The man’s grief also reminds readers that grace can expose attachments painfully. Divine possibility does not always feel immediately comfortable because liberation may require surrendering what once supplied identity.
