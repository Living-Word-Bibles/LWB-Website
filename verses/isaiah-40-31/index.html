Bible Study: Isaiah 40:31

Isaiah 40:31 closes a chapter of consolation with one of Scripture’s most memorable promises: “they that wait upon the LORD shall renew their strength; they shall mount up with wings as eagles; they shall run, and not be weary; and they shall walk, and not faint.” The verse is often read as a promise of extraordinary energy, but its original audience was exhausted. Isaiah 40 addresses Judean exiles who feared that God had forgotten them.

The chapter opens, “Comfort ye, comfort ye my people.” The setting assumed by Isaiah 40–55 is the Babylonian exile, after Jerusalem’s destruction and before the return made possible under Persian rule. The prophet announces that Israel’s period of punishment is ending and that God is preparing a new exodus home.

Against that background, verse 27 voices the people’s complaint: “My way is hid from the LORD, and my judgment is passed over from my God.” They do not merely feel physically tired; they feel unseen. Exile has become evidence, in their minds, that God no longer notices their condition.

The response begins with God’s identity. The Lord is everlasting, Creator of the ends of the earth, and does not grow weary. Human strength is finite; divine strength is not. More importantly, God “giveth power to the faint.” The point is not simply that God possesses limitless energy but that God shares strength with exhausted people.

Even “the youths shall faint and be weary.” Youth represents natural human vigor, yet even the strongest human resources eventually fail. Isaiah therefore dismantles confidence in self-sufficiency. The promise belongs especially to those who know they do not have enough strength on their own.

“Wait upon the LORD” can also be translated hope in or look eagerly to the Lord. Waiting is not passive resignation. It is sustained trust oriented toward God’s promised action. The exiles are being asked to live as though God’s announced restoration is trustworthy even before they see the journey home completed.

The eagle image communicates renewed capacity and elevation. Yet the final line may be even more pastorally important: “they shall walk, and not faint.” Not every season feels like soaring. Some days faithfulness looks like running; many days it looks like walking. Isaiah includes all three images so that ordinary endurance is not treated as spiritual failure.

This verse should not be used to promise limitless productivity. Christians sometimes quote it while ignoring sleep, health, grief, disability, or burnout. Isaiah is not teaching that trusting God makes human bodies inexhaustible. Its message is addressed precisely to human weakness. Renewal comes from God and may include rest, community, changed expectations, or strength sufficient for the next step.

The historical setting also guards against individualism. The “they” are an exiled people. God is renewing a community for return and restoration. Churches therefore should not tell tired individuals merely to “wait on God” while refusing practical help. God’s strengthening work can come through shared burdens, food, money, advocacy, companionship, and wise care.

For Christian discipleship, Isaiah 40:31 offers hope when strength has reached its limits. The believer does not need to pretend exhaustion is absent. The exiles themselves name it. The call is to direct hope toward the Creator who does not forget His people.

The verse’s enduring beauty lies in its movement from weakness to renewed life. God does not shame the faint; He gives them power. The strongest human beings eventually tire, but those who hope in the Lord are not abandoned to depletion. Sometimes renewal feels like soaring, sometimes running, and sometimes simply taking another faithful step. In every form, the strength is received from the God whose faithfulness outlasts human exhaustion.

The sequence from soaring to running to walking also honors different forms of faithfulness. Dramatic renewal is not the only evidence of God’s strength. Quiet endurance through repetitive days can be just as faithful as a visibly powerful season.

The chapter also contrasts God’s incomparable power with idols and imperial claims. Babylon may appear dominant, but it does not possess the Creator’s permanence. The exiles’ exhaustion is therefore interpreted within a larger theological reversal: the empire that seems strong is temporary, while the people who seem powerless are sustained by the everlasting God. Waiting on the Lord becomes an act of resistance against the assumption that visible political strength defines reality. Hope rests in the Creator who calls the stars by name and does not lose track of His people.

Waiting, then, is active hope.
