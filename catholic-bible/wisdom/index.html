The Holy Bible: Douay-Rheims — Wisdom (of Solomon)

Summary, History, Theology & Annotated Bibliography

Read Wisdom 1:1

Overview

The Book of Wisdom, often called the Wisdom of Solomon, is one of the most philosophically sophisticated books of the Catholic Old Testament. It was written in Greek rather than translated from a known Hebrew original and places much of its teaching in the voice of Solomon, the biblical king traditionally associated with wisdom. The United States Conference of Catholic Bishops places its composition roughly fifty years before Christ and identifies its author as an anonymous member of the Jewish community in Alexandria, Egypt. [1]

Alexandria and the World of Hellenistic Judaism

Alexandria was a major intellectual center and home to a large Greek-speaking Jewish population. Wisdom reflects this environment: its language is Greek, its rhetoric polished, and its author uses concepts recognizable from Hellenistic philosophy while remaining rooted in Israel’s Scriptures. Jason M. Zurawski describes a rhetorically sophisticated Jewish composition from Alexandria, while David Winston emphasizes its combination of biblical thought with Middle Platonic language. [5] [6]

The author speaks as Solomon, but modern scholarship does not treat the historical king as the literal author. The Solomonic voice supplies authority and a literary setting for instruction addressed to a later community.

Righteousness, Death, and Immortality

The opening chapters confront a central moral problem: why do the righteous suffer while the wicked often appear successful? The author rejects the idea that death has the final word. Wisdom 3 declares that the souls of the righteous are in God’s hand and that their apparent destruction is not their true end. Their hope is “full of immortality,” and suffering becomes a testing and purification rather than proof of divine abandonment. [4]

This develops older biblical discussions of retribution. Earthly prosperity, long life, and many descendants are no longer sufficient measures of divine favor. Virtue and fidelity possess an eternal horizon, and righteousness participates in a reality stronger than death.

The Righteous One and the Logic of Persecution

Wisdom 2 places a chilling speech in the mouths of the wicked. They resent the righteous person because his life exposes their conduct, and they resolve to test him with insult and torture, even condemning him to a shameful death to see whether God will rescue him. The passage became especially important in Christian interpretation because its language resonated with the passion of Christ. The USCCB notes more broadly that Wisdom’s first ten chapters provide significant background for New Testament theology. [1]

Personified Wisdom

Chapters 6–9 contain the book’s great praise of divine Wisdom. Wisdom is described not merely as practical intelligence but as a radiant, holy, life-giving reality that comes from God and enables human beings to know what is true and just. Chapter 7 presents Wisdom as more valuable than power, wealth, health, or beauty and describes her as intimately connected with divine activity in the world. [3]

Wisdom 9 places a prayer on Solomon’s lips asking God to send Wisdom from heaven so that he may judge rightly. She is said to have been present when God made the world and to know what pleases him. [8] The imagery builds on Proverbs while expressing Jewish wisdom in a Greek intellectual environment. Matthew Goff notes language comparable to Stoic ideas about divine reason permeating the cosmos, though the work remains thoroughly Jewish in its scriptural commitments. [7]

Wisdom as the Guide of Israel

Chapter 10 turns from philosophical praise to biblical history. Without naming the major figures directly, the author retells episodes involving Adam, Cain, Noah, Abraham, Lot, Jacob, Joseph, Moses, and Israel. Wisdom is portrayed as the power that preserves the righteous, guides the patriarchs, rescues Joseph, strengthens Moses, and leads Israel through the sea. [9]

Wisdom is not an abstract principle detached from Israel’s history. The same divine Wisdom that orders creation acts within the covenant story, binding cosmology, ethics, revelation, and salvation history together.

The Exodus Retold

Chapters 11–19 form an extended meditation on the Exodus. Rather than simply repeating the narrative, the author compares the experiences of Israel and Egypt through a series of contrasts. Elements of creation that punish Egypt become instruments of protection for Israel. Water, animals, darkness, food, and the sea are interpreted as signs that creation itself serves divine justice. [1]

The retelling also stresses mercy. Wisdom 11–12 presents divine power as patient, giving sinners opportunities for repentance and holding justice and mercy together.

The Critique of Idolatry

Wisdom 13–15 contains one of antiquity’s most sustained Jewish critiques of idolatry. Some people mistake the beauty and power of created things—fire, wind, stars, water, heavenly bodies—for gods instead of reasoning from creation to its Maker. Others manufacture an image from wood or stone and then worship what their own hands produced. The satire exposes the contradiction of asking a powerless object for life, health, protection, or success.

The argument is also philosophical: creation bears witness to a Creator, reason carries moral responsibility, and worship becomes disordered when created things replace God. Matthew Goff describes the book as presenting Jewish scriptural religion as a moral philosophy capable of addressing humanity broadly. [7]

Structure and Literary Unity

The USCCB divides Wisdom into three major movements: the reward of righteousness in chapters 1–6, praise of Wisdom in 6:22–11:1, and the providential interpretation of the Exodus in chapters 11–19. [1] Earlier scholarship sometimes proposed multiple authors because these sections differ in style and subject, but Zurawski notes that modern research has strongly supported the literary unity of the work. [5]

Date and Authorship

The exact date remains debated. The USCCB’s traditional introduction places the book about fifty years before Christ. Other scholars favor a date near the turn of the era, and Winston argued for an even later setting during the reign of Caligula in A.D. 37–41. [6] What is far more certain is that the work belongs to Greek-speaking Judaism, was composed by a highly educated Jewish author, and reflects sustained engagement with both biblical tradition and Greek intellectual culture.

Canon and Christian Reception

Wisdom is one of the Catholic deuterocanonical books and belongs to the traditional group of biblical wisdom writings alongside Job, Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, and Sirach. [2] The Council of Trent formally included Sapientia—Wisdom—in its canonical list. [3] It is also received in Eastern Orthodox and other ancient Christian traditions, while Protestant Old Testaments generally exclude it from the canon and may print it in an Apocrypha section.

Lasting Importance

The Wisdom of Solomon occupies a remarkable place between biblical wisdom, Jewish theology, Greek philosophy, and early Christian interpretation. It teaches that righteousness is stronger than apparent defeat, that immortality gives moral life an eternal dimension, that Wisdom comes from God and orders both creation and history, and that the Exodus remains a pattern for understanding divine justice and mercy. Its author does not abandon Israel’s Scriptures in order to speak to the Hellenistic world; he uses the language and intellectual resources of that world to defend and deepen them. The result is a book confident that revelation and reason need not be enemies and that true wisdom ultimately leads the human person back to the Creator.
