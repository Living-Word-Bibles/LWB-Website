Bible Study: Exodus 20:12

Exodus 20:12 commands: “Honour thy father and thy mother: that thy days may be long upon the land which the LORD thy God giveth thee.” It is the first commandment in the Decalogue focused directly on human relationships and the only one in Exodus 20 accompanied by an explicit promise. The command has shaped Jewish and Christian teaching for centuries, but it needs careful interpretation because “honor” has sometimes been confused with unlimited obedience.

The Ten Commandments begin with grace before command: “I am the LORD thy God, which have brought thee out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of bondage.” Israel’s obedience is a response to liberation. The commandments describe how a freed people is to live under God’s covenant. Honoring parents belongs to that larger social order.

The Hebrew word for “honor” is related to weight or heaviness. To honor someone is to treat that person as significant rather than disposable. In the ancient household, aging parents depended on adult children for material support and social protection. Many scholars therefore emphasize that the command is addressed not simply to small children but to the covenant community, including adults responsible for the care of parents.

This distinction is pastorally important. “Honor” is not identical with obeying every parental demand. Scripture itself recognizes that human authorities can act wrongly. Jesus places allegiance to God above family loyalty when the two conflict. An adult may honor a parent while disagreeing, establishing boundaries, or refusing participation in harmful behavior.

The command has been especially painful when used against abused children or adults from abusive families. Responsible interpretation must state clearly that Exodus 20:12 does not require a person to remain in danger, conceal abuse, or submit to criminal or destructive conduct. Seeking safety, reporting abuse, limiting contact, or involving authorities can be consistent with honoring the seriousness of truth and human dignity.

At the same time, the command challenges cultures that treat aging parents as burdens once they are no longer economically productive. Biblical honor includes tangible care. In the New Testament, Jesus criticizes religious practices that allow people to neglect responsibility toward parents while claiming devotion to God. First Timothy likewise speaks strongly about caring for relatives.

“That thy days may be long upon the land” connects family faithfulness with Israel’s corporate life in the promised land. Stable communities depend partly on intergenerational responsibility. The command is not best read as a mechanical guarantee that every respectful individual will live to an old age. Scripture contains faithful people who die young. The promise concerns the flourishing and continuity of covenant life.

Paul later cites the command in Ephesians 6, calling it “the first commandment with promise.” Christian appropriation therefore continues to value honor while placing family life under the lordship of Christ. Parents are also commanded not to provoke children but to nurture them responsibly. Biblical family ethics are reciprocal rather than a license for unchecked parental power.

Communities also share responsibility for making honor possible. Churches should support caregivers, protect children, challenge abusive authority, and refuse to leave elderly or disabled family members isolated. The command concerns the moral fabric of a whole people, not merely private obedience inside individual households.

For discipleship, Exodus 20:12 asks Christians to treat family relationships as morally significant without turning family into an idol. Parents are not ultimate; God is. Yet the people who gave life, care, culture, or nurture should not be casually discarded. The command calls communities to intergenerational responsibility, care for aging people, gratitude where gratitude is due, truth where truth is needed, and protection where harm exists. Biblical honor is weighty because human relationships matter before God.

This passage also belongs to the larger argument of the book, so faithful interpretation should keep the immediate verse connected to the gospel, the community, and the surrounding commands. Reading it in context protects the text from becoming a slogan and helps its theological force shape ordinary Christian practice.

Bibliography & Further Reading

  • The Holy Bible, King James Version — Exodus 20:1–17. Primary Decalogue context.
  • Working Preacher, “Commentary on Exodus 20:1–4, 7–9, 12–20”. Careful discussion of honoring parents, including the distinction between honor and obedience and the care of elderly parents.
  • Working Preacher, “Commentary on Exodus 20:1–17”. Places the commandments within Israel’s liberation and covenant vocation.
  • Nahum M. Sarna, Exodus, JPS Torah Commentary (Jewish Publication Society, 1991). Major Jewish commentary on the Decalogue and Exodus’ covenant theology.
  • William H. C. Propp, Exodus 19–40, Anchor Bible 2A (Doubleday, 2006). Detailed historical and philological commentary on Sinai and the Decalogue.
