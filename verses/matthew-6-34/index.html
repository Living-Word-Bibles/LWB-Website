Bible Study: Matthew 6:34

Matthew 6:34 closes Jesus’ teaching about worry with a memorable sentence: “Take therefore no thought for the morrow: for the morrow shall take thought for the things of itself. Sufficient unto the day is the evil thereof.” In older English, “take no thought” means do not be anxious, not never think about tomorrow. Jesus is not condemning planning. He is warning against allowing tomorrow’s uncertainties to dominate today’s faithfulness.

The verse belongs to Matthew 6:25–34, where Jesus speaks about food, drink, clothing, birds, lilies, and the Father’s knowledge of human need. The section follows His warning that no one can serve both God and mammon. Worry is therefore connected to questions of trust and allegiance, not merely to emotional temperament.

Jesus’ illustrations do not romanticize life. Birds still search for food, and human beings still need to work. The lilies’ beauty does not mean drought never comes. The argument is theological: if God’s care extends throughout creation, disciples need not live as though everything depends on their ability to control the future.

Verse 33 has just told disciples to “seek first the kingdom of God, and his righteousness.” Verse 34 then narrows attention to the present day. Seeking the kingdom is lived one day at a time. Tomorrow is real, but tomorrow does not yet belong to human action.

“Sufficient unto the day is the evil thereof” is strikingly realistic. Jesus does not say each day contains only blessing. The word translated “evil” can refer to trouble or difficulty. Every day already brings enough challenge without importing all imagined future trouble into the present.

This makes the saying different from superficial positivity. Jesus is not telling disciples to pretend hardship is unreal. He is telling them not to multiply hardship through anticipatory fear. Today’s difficulty deserves today’s faithful response.

The verse should not be used to discourage prudent planning. Scripture contains examples of saving grain, preparing for famine, counting costs, managing households, and acting wisely about the future. Jesus Himself plans journeys and prepares disciples for coming events. Responsible planning asks, “What wise action can I take?” Anxiety repeatedly asks, “What if everything goes wrong?”

The difference is not always easy to identify. Planning can become an attempt to eliminate all uncertainty, while anxiety can disguise itself as responsibility. Matthew’s solution is not irresponsibility but trust in the Father while doing what faithfulness requires now.

The verse also needs pastoral sensitivity. People with anxiety disorders, trauma histories, obsessive thoughts, or panic cannot necessarily stop worrying through a single act of will. Telling them “Jesus said not to worry” can intensify shame. Prayer, Scripture, counseling, medication, sleep, exercise, community, and medical care can all belong to faithful response.

Jesus’ teaching is better heard as invitation than accusation. The Father knows what disciples need. Their value exceeds that of the birds God feeds. They are invited to release the illusion that fear can secure tomorrow.

This also has economic implications. Working Preacher connects Matthew 6:25–34 with the preceding teaching about wealth. Some forms of accumulation are driven by fear that no amount will ever be enough. Jesus challenges the endless cycle in which tomorrow’s imagined scarcity prevents generosity today.

That does not mean everyone should abandon savings or emergency preparation. Prudence can serve love of neighbor and family. The deeper question is whether preparation remains a tool or becomes the master around which life is organized.

“The morrow shall take thought for the things of itself” gives tomorrow back to tomorrow. Christians do not need to solve problems that have not happened yet. They can prepare reasonably and then return attention to present obedience.

For discipleship, Matthew 6:34 can become a practical discipline. When fear turns toward tomorrow, ask: Is there something faithful I can do today? If so, do it. If not, name the concern in prayer and release what cannot yet be acted upon.

The verse also encourages presence in relationships. Anxiety about future possibilities can make people unavailable to the persons actually before them. Jesus’ teaching redirects attention toward today’s neighbor, today’s work, today’s prayer, and today’s opportunity for mercy.

Christian hope is not built on knowing that tomorrow will be easy. Jesus openly admits tomorrow will have trouble of its own. Hope rests in the conviction that the Father who is present today will not cease to be God tomorrow.

Matthew 6:34 therefore offers neither recklessness nor denial. It offers temporal humility. Human beings live one day at a time because that is how created life works. Today has enough trouble, enough grace, and enough opportunity for faithfulness. Tomorrow can be entrusted to God until tomorrow becomes today.

Bibliography & Further Reading

  • The Holy Bible, King James Version — Matthew 6:25–34. Primary teaching on worry, necessities, creation, the Father’s care, and the future.
  • Working Preacher, “Commentary on Matthew 6:24–34”. Explores worry in relation to money, possessions, necessities, and seeking God’s rule.

  • R. T. France, The Gospel of Matthew, NICNT (Eerdmans, 2007). Major commentary on Matthew’s language, discipleship, providence, and daily trouble.
