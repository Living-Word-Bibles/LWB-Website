Bible Study: Romans 1:16

Romans 1:16 says, “For I am not ashamed of the gospel of Christ: for it is the power of God unto salvation to every one that believeth.” Paul’s statement functions as a thesis for much of Romans. The gospel is not merely information about religion or a set of ideas believers must defend. Paul describes it as God’s active power bringing salvation.

Romans opens with Paul identifying himself as an apostle set apart for the gospel, which God promised beforehand through the prophets. The good news concerns Jesus Christ, descended from David and declared Son of God in power through resurrection.

Working Preacher emphasizes the Jewish character of Paul’s gospel. It arises from Israel’s Scriptures, concerns Israel’s Messiah, and then extends to the nations. “To the Jew first, and also to the Greek” preserves both historical particularity and universal scope.

This is especially important because Romans has sometimes been interpreted in anti-Jewish ways. Paul does not present Christianity as a replacement for a failed Jewish religion. Later chapters insist that God has not rejected Israel and that divine gifts and calling remain irrevocable.

“I am not ashamed” may reflect the social vulnerability of proclaiming a crucified Messiah in the heart of the Roman world. Crucifixion was a shameful imperial execution. Paul nevertheless announces that the crucified and risen Jesus reveals the true power of God.

That claim creates a contrast with Roman ideas of power. Imperial power displayed itself through military conquest, status, wealth, and public honor. Paul calls the gospel “power” because God’s power saves rather than merely dominates.

Working Preacher describes this gospel power as resurrection power. The good news announces that the crucified Jesus has been raised and enthroned. Salvation comes through divine action that brings life from death.

“Unto salvation” is broader than merely avoiding punishment after death. Romans will describe liberation from sin’s enslaving power, reconciliation with God, life in the Spirit, adoption, transformed community, and hope for creation’s renewal.

“To every one that believeth” emphasizes trust. Faith in Romans is not simply intellectual agreement but reliance upon God’s saving action in Christ and a life increasingly shaped by that allegiance.

The verse therefore should not be used primarily as a command to feel embarrassed whenever one is nervous about discussing religion. Paul’s concern is theological confidence in the gospel itself, not personality type.

Some Christians are naturally quiet, cautious, or socially anxious. Faithful witness can take many forms: conversation, service, teaching, writing, generosity, hospitality, or simply answering honestly when asked.

Being “unashamed” also does not authorize arrogance. If the gospel is God’s power rather than the believer’s achievement, boasting in personal superiority is excluded.

Christian witness should therefore reflect the character of the gospel’s power. Coercion, manipulation, and humiliation are poor methods for proclaiming a saving power revealed through the self-giving Christ.

The verse also gives courage when Christian faith is culturally unpopular. Paul does not measure truth by prestige. Yet unashamed faith should remain intellectually honest and ethically accountable.

For churches, Romans 1:16 asks whether the gospel remains central or whether institutional survival, political influence, or branding has become the real source of confidence.

The phrase “power of God” also protects believers from reducing Christianity to moral advice. The gospel does not merely tell people to improve themselves. Paul believes God acts through the good news to create a new reality.

The verse therefore creates courage without making Christian witness combative. Confidence belongs in the saving power of God, not in the believer’s ability to dominate an argument or defeat another person socially.

For discipleship, the verse invites confidence joined with humility: “I do not need to be ashamed of God’s saving work, and I do not need to make myself impressive in order to defend it.” The power belongs to God.

Romans 1:16 therefore announces the direction of the letter. God has acted in Christ, that action is saving power, the invitation extends across Jew and Gentile, and faith receives what God has done. Paul’s lack of shame rests not in human religious success but in confidence that the gospel carries the power of the God who raises the dead.

Paul’s statement also challenges Christians to distinguish confidence from defensiveness. The gospel does not become more powerful when believers exaggerate, suppress questions, or treat outsiders with contempt. If the power belongs to God, witness can remain truthful, patient, and humble.

Paul’s language also places mission beneath grace. The gospel reaches people across ethnic and cultural boundaries because God acts first. That means Christian witness can be confident without becoming possessive. The church does not own the saving power it proclaims; it serves as a messenger of a power whose source, scope, and effectiveness belong to God.

Bibliography & Further Reading

  • The Holy Bible, King James Version — Romans 1:1–17. Primary opening context of gospel, Messiah, mission, faith, salvation, and God’s righteousness.
  • Yale Bible Study, “Romans 1–3 — Big Human Problem, Bigger Divine Solution”. Academic overview of Romans’ opening themes.
  • Working Preacher, “Commentary on Romans 1:1–17”. Explains the gospel as God’s saving power with Jewish particularity and universal reach.
  • Robert Jewett, Romans, Hermeneia (Fortress, 2007). Major historical and exegetical commentary on Romans and its Roman social setting.
  • Douglas J. Moo, The Epistle to the Romans, 2nd ed., NICNT (Eerdmans, 2018). Detailed exegetical and theological commentary on Paul’s argument in Romans.
