Bible Study: Exodus 20:13

Exodus 20:13 states one of the shortest commandments in Scripture: “Thou shalt not kill.” The traditional English wording is familiar, but the Hebrew verb is more specific than the English word “kill” can suggest. The verb ratsach most often refers to unlawful killing or murder, though it can also appear in contexts involving accidental homicide. The command therefore should not be read as a simple prohibition on every possible taking of life. The laws immediately following the Decalogue distinguish intentional murder from accidental death and establish different legal responses.

The command belongs to the covenant given after Israel’s deliverance from Egypt. Exodus 20 begins not with a rule but with God’s act of liberation: “I am the LORD thy God, which have brought thee out of the land of Egypt.” The commandments describe the kind of community a freed people is to become. A people rescued from violent oppression is not to reproduce violence casually within its own common life.

The prohibition protects the value of human life by placing lethal violence under moral restraint. Human beings are not free to decide that another person’s life is disposable simply because of anger, rivalry, property, status, or convenience. Later biblical law requires testimony, distinguishes types of homicide, and limits private vengeance. These legal developments show that the command concerns justice as well as individual morality.

Genesis 9 provides a related theological foundation: human life is treated as weighty because human beings bear the image of God. The command not to murder therefore belongs to a wider biblical conviction that human life cannot be reduced to usefulness, power, wealth, race, class, or social standing. Every person stands before God as a creature of profound worth.

Jesus deepens the command in the Sermon on the Mount by addressing anger, insult, and contempt. He does not erase the distinction between anger and homicide; rather, He exposes the inner dispositions from which violence can grow. Hatred begins to deform relationships before physical violence occurs. Christian obedience therefore asks not only whether someone has physically harmed another person but whether contempt and dehumanization are being cultivated in the heart.

This does not mean all anger is sinful. Scripture contains righteous anger at oppression and injustice. The moral issue is what anger becomes. Anger can alert a person to wrongdoing, but it can also turn into revenge, cruelty, or a desire to destroy. Christian discipleship seeks ways to confront evil while refusing to let hatred become the governing force.

The verse also requires careful application in discussions of war, self-defense, policing, capital punishment, abortion, and end-of-life ethics. Christian traditions have reached different conclusions in these areas because Exodus 20:13 by itself does not answer every circumstance involving life and death. Some Christians adopt pacifist interpretations; others allow carefully limited uses of force. Responsible study should acknowledge those differences rather than pretending the six-word command settles every later ethical debate automatically.

What the verse does establish clearly is a presumption against wrongful killing and a responsibility to protect life. It condemns murder and challenges every social practice that treats human beings as expendable. It also calls communities to build systems of justice that do not depend on private revenge, mob violence, or false accusation.

The command additionally places responsibility on communities to distinguish justice from vengeance. Biblical law develops procedures, witnesses, cities of refuge, and penalties precisely because taking life cannot be left to uncontrolled personal retaliation. Restraint and due process are therefore part of the command’s moral world. Protection of life includes protection from both private violence and unjust punishment.

For modern discipleship, Exodus 20:13 reaches beyond avoiding homicide. Christians are called to resist the habits that make violence easier: contempt, hatred, racist dehumanization, abusive speech, indifference to vulnerable lives, and the glorification of cruelty. They are also called to protect those at risk and to support structures that seek justice fairly.

The command’s enduring force is therefore both negative and positive. “Do not murder” establishes a boundary that protects life, but the wider biblical witness moves believers toward a life-giving posture—loving neighbors, seeking peace, defending the vulnerable, and recognizing the image of God in people they might otherwise be tempted to dismiss. The God who delivered Israel from oppression calls His people to become a community where human life is treated as sacred rather than disposable.

Christian communities also honor this command by cultivating alternatives to violence: reconciliation where possible, lawful protection where necessary, truthful investigation, and care for those harmed. The command is not passive; it creates responsibility for preserving life and refusing systems of retaliation that multiply death.
