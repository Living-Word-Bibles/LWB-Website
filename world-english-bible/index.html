The Holy Bible: World English Bible (WEB)

Annotated History & Bibliography

Read the World English Bible

Overview

The World English Bible (WEB) is a modern English Bible translation created with an unusual combination of goals: to preserve much of the formal, text-conscious character of the American Standard Version (ASV) of 1901, to update its language for contemporary readers, and to place the resulting text permanently in the public domain. That final commitment is one of the WEB’s defining features. Its publishers explicitly permit readers to copy, print, publish, quote, broadcast, distribute, sell, teach from, and otherwise use the text without paying royalties or seeking ordinary copyright permission.1

The project is closely associated with Michael Paul Johnson, founder of eBible.org and senior editor of the WEB, together with a broad volunteer network that included proofreaders, consultants, and contributors with biblical language expertise. The translation grew during the rise of the public internet, and its development method reflected that environment: drafts could be posted, examined, corrected, and redistributed electronically at very low cost. In that sense the WEB belongs to a new chapter in Bible history—the era in which a translation could be built for digital circulation from the beginning rather than adapted to digital media after print publication.

Origins: A Public-Domain Bible for the Digital Age

Johnson’s own history of the project traces its beginning to March 1994. He describes praying about the absence of a high-quality modern English Bible that could be copied freely in electronic and print media without royalty payments or copyright restrictions. He initially experimented with fresh translation work, including material that became known as God’s Living Word. That process proved too slow for the larger goal, so the project changed direction: instead of beginning from a blank page, it would modernize a strong existing English translation already in the public domain—the American Standard Version.2

The ASV was a natural foundation. It had been published in 1901, was respected for its formal approach, and had long since entered the public domain in the United States. Its archaic pronouns, vocabulary, punctuation, and some textual decisions, however, made it increasingly distant from ordinary modern English. By revising the ASV rather than creating an entirely new translation, the WEB could preserve a substantial historic translation base while concentrating editorial effort on modernization, textual review, and consistency.

eBible.org registered its domain in 1997 and began distributing draft copies of the World English Bible along with other public-domain Bible texts. Draft publication was integral to the process. Readers could see the latest text, report problems, and participate in proofreading. The resulting model was neither a conventional commercial translation committee nor an anonymous open wiki. Editorial responsibility remained with the project leadership, but the internet made large-scale volunteer review practical in a way that had not existed for earlier English Bible projects.

From the American Standard Version to Modern English

The WEB retains a recognizable ASV lineage. Many sentence structures and formal correspondences remain close enough that readers familiar with the ASV can hear the family resemblance. Yet the purpose was not merely to replace “thee” and “thou.” The editors modernized spelling, punctuation, vocabulary, quotation conventions, and expressions whose meanings had shifted. They also reexamined the underlying Hebrew and Greek where the ASV needed correction or where later textual resources justified a different decision.

This gives the WEB a generally formal-equivalence character: it tends to preserve source-language structure and vocabulary when intelligible English permits, while still aiming to sound like contemporary prose rather than a minimally altered 1901 text. That balance explains why the WEB can feel more literal than many modern translations but noticeably smoother than the ASV. It was designed for reading aloud, personal study, digital publishing, missionary distribution, and reuse in projects where licensing freedom matters.

Old Testament Textual Basis

The official WEB overview identifies the Old Testament basis with the Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia (BHS), the standard scholarly edition of the Masoretic Hebrew Bible used widely during the period in which the WEB was developed.3 The Masoretic Text is therefore the primary textual foundation, as it is for most modern Protestant Old Testament translations, while ancient versions and textual evidence can inform difficult passages.

Because the WEB began as an ASV revision, its Old Testament history is layered: the translators inherited a substantial English base from the ASV but checked and updated that base against modern Hebrew editions and scholarship. This is important when describing the WEB accurately. It is not simply “the ASV with modern pronouns,” nor is it a wholly independent rendering produced without reference to earlier English. It is a deliberate revision whose textual and linguistic judgments were revisited during development.

New Testament Textual Basis and the Majority Text

The WEB’s New Testament is often described as a Majority Text translation, but the project’s present description is more precise. The official site says the WEB is based on a combination of the ASV’s original source-text choices and the Greek Majority Text, with the New Testament conformed to the Majority Text especially where the editors judged the differences important.4

“Majority Text” generally refers to readings supported by the large Byzantine manuscript tradition that forms the numerical majority of surviving medieval Greek New Testament witnesses. That textual approach differs at a number of points from the eclectic critical text underlying translations such as the NRSV, ESV, NIV, NASB, NET, and many others. Consequently, a comparison between the WEB and another modern Bible may reveal differences caused not by English style but by different Greek readings.

The WEB’s own recent editorial notes are candid about this complexity. In 2024 the project revised its preface to avoid implying that the New Testament was mechanically conformed to the Byzantine Majority Text in every reading. The current description is therefore best understood as Majority-Text-influenced rather than as a claim that every line follows a single Majority Text edition without exception.

The Divine Name: “Yahweh” and “LORD”

One of the most visible differences among WEB editions concerns the Hebrew divine name, the Tetragrammaton יהוה. The World English Bible Classic renders the name as “Yahweh”, making the proper name explicit to English readers. The Updated and British/International editions instead use the traditional English convention LORD, or GOD in contexts where “Lord” already represents Hebrew Adonai.5

This distinction allows communities to choose between two legitimate editorial preferences. Readers who value seeing the personal divine name directly can select the Classic edition. Readers accustomed to the long English and Jewish convention of substituting “LORD” can choose the Updated or British/International forms. The project notes that the editions are otherwise substantially the same apart from spelling conventions and the handling of the divine name.

Classic, Updated, British/International, Protestant, Catholic, and Ecumenical Forms

The phrase “World English Bible” now refers to a family of closely related editions. The official site distinguishes Classic, Updated, and British/International forms. Classic uses American spelling and “Yahweh.” Updated uses American spelling and “LORD/GOD.” British/International uses corresponding British spelling together with “LORD/GOD.” The project avoids, where practical, vocabulary that would have sharply different meanings in American and international English.

The WEB also offers different book sets. Protestant editions contain the familiar sixty-six books of the Old and New Testaments. Ecumenical editions include a larger collection of books commonly called the Deuterocanon or Apocrypha, while a Catholic edition presents the books recognized in the Catholic canon and orders them accordingly. The official site explicitly explains that these differences concern book selection and arrangement rather than the creation of fundamentally different translations of the shared books.6

For the additional books, the WEB project drew on public-domain sources including the Revised Version Apocrypha and Brenton’s English Septuagint, with subsequent modernization and revision. This reflects the larger strategy of the WEB: begin from legally reusable historical material, then improve and standardize it for modern digital and print use.

The 2020 Stable Text and Continuing Corrections

The year 2020 marks an important milestone. The WEB Classic reached what the project calls a stable text edition: the primary work of language modernization was considered complete, with future changes generally limited to verified typographical errors or significant problems.7 This stability matters for publishers because a public-domain Bible is far more useful when its wording is not constantly shifting.

“Stable,” however, does not mean digitally abandoned. eBible.org continues to maintain a public update log. As of 2026, that log records corrections to spelling, grammar, punctuation, footnotes, and occasional wording where the editors judge a clarification necessary. The Updated edition has also continued to receive selected language refinements. The better description, therefore, is that the Classic WEB reached a stable editorial state in 2020 while the broader WEB family remains actively maintained rather than frozen forever.

Public Domain, Trademark, and What “Free” Means

The WEB’s legal status is central to its mission. eBible.org states plainly that the Bible text is not copyrighted. Users may copy it, distribute it, publish it commercially, give it away, broadcast it, post it online, print it, memorize it, quote it, and build ministries or products around it without ordinary Bible-text licensing fees.8

At the same time, “World English Bible” is a trademark of eBible.org. The distinction is deliberate: the words of the translation are public domain, but the name is protected so that a substantially modified text cannot be presented misleadingly as if it were the official WEB. eBible.org grants use of the name and logo for faithful copies. If someone changes the actual text, the project asks that the altered work be given another name. This protects identity and textual integrity without withdrawing the public-domain freedom of the Bible itself.

A Bible Built for Digital Distribution

The WEB’s significance extends beyond its wording. It anticipated a world in which Scripture would circulate through websites, phones, apps, ebooks, audio players, print-on-demand systems, and downloadable data sets. A copyrighted translation can certainly serve those media, but each new distribution channel may involve permissions or contractual limitations. A public-domain translation removes that friction.

That freedom has made the WEB useful to software developers, small churches, missionaries, accessibility projects, independent publishers, audio ministries, and websites that could not justify commercial licensing arrangements. eBible itself distributes the text in multiple electronic and print-ready formats. The project’s model demonstrates how digital infrastructure can serve an old Christian purpose: placing Scripture into as many hands as possible.

Translation Character: Strengths and Trade-Offs

Readers who value formal correspondence will often appreciate the WEB. Its ASV ancestry gives it a relatively transparent relationship to Hebrew and Greek structures, while its modernization removes much of the linguistic distance of early twentieth-century English. Its prose is generally suitable for public reading, and its vocabulary is intentionally less idiomatic than translations that prioritize dynamic or functional equivalence.

No translation philosophy is without trade-offs. Formal renderings can preserve repeated words and grammatical structure but sometimes sound less natural. Majority-Text New Testament choices will differ from the critical-text choices familiar to readers of many contemporary translations. The several WEB editions can also cause confusion if a quotation is labeled merely “WEB” without noting whether the Classic or Updated form is intended. These are not defects so much as realities a careful reader should understand.

The WEB is therefore especially useful in comparison. Reading it beside the ASV reveals a century of English modernization; reading it beside an eclectic-critical-text translation highlights textual choices; comparing Classic and Updated editions shows how the divine name can be represented differently without changing the underlying Hebrew.

Use in the Church and the WEB’s Continuing Legacy

The World English Bible has never depended on a single denominational publishing system for its reach. Its public-domain status enables churches, individuals, and ministries to reproduce it freely, while its range of Protestant, Catholic, Ecumenical, American, and international forms makes it adaptable to different communities. The project itself presents the translation as suitable for Christians across denominational lines.

Its historical importance lies in the conjunction of three things: an established English Bible lineage through the ASV, a consciously modern digital workflow, and a public-domain theology of distribution. Earlier English Bibles became public domain through age; the WEB was intentionally designed to be freely reusable from the outset. That makes it one of the clearest examples of how internet-era Christians have used technology and open distribution to extend the long history of translating, copying, printing, and sharing Scripture.

For readers today, the WEB offers a serious, readable, freely shareable translation with a distinctive textual profile. For ministries and publishers, it offers something rarer: the ability to place a complete modern English Bible into digital or print projects without negotiating ordinary text royalties. In that respect the World English Bible continues the mission stated at its beginning—to make a trustworthy English Bible available for the widest possible circulation.
