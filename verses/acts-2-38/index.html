Bible Study: Acts 2:38

Acts 2:38 records Peter’s response when the Pentecost crowd asks, “Men and brethren, what shall we do?” Peter answers: “Repent, and be baptized every one of you in the name of Jesus Christ for the remission of sins, and ye shall receive the gift of the Holy Ghost.” The verse stands at the climax of Peter’s Pentecost sermon and gathers together repentance, baptism, forgiveness, Jesus’ name, and the Holy Spirit.

The story begins with the Spirit descending on Jesus’ followers, who speak in languages understood by pilgrims gathered in Jerusalem. Peter interprets the event through the prophet Joel and proclaims Jesus’ crucifixion, resurrection, and exaltation. The crowd’s question comes after hearing that God has made the crucified Jesus “both Lord and Christ.” Acts 2:38 is therefore not a detached ritual formula; it is the response to the gospel Peter has just preached.

“Repent” means a decisive turning. Biblical repentance includes changed thinking, but it is more than intellectual reconsideration. It involves turning from sin toward God. In Acts, repentance repeatedly appears alongside forgiveness and faith. Peter’s hearers are not told merely to feel guilty about Jesus’ death; they are summoned into a new allegiance shaped by the risen Messiah.

Baptism is the public and communal sign of that turning. “Be baptized every one of you in the name of Jesus Christ” identifies the new believer with Jesus. Early Christian baptism draws on Jewish washing traditions but takes on distinctive significance through Jesus’ death, resurrection, and lordship. In Acts, baptism normally follows reception of the gospel and marks incorporation into the community of disciples.

The phrase “for the remission of sins” has been debated in later Christian theology, especially regarding the precise relationship between faith, repentance, baptism, and forgiveness. Christian traditions arrange those relationships differently. Some emphasize baptism as a sacramental means through which God grants grace; others emphasize baptism as the commanded sign and confession of salvation received through faith. Acts 2 itself tightly joins the elements rather than presenting them as rival alternatives.

Peter also promises “the gift of the Holy Ghost.” Pentecost is not only an event for the apostles. Verse 39 extends the promise to the hearers, their children, and “all that are afar off, even as many as the Lord our God shall call.” As Acts unfolds, that horizon broadens dramatically to Samaritans and Gentiles. The Spirit is God’s gift to the expanding people formed around Jesus.

The sequence in Acts is not mechanically identical in every conversion story. In Acts 8, Samaritans are baptized and later receive the Spirit through apostolic prayer. In Acts 10, the Spirit falls on Gentiles before their baptism. These variations warn against reducing Acts 2:38 to a rigid timetable. Luke’s larger emphasis is that repentance, faith in Jesus, baptism, forgiveness, the Spirit, and incorporation into the church belong together in Christian initiation.

The verses after 2:38 show what this new life becomes. Those baptized devote themselves to apostolic teaching, fellowship, breaking of bread, and prayers. They share possessions, worship together, and care for one another. Salvation is not simply an individual spiritual transaction. It creates a community with new practices and loyalties.

For discipleship, Acts 2:38 insists that the gospel calls for response. The resurrection is not merely information to admire. People are invited to turn, identify themselves with Jesus, receive forgiveness, and live by the Spirit. The verse also places grace at the center: forgiveness and the Spirit are gifts from God, not rewards for religious achievement. The Pentecost crowd begins convicted and uncertain; Peter directs them toward the mercy and lordship of Christ. Their answer becomes the beginning of a life of teaching, prayer, fellowship, worship, and witness in the Spirit.

The Pentecost setting also helps Christians avoid turning repentance into private introspection. Peter’s hearers enter a public community with shared practices. Acts 2:42–47 describes teaching, fellowship, prayer, meals, generosity, and worship. The Spirit’s gift creates a people. This means the question “What shall we do?” receives both a personal and communal answer. Individuals repent and are baptized, but they do not remain isolated converts. They become part of a worshiping body shaped by apostolic teaching and care for one another. Modern application should preserve that connection. Christian initiation is not merely a private decision; it is entrance into a life of discipleship with other believers under the lordship of Jesus.

The result is not merely a changed opinion but a changed allegiance, publicly confessed and sustained within the life of the church.
