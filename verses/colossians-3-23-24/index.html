Bible Study: Colossians 3:23-24

Colossians 3:23–24 is frequently applied to jobs, school, ministry, household responsibilities, and everyday tasks: “whatsoever ye do, do it heartily, as to the Lord, and not unto men.” That application is valuable, but the original setting is difficult and must not be skipped. These words occur inside a Greco-Roman household code and are addressed directly to enslaved people. The gospel is speaking into a social structure in which many workers had little freedom and could be treated as property.

The command “do it heartily” literally has the sense of working “from the soul.” The enslaved believer’s work is not ultimately defined by the human master’s status or opinion. “Ye serve the Lord Christ.” That statement gives spiritual dignity to people whom Roman society often denied full social standing. Their deepest allegiance belongs to Christ, and His judgment outranks the judgment of earthly masters.

Verse 24 adds a striking promise: “of the Lord ye shall receive the reward of the inheritance.” In the ancient world, enslaved people normally did not inherit from masters as children did. Colossians announces that believers who may lack earthly inheritance nevertheless belong to a Lord who grants inheritance. The language lifts their identity above the social category imposed on them.

At the same time, Christians must acknowledge how household-code texts have been misused. Slaveholders and defenders of slavery quoted passages like this to demand obedience while ignoring the moral evil of owning human beings. Responsible reading cannot turn Paul’s pastoral instruction to vulnerable people inside an existing institution into divine approval of that institution. The New Testament contains seeds that challenge slavery’s logic: believers share one Lord, social status does not determine worth in Christ, and masters themselves stand under God’s judgment.

The next verse reinforces accountability: “he that doeth wrong shall receive for the wrong which he hath done: and there is no respect of persons.” Chapter 4 then tells masters to give servants what is just and equal, remembering that they also have a Master in heaven. The text therefore places every human authority under divine authority. A boss, employer, supervisor, parent, pastor, or political leader is never absolute.

When the principle is applied to modern work, “as to the Lord” gives ordinary labor spiritual significance. Christian vocation is not limited to church employment. Farming, teaching, caregiving, construction, administration, cleaning, engineering, customer service, study, and unpaid household labor can all be offered faithfully to God. The value of work does not depend entirely on prestige or applause.

Yet the verse should not be used to glorify exploitation. Working “heartily” does not require tolerating unsafe conditions, wage theft, harassment, or abuse. The Lord whom Christians serve is also the Lord of justice. Employees may appropriately seek fair pay, organize, report wrongdoing, change jobs, or protect themselves. Serving Christ means integrity in work, but it also means refusing to treat other workers as disposable.

The passage also challenges people who work primarily for recognition. Human praise fluctuates. Some excellent work goes unnoticed; some mediocre work is rewarded. Colossians gives believers a deeper audience. This does not mean earthly evaluation is irrelevant, but it frees Christians from making approval the ultimate measure of worth. Faithfulness matters even when no one is watching.

For discipleship, Colossians 3:23–24 asks three questions: What work has been entrusted to me? How am I doing it? Whom do I finally serve? The Christian answer transforms both mundane tasks and major responsibilities. Work can become an expression of love, honesty, creativity, service, and stewardship. But the verse also relativizes every workplace. Employers are not gods, careers are not identities, and success is not salvation. “Ye serve the Lord Christ.” That confession gives dignity to work while keeping work in its proper place.

The passage also reminds Christians that work is not the whole of life. Because Christ is Lord, no career can claim absolute devotion. Rest, worship, family, mercy, and care for neighbors remain part of faithful vocation. Working for the Lord should deepen integrity, not produce workaholism or neglect of other God-given responsibilities.

Because the text was first addressed to enslaved people, modern readers should be especially careful not to turn it into a tool for employers to demand endless sacrifice. The command is addressed to the worker’s allegiance to Christ, while the surrounding passage also places masters under divine judgment. Christian application therefore has two directions: workers are called to integrity, and those with authority are called to justice. The lordship of Christ dignifies labor while simultaneously limiting every human boss, institution, and economic system.

  • Theology of Work Project, “Honoring God at Work (Colossians 3:23–24)”. Discusses work in Jesus’ name, accountability, and honoring God through daily labor.
