Bible Study: Ephesians 4:2

Ephesians 4:2 describes the character needed for Christian unity: “with all lowliness and meekness, with longsuffering, forbearing one another in love.” The verse comes at a major turning point in Ephesians. Chapters 1–3 celebrate what God has done in Christ—grace, salvation, reconciliation, the breaking down of hostility, and the creation of one new humanity. Chapter 4 begins to ask what kind of life corresponds to that calling.

Verse 1 urges believers to “walk worthy of the vocation wherewith ye are called.” “Worthy” does not mean living well enough to earn God’s call. Ephesians has already insisted that salvation is by grace. The idea is that conduct should correspond to identity. Because God has reconciled people in Christ, their relationships should begin to look reconciled.

The first quality is “lowliness,” or humility. In the Greco-Roman world, humility was not always treated as a public virtue. Ephesians gives it a central place because the gospel undercuts boasting. Jews and Gentiles have both been reconciled by Christ; neither group can claim superiority. Humility therefore means refusing to place oneself above another member of the body.

“Meekness,” or gentleness, is not weakness. It is power governed by love. Gentleness refuses unnecessary harshness and domination. A gentle Christian can still speak truth, set boundaries, confront sin, and act decisively. What gentleness changes is the spirit in which those actions occur. The other person is not treated as an enemy to crush.

“Longsuffering” means patience or endurance with difficult circumstances and difficult people. Christian communities need patience because unity does not erase difference. Ephesians soon speaks of diverse gifts within one body. Members mature at different rates, understand issues differently, and carry different histories. Patience gives relationships room to survive disagreement and growth.

“Forbearing one another in love” assumes that people will sometimes have to bear with one another. The New Testament is realistic about community. Churches are not gatherings of perfectly compatible personalities. Love is needed precisely because irritation, misunderstanding, weakness, and difference are real. Forbearance is not pretending every behavior is acceptable; it is the commitment not to abandon relationship simply because another person is inconvenient.

The next verse gives the purpose: “endeavouring to keep the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace.” Christians do not manufacture the church’s deepest unity. The Spirit creates it. Believers are responsible to “keep,” guard, and embody that gift. Humility, gentleness, patience, and love are therefore not merely private personality traits; they are practices that protect a God-given unity.

Ephesians 4 also makes clear that unity is not uniformity. The one body contains diverse gifts—apostles, prophets, evangelists, pastors, teachers, and other members equipped for service. Mature unity does not eliminate difference; it orders difference toward the building up of the body. Attempts to force everyone into the same personality, culture, or secondary opinion can actually contradict the letter’s vision.

The verse is especially relevant in polarized communities. Christians can become convinced that faithfulness requires contempt for people on the other side of every disagreement. Ephesians offers a harder path. Truth matters—the chapter later commands “speaking the truth in love”—but truth and love are not competitors. Humility allows a believer to admit limits; gentleness controls tone; patience stays in the conversation; love seeks the other person’s good.

For discipleship, Ephesians 4:2 provides a practical test of whether theology has entered relationships. Grace should produce humility. Reconciliation should produce patience. Christ’s love should produce forbearance. The verse does not call Christians to become passive or conflict-avoidant. It calls them to handle conflict as people who know they themselves live by grace. The church walks worthy of its calling when the unity God has given becomes visible in the way believers treat one another.

These virtues are especially demanding because they are reciprocal. Every member of the body both gives and receives patience. Every believer will sometimes need another person’s forbearance. Humility grows when Christians remember that they are not only the ones tolerating weaknesses; they are also people whose own weaknesses are being carried by others.

There is also a strong connection between humility and truth. Humility does not require pretending convictions do not matter. Later in the chapter, the church is told to speak the truth in love. Humility changes the posture of that truth-telling: Christians remember that truth is received rather than owned, that they remain learners, and that the person being corrected is still a member or potential member of the body Christ loves. Gentleness and patience therefore protect truth from becoming an excuse for pride.
