The Holy Bible: Douay-Rheims — Tobit (Tobias)

Summary, History, Theology & Annotated Bibliography

Read Tobit 1:1

Overview

Tobit, traditionally called Tobias in the Douay-Rheims Bible, is one of the deuterocanonical books of the Old Testament and one of the most memorable narratives of Second Temple Jewish literature. Set among Israelites living under Assyrian rule, it combines family drama, wisdom teaching, prayer, angelic guidance, danger, healing, and hope. The United States Conference of Catholic Bishops describes the work as a religious novel written for instruction and edification, combining Jewish piety and morality with folklore and wisdom traditions. [1]

Tobit in Exile

Tobit identifies himself as a member of the tribe of Naphtali who has been taken into captivity and now lives in Nineveh. Although surrounded by the pressures of exile, he remains devoted to Israel’s God and law. He gives alms, supports fellow Israelites, remembers Jerusalem, and risks punishment by burying Israelites whose bodies have been left exposed. Charity, burial of the dead, prayer, family duty, and fidelity to God become recurring signs of righteousness. [1]

His virtue does not spare him from suffering. After carrying out a burial, Tobit sleeps outside and later becomes blind. His condition strains the household and leaves him dependent on Anna’s work. In grief, Tobit prays that he might die. His suffering establishes a central tension: severe affliction does not prove that God has abandoned the righteous.

Sarah’s Prayer and God’s Hidden Answer

At the same time in Ecbatana, Sarah, daughter of Raguel and Edna, faces her own crisis. Seven husbands have died before their marriages to her could be consummated, deaths attributed in the story to the demon Asmodeus. After being cruelly blamed for their deaths, Sarah is overwhelmed by shame. She considers ending her life but turns away because of the sorrow it would bring her father. Instead she prays to God.

The narrator deliberately joins Sarah’s prayer to Tobit’s. Both believe themselves trapped, yet both prayers are heard. God sends Raphael to heal Tobit and deliver Sarah. The structure establishes a central theme: divine providence may be moving toward an answer before anyone can see it. Raphael’s mission joins two households and two apparently unrelated tragedies into one story of restoration. [1]

Tobiah’s Journey and Raphael

Tobit remembers that he once deposited a large sum of silver with Gabael in Media and sends Tobiah to recover it. Before his son leaves, Tobit gives him moral instructions: honor his parents, remember God, reject sin, give to the poor, pay workers promptly, seek wise counsel, and remain faithful. These maxims connect Tobit with biblical wisdom literature. Major commentaries by Joseph A. Fitzmyer and Carey A. Moore examine the book within its early Jewish, literary, religious, social, and historical setting. [3] [4]

Tobiah needs a guide and meets a man calling himself Azariah. Neither Tobiah nor Tobit knows that the stranger is Raphael. During the journey, Tobiah encounters a large fish in the Tigris River. Raphael tells him to preserve its heart, liver, and gall. These unusual instructions later become part of Sarah’s deliverance and Tobit’s healing. The journey becomes the means through which both prayers are answered.

The Marriage of Tobiah and Sarah

Raphael leads Tobiah to Raguel’s household and explains that Sarah is his kinswoman and may properly become his wife. Tobiah fears what happened to her previous husbands, but Raphael reassures him. On their wedding night Tobiah and Sarah pray together, recalling God’s creation of man and woman and asking for mercy. Their union is presented in the context of fidelity, prayer, family responsibility, and dependence upon God.

Tobiah follows Raphael’s instructions concerning the fish, and Asmodeus is driven away. Raguel, expecting another tragedy, prepares for the possibility of a burial; instead the household discovers Tobiah alive, and anticipated mourning becomes a wedding celebration. Raphael then completes the journey’s original financial purpose by recovering Tobit’s money from Gabael.

Return, Healing, and Revelation

Tobiah, Sarah, and Raphael return toward Nineveh, where Anna has anxiously watched for her son. Raphael instructs Tobiah to use the fish’s gall in treating his father’s eyes, and Tobit’s sight is restored. Sarah has been freed from the terror surrounding her marriages, Tobit can see again, and the two families have been joined.

When Tobit and Tobiah try to reward their guide, Raphael reveals that he is an angel and that he presented their prayers before God. His disclosure explains the journey: the apparently ordinary traveler was the divine messenger connecting its events. Raphael also reinforces the book’s teaching about prayer, fasting, righteousness, and almsgiving. Providence is shown through charity, family loyalty, wise instruction, and help that may initially arrive unrecognized.

Tobit’s Song and the Fall of Nineveh

Tobit answers his healing with a hymn praising God’s justice and mercy and looking toward the restoration of Jerusalem and the gathering of God’s scattered people. The final chapter returns to the world of exile. Tobit instructs Tobiah, warns that Nineveh will fall, and eventually dies after a long life. Tobiah later leaves Nineveh with his family and lives long enough to hear of the city’s destruction, contrasting temporary imperial power with enduring faithfulness to God.

Date, Language, and Textual History

Although the story is set under the Assyrian Empire, modern scholarship generally places its composition much later. The USCCB dates the book probably to the early second century B.C. and notes that some of its chronology and geography function as features of a didactic narrative rather than precise historiography. [1] Fitzmyer’s Tobit is one of the standard modern scholarly commentaries on its language, history, theology, and early Jewish setting. [3]

The book’s textual history is especially important. For centuries it was known mainly through Greek and Latin forms. Discoveries at Qumran transformed the discussion: fragments of four Aramaic manuscripts and one Hebrew manuscript were found in Cave 4. The Israel Antiquities Authority’s Leon Levy Dead Sea Scrolls Digital Library preserves manuscript data and images for these witnesses, including the Aramaic 4Q196. The Semitic fragments substantially agree with the longer Greek recension represented by Codex Sinaiticus and confirm that Tobit circulated in Hebrew and Aramaic in antiquity. [5] Moore’s 1996 commentary was among the important modern studies to incorporate the Qumran Tobit evidence. [4]

Canon and Lasting Importance

In Catholic tradition, Tobit is inspired Scripture and belongs to the Old Testament deuterocanon. The Council of Trent’s Fourth Session, held April 8, 1546, explicitly lists “Tobias” among the canonical books. [2] It is also canonical in Eastern Orthodox and other ancient Christian traditions. Most Protestant Old Testaments do not include it among the canonical books, although Tobit has long appeared in collections of the Apocrypha.

The enduring appeal of Tobit lies in the way its theology is embodied in an intimate family story. Tobit suffers despite doing good. Sarah is humiliated despite her innocence. Anna fears her son may never return. Raguel expects a funeral on what should be a wedding night. Yet suffering is not the final word. Prayer is heard, mercy matters, marriage is treated as sacred, parents pass faith to their children, generosity toward the poor has lasting value, burial of the dead is honored, and divine providence may operate before its purpose becomes visible. Its central message is not that the faithful will avoid suffering, but that fidelity, prayer, charity, and trust remain meaningful even when God’s answer is not yet seen.
