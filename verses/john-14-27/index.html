Bible Study: John 14:27

John 14:27 records Jesus’ promise on the night before His death: “Peace I leave with you, my peace I give unto you: not as the world giveth, give I unto you.” He then repeats the chapter’s opening command: “Let not your heart be troubled, neither let it be afraid.” The verse is not spoken from a peaceful setting. Betrayal, denial, departure, arrest, and crucifixion are approaching.

John 14 belongs to the Farewell Discourse, where Jesus prepares the disciples for life after His departure. The disciples are destabilized. Judas has left, Peter’s failure has been predicted, and Jesus has told them He is going where they cannot immediately follow. Peace is therefore given to people whose circumstances are about to become more frightening.

Working Preacher notes an important tension: Jesus Himself has been “troubled” in John’s Gospel—at Lazarus’ tomb, in anticipation of His death, and before Judas’ betrayal. His command not to let the disciples’ hearts be troubled cannot mean that faithful people never experience distressing emotion.

The concern is specifically that His absence not destroy their trust. Jesus has promised that He will not leave them orphaned. The Father will send the Advocate, the Holy Spirit, who will teach and remind them of Jesus’ words. Peace belongs to this promise of continuing divine presence.

The Greek word for peace, eirēnē, stands within the biblical world of shalom. Working Preacher emphasizes that shalom means more than absence of conflict. It concerns wholeness, well-being, restored relationship, and the life that comes from God.

“My peace I give” also distinguishes Jesus’ peace from mere political stability. The Roman Empire celebrated the Pax Romana, peace secured by imperial force. John’s Gospel presents a different peace: one given by the crucified Christ and sustained by the Spirit.

This does not make Christian peace passive. Jesus will confront injustice and violence without surrendering to their logic. His followers are called to embody peace that can survive conflict without becoming hatred.

The verse should not be used to shame people with anxiety disorders, trauma, grief, or panic. “Let not your heart be troubled” is not a medical diagnosis. Christians may need therapy, medication, rest, supportive relationships, and practical safety while also receiving Jesus’ promise.

Nor does peace require ignoring danger. The disciples will soon face real persecution. Jesus openly tells them difficult things are coming. Biblical peace is therefore compatible with vigilance and wise preparation.

The phrase “not as the world giveth” also highlights the character of Jesus’ giving. Human gifts can be conditional, temporary, manipulative, or withdrawn. Jesus gives peace as part of His self-giving love and continuing presence.

The resurrection scenes reinforce this. When Jesus appears to frightened disciples behind locked doors in John 20, His repeated greeting is “Peace be unto you.” The promise of John 14 becomes resurrection reality.

Peace is also connected with mission. In John 20, Jesus speaks peace, sends the disciples, and breathes the Holy Spirit. Christian peace does not end in private serenity; it equips people to be sent into the world.

For discipleship, John 14:27 invites believers to receive peace as relationship with Christ rather than as perfect emotional control. The heart can be troubled and still return to trust. Fear can be present without becoming lord.

Prayer, Scripture, worship, friendship, confession, and the Spirit’s reminding work can all become ways believers receive again what Jesus says He has already given. Peace is not always a feeling that appears on command; it can be a deeper orientation sustained beneath unsettled emotions.

The verse also challenges churches to become communities of peace. If Jesus gives peace, Christian relationships should not be governed by intimidation, manipulation, or constant fear. Leadership that produces chronic terror contradicts the gift Christ leaves His disciples.

John 14:27 therefore offers peace sturdy enough for the night before the cross. It does not promise calm circumstances. It promises that Jesus’ departure is not abandonment, the Spirit will remain, and fear need not define the community’s future. The peace of Christ is the presence of God holding disciples together when the world around them is anything but peaceful.

The repeated resurrection greeting of peace confirms that the promise survives the cross. What Jesus gives before His death is what He speaks again after rising.

  • Working Preacher, “Commentary on John 14:23–29”. Explores peace, shalom, the Holy Spirit, and Jesus’ repeated command against troubled hearts.
  • Working Preacher, “Commentary on John 14:1–14”. Notes that Jesus Himself is troubled in John and places the disciples’ fear within Jesus’ promised continuing presence.
  • Yale Bible Study, “The Gospel of John → The Last Supper — Study Guide”. Academic treatment of John 13–17, footwashing, love, departure, and the Spirit.
  • Raymond E. Brown, The Gospel According to John XIII–XXI, Anchor Bible 29A (Doubleday, 1970). Classic commentary on the Farewell Discourse, Passion, and Resurrection.
