Bible Study: Exodus 20:14

Exodus 20:14 commands, “Thou shalt not commit adultery.” In its ancient setting, adultery referred specifically to sexual violation of a marriage covenant. The command protected marriage, family stability, inheritance, trust, and the social bonds on which households depended. Although ancient Israel’s social assumptions differed from modern ones, the enduring moral core is clear: covenant relationships are not to be treated casually or betrayed for private desire.

The command appears within the Decalogue given to Israel after liberation from Egypt. The covenant begins with God’s faithfulness to His people, and the commandments call Israel into corresponding faithfulness. Adultery is therefore more than a private sexual mistake. It is a breach of trust within a covenant relationship.

Ancient marriage practices were patriarchal, and biblical law reflects that world. A married woman’s adultery violated her husband’s marriage rights, while a man’s adultery was defined in relation to another man’s wife. Modern readers should acknowledge that historical context rather than pretending the law arose in a social world identical to ours. At the same time, biblical theology develops toward a strong expectation of mutual fidelity between spouses.

Jesus intensifies the command in Matthew 5 by addressing lust. His point is not that sexual attraction itself is equivalent to adultery, but that the moral life includes the way another person is regarded in the heart. A person can violate the dignity of another by reducing that person to an object of possession or fantasy even before an outward act occurs. Jesus’ teaching moves attention from legal boundaries toward the desires that can erode faithfulness.

This internal dimension connects the adultery command with the later prohibition on coveting a neighbor’s spouse. The Decalogue does not only regulate actions; it also recognizes that destructive actions often begin with disordered desire. Christian discipleship therefore includes learning to see other people as neighbors to love rather than objects to acquire.

The command also challenges the idea that sexual behavior is purely private. Adultery can affect spouses, children, extended families, friendships, churches, and communities. The damage involves secrecy, betrayal, broken promises, and the loss of trust. Biblical sexual ethics takes those relational consequences seriously.

At the same time, Christians must avoid using this command as a weapon of humiliation. The New Testament joins sexual ethics with grace, repentance, forgiveness, and restoration. Adultery is serious, but it is not an unforgivable sin. People who have failed sexually are not excluded from the possibility of repentance and renewed faithfulness.

The command should also never be used to pressure someone to remain in an abusive or dangerous marriage. Fidelity does not require tolerating violence, coercion, exploitation, or threats. Christian traditions differ on divorce and remarriage, but protecting a person from abuse is not the same as celebrating covenant betrayal.

Modern technology introduces new contexts in which the command’s principles matter. Secret online relationships, pornography, emotional affairs, and digital deception can undermine marital fidelity even when no physical affair occurs. The exact ethical analysis may differ across situations, but the guiding question remains whether a person is honoring the promises, exclusivity, trust, and dignity that belong to covenant relationship.

Faithfulness also requires honesty before temptation becomes betrayal. Wise boundaries, truthful communication, accountability, and care for the marriage relationship are preventative practices rather than signs of weakness. The command protects trust by asking people to act before secrecy has matured into covenant-breaking.

For discipleship, Exodus 20:14 calls Christians toward faithfulness rather than merely rule avoidance. Married believers are called to honor their vows through truthfulness, sexual integrity, communication, forgiveness, tenderness, and appropriate boundaries. Unmarried believers are called to honor the marriages of others and to treat other people’s bodies and relationships with respect.

The deeper biblical pattern is covenant loyalty. God is repeatedly described as faithful even when His people are not. Human marriage is never a perfect copy of divine faithfulness, but it can become a place where promises are taken seriously, trust is protected, and love is practiced over time. “Thou shalt not commit adultery” therefore protects more than a rule; it protects the integrity of relationships that depend on faithful love.

The command also reminds spouses that fidelity is positive as well as negative. Marriage is strengthened by attention, honesty, affection, shared responsibility, and the willingness to repair breaches of trust. Avoiding adultery is the boundary; cultivating faithful love is the fuller vocation.
