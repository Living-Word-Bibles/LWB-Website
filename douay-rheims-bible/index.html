The Holy Bible: Douay-Rheims (DRB)

Annotated History, Canon, & Bibliography

Read the Douay-Rheims Bible

Overview

The Douay-Rheims Bible is one of the great monuments of English Catholic Christianity. Produced by English Catholic scholars living in continental Europe during the religious upheavals of the sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries, it gave English-speaking Catholics a complete vernacular Bible rooted in the Latin Vulgate tradition. The New Testament was printed at Reims in 1582; the Old Testament followed at Douai in two volumes in 1609 and 1610.1 The name “Douay-Rheims” therefore joins the two cities associated with its publication.

The Bible most readers encounter today under that name is usually not the untouched sixteenth-century text. During 1749–1752, Bishop Richard Challoner substantially revised the translation, removing many difficult Latinisms, modernizing obsolete English, shortening or replacing notes, and drawing its diction closer in many places to the English biblical tradition represented by the King James Version. Modern scholarship estimates that Challoner altered about fifteen percent of the text, making the familiar “Douay-Rheims-Challoner” a major historical revision rather than merely a respelled reprint.2

Taken across its long history, the Douay-Rheims is therefore best understood as both a translation and a tradition of revision. The original Rheims and Douai volumes preserve the concerns of English Catholics in the Counter-Reformation; Challoner’s version reflects the needs of eighteenth-century Catholics living in a changed linguistic and religious environment. Together they shaped Catholic biblical vocabulary, devotional reading, catechesis, apologetics, and English Bible history for centuries.

England’s Reformation and the Need for an English Catholic Bible

The Douay-Rheims arose from a divided Christian Europe. By the middle of the sixteenth century, Protestant English translations—including the work of William Tyndale, the Great Bible, the Geneva Bible, and later the Bishops’ Bible—had made vernacular Scripture central to English Protestant life. English Catholics faced a different problem. They lived through the break with Rome, the restoration of Catholicism under Mary I, and the Elizabethan religious settlement, while many Catholic clergy and students continued their formation in exile.

In 1568, William Allen founded the English College at Douai in the Spanish Netherlands. The institution trained priests for ministry among English Catholics and became an intellectual center of the English Catholic exile. Political circumstances forced the college to move temporarily to Reims in 1578. It was during this Reims period that the New Testament translation was completed and printed. When the college later returned to Douai, the Old Testament was eventually issued there.

The project was therefore not simply literary. It was pastoral, theological, and apologetic. English Catholics needed a Bible they could read in their own language while remaining connected to the Latin scriptural tradition used throughout Western Catholic worship and theology. The translators also wanted to answer Protestant interpretations that they believed depended partly on disputed translation choices. The result was a Bible whose text, prefaces, marginal notes, glossary, and controversial annotations all participated in the confessional debates of the age.

Gregory Martin and the Translation Team

The principal translator was Gregory Martin, an Oxford-trained scholar and former fellow of St John’s College. Historical accounts describe Martin beginning the main translation work in 1578 and producing one or two chapters a day. His work was then reviewed, criticized, and corrected by colleagues associated with the English College, particularly William Allen, Richard Bristow, Thomas Worthington, and William Reynolds. Bristow, Allen, and Worthington were especially important in preparing the extensive annotations that accompanied the Rheims New Testament.3

This collaborative process matters because the Douay-Rheims was never merely the private rendering of one translator. Martin supplied the central linguistic labor, but the published Bible emerged from a community engaged in theology, patristics, biblical languages, pastoral formation, and controversy. The English College saw itself as preparing Scripture for a persecuted and dispersed Catholic community. Its Bible therefore combined translation with explanation: readers were not only given English words but also notes explaining doctrine, disputed passages, traditional readings, and the reasons for particular choices.

Why Translate from the Latin Vulgate?

The defining textual characteristic of the original Douay-Rheims is its close dependence on the Latin Vulgate. The Vulgate was the Bible of the Western Church’s liturgy, theology, preaching, and education for many centuries. Much of it derived from the work of St. Jerome in the late fourth and early fifth centuries, alongside older Latin forms preserved within the medieval textual tradition.

The translators did not believe Hebrew and Greek were irrelevant. Martin and his colleagues were educated men capable of consulting the original languages, and later Douay-Rheims title pages explicitly described the Vulgate text as “diligently compared with the Hebrew, Greek, and other editions in divers languages.” Nevertheless, their governing English base was Latin. They considered the Vulgate a uniquely important ecclesial witness because it had been received, read, argued from, and prayed through across Western Christendom.

That decision also followed the Council of Trent. In its Fourth Session on April 8, 1546, Trent listed the books it received as sacred and canonical and declared the old Latin Vulgate “authentic” for public readings, disputations, sermons, and exposition after its long use in the Church.4 It is important not to exaggerate what this meant. Catholic teaching did not thereby declare Hebrew and Greek useless or prohibit translation from the original languages. In 1943, Pope Pius XII explicitly explained that the Vulgate’s doctrinal authority did not prevent—and in scholarship often required—recourse to the original texts, and that Trent did not forbid vernacular translations made directly from them.5

The Douay-Rheims thus belongs to a particular historical moment when English Catholic translators deliberately emphasized continuity with the Church’s Latin Bible. Its wording often preserves that connection visibly, sometimes at the cost of natural English. That feature is central to both its historical importance and its distinctive sound.

The Rheims New Testament of 1582

The New Testament appeared first because the translators considered it the more urgent pastoral need and because finances were limited. Printed at Reims in 1582, it arrived nearly three decades before the King James Version. Its full title announced that it was translated “faithfully into English, out of the authentical Latin,” and it included extensive annotations and other study material.

The Rheims Testament was deliberately conservative in vocabulary. Where translators believed ordinary English words carried Protestant theological associations or failed to represent the Latin adequately, they sometimes transferred a Latin-derived term into English. Critics mocked this practice, and even Catholic historians later acknowledged that the original could be cumbersome. Yet several Latinate words associated with Rheims eventually became familiar in English religious or general vocabulary. Modern scholarship has connected the translation with terms such as advent, evangelize, neophyte, victim, and other Latin-derived expressions, although individual histories of English words can be complex.6

The annotations were equally distinctive. The 1582 volume was produced amid sharp disputes over the Church, sacraments, justification, tradition, ecclesiastical authority, and interpretation. Its notes frequently defended Catholic doctrine against Protestant arguments. An index of controversies directed readers to passages relevant to disputed teachings. For modern readers, these annotations are valuable historical evidence: they show not merely what the translators thought a verse meant, but how biblical translation operated within the confessional struggle of Elizabethan England.

The Original Annotations as a Catholic Study Apparatus

The early Douay-Rheims project was not conceived as bare biblical text. The Rheims New Testament in particular surrounded Scripture with prefaces, marginal material, explanatory notes, a glossary of difficult words, and an index directing the reader to passages involved in religious controversy. Recent scholarship on early modern Bible paratexts emphasizes that these features were part of the book’s argument: the editors wanted readers to encounter Scripture within a Catholic interpretive framework rather than treat the English wording as detached from the Church’s theological tradition.

This apparatus also helps explain the intensity of Protestant reaction. William Fulke’s well-known response printed the Rheims text and its claims in a format designed to refute them point by point. Ironically, that polemical strategy helped preserve and circulate the Catholic translation among Protestant readers and scholars. The annotations thus belong to the history of the English Bible almost as much as the translated text itself. They show how sixteenth-century Christians argued over not only which English words should represent Scripture, but also who possessed authority to interpret those words, how tradition should guide reading, and how doctrinal vocabulary should be carried from Latin Christianity into English.

The Douai Old Testament, 1609–1610

The Old Testament translation was substantially prepared long before it appeared in print, but financial limitations delayed publication. After the English College returned to Douai, Thomas Worthington helped bring the work to completion. The Old Testament finally appeared in two volumes in 1609 and 1610.7 With those volumes, English Catholics possessed a complete Bible corresponding to the canon received by the Catholic Church.

The Old Testament continued the New Testament’s close Vulgate orientation. Readers encounter forms of names familiar from Latin ecclesiastical usage—Josue for Joshua, Paralipomenon for Chronicles, Osee for Hosea, Aggeus for Haggai, Machabees for Maccabees, and other forms. These titles do not indicate different biblical stories; they reflect the naming conventions transmitted through the Greek and Latin biblical traditions.

The Catholic Canon and the Deuterocanonical Books

The Douay-Rheims contains the Catholic Old Testament canon. The Catholic Church counts 46 books in the Old Testament and 27 in the New Testament. The seven Old Testament books not included in the standard Protestant thirty-nine-book Old Testament are Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch, 1 Maccabees, and 2 Maccabees. Catholic editions also contain additional material in Esther and Daniel. The United States Conference of Catholic Bishops describes these works as the deuterocanonical books and identifies them as part of the Catholic canon.8

The word deuterocanonical does not mean “less inspired” in Catholic usage. It distinguishes books whose canonical status was disputed in some settings from those more universally received earlier. Trent’s 1546 decree enumerated Tobias, Judith, Wisdom, Ecclesiasticus (Sirach), Baruch with Jeremiah, and the two books of Maccabees among the sacred books, and received the biblical books “with all their parts” as they had been read in the Catholic Church and contained in the old Latin Vulgate.9

This difference in canon is one reason a Douay-Rheims Bible feels structurally different from many Protestant Bibles. Books such as Wisdom and Sirach appear within the Old Testament rather than in a separate “Apocrypha” section, and the additions to Esther and Daniel are treated as Scripture rather than detached supplements.

Psalm Numbering and Other Navigational Differences

Readers comparing the DRB with many contemporary English Bibles may also notice different Psalm numbers. The Latin Vulgate follows the traditional Greek Septuagint division of several Psalms, while most modern English translations follow the Hebrew Masoretic numbering. The USCCB notes that the Greek/Vulgate sequence is therefore commonly one number behind the Hebrew sequence through a substantial portion of the Psalter.10

The familiar “Psalm 23” in Hebrew-numbered Protestant Bibles, for example, corresponds to Psalm 22 in the traditional Vulgate numbering. Both systems still contain 150 canonical Psalms; the difference comes from combining or dividing particular compositions differently. This is a matter of numbering, not missing Scripture. Similar differences in book titles—1 and 2 Paralipomenon, 1–4 Kings in older Vulgate conventions, or Apocalypse for Revelation—reflect inherited Latin naming and arrangement.

Language: Literal Latinism, Precision, and Difficulty

The original Douay-Rheims is famous for what might be called ecclesiastical literalism. Its translators frequently kept Latin sentence structure and vocabulary close to the surface. In some places that practice preserves relationships with the Vulgate that smoother English would hide. In others it produces sentences that feel foreign even to readers accustomed to the King James Version.

This was partly intentional. The translators believed that familiar theological terms carried accumulated meaning within the Church and should not be casually replaced. They were wary of translation choices that, in their judgment, smuggled doctrinal interpretation into the English text. Their preference for terms connected to Latin Christian usage therefore reflected both linguistic caution and Catholic identity.

The cost was readability. The early twentieth-century Catholic Encyclopedia, while defending the scholarship of the translation, described the original as burdened by Latinisms and at times difficult to read. Challoner’s later success can be explained largely by his willingness to retain the Catholic textual tradition while making the English far more usable.

The Rheims New Testament and the King James Version

The history of English Bible translation is not a simple sequence of isolated Catholic and Protestant streams. The Rheims New Testament became part of the textual environment from which the King James Version of 1611 emerged. Cambridge scholar David Norton describes the KJV as the culmination of earlier English work including Tyndale, Coverdale, the Great Bible, Geneva, the Bishops’ Bible, and the Rheims New Testament.11

One route of influence was controversy itself. Protestant scholar William Fulke printed the Rheims text beside the Bishops’ Bible with extensive refutation. That hostile comparative format nevertheless placed Rheims wording directly in front of Protestant readers and scholars. Modern work on early modern Bible paratexts notes that Fulke’s edition helped transmit the Rheims New Testament to the circle from which the King James translation emerged.

Influence does not mean that the KJV became a Vulgate translation or that the two versions shared the same theology. The KJV translators worked principally with Hebrew and Greek and under their own translation rules. But English wording travels across confessional boundaries, and later research has identified places where Rheims diction or phrasing entered the broader English biblical register. The story is therefore reciprocal: Rheims affected the English language available to later Protestant translators, and a century and a half later the KJV would significantly influence Challoner’s Catholic revision.

Richard Challoner and the Bible Most Readers Know as “Douay-Rheims”

By the eighteenth century, the original Douay-Rheims had become linguistically remote. Bishop Richard Challoner (1691–1781), an important leader of English Catholicism during the penal era, undertook a thorough revision. His first revised New Testament appeared in 1749; another New Testament and an Old Testament followed in 1750; a third New Testament edition appeared in 1752 with substantial additional changes.12

Challoner removed many obscure literal renderings, replaced obsolete expressions, shortened the massive controversial apparatus, altered punctuation and presentation, and made the Bible more practical in size and price. He also printed verses separately, a format increasingly familiar to readers. These changes made the text much more accessible for ordinary English-speaking Catholics.

The degree of revision was considerable. The Catholic Encyclopedia records earlier Catholic judgments that the familiar Challoner Bible differed so much from the original that calling it simply the old Douay or Rheims version could be misleading. Modern textual research by Leslie K. Arnovick and Henry Ansgar Kelly reaches a more measured but still striking conclusion: Challoner changed roughly 15 percent of the text, and a large proportion of his changes coincided with King James wording.13

Challoner, the KJV, and a Shared English Biblical Register

Challoner’s use of KJV-style diction is one of the most fascinating episodes in English Bible history. He was not converting the Catholic Bible into a Protestant translation. He retained its Catholic canon, Vulgate orientation, ecclesiastical approval, and Catholic explanatory tradition. Yet he recognized that generations of English readers had come to associate the cadences of the Authorized Version with the sound of Scripture.

Oxford scholarship has shown that many of Challoner’s changes parallel the KJV and that much of that apparently “Protestant” language ultimately descended from Tyndale and the common inheritance of English biblical prose. Challoner could therefore improve readability by drawing from an English register familiar beyond confessional boundaries while maintaining a distinctly Catholic Bible. The result was a hybrid in the best historical sense: Catholic in canon and ecclesial use, Vulgate-rooted in textual heritage, but increasingly at home in the classic English style shared across seventeenth- and eighteenth-century Christianity.

Haydock, Kenrick, and Nineteenth-Century Catholic Editions

Challoner did not end the story. The nineteenth century saw numerous editions adapted for different Catholic audiences. The famous Haydock Bible, associated with Father George Leo Haydock, began publication in Manchester in 1811–1812 and surrounded the biblical text with extensive commentary assembled from Catholic interpreters. It became a major family and study Bible in the English-speaking Catholic world.

In the United States, Bishop and later Archbishop Francis Patrick Kenrick produced another important nineteenth-century revision and translation effort. Publishers also issued heavily illustrated family Bibles and editions carrying episcopal approbations. Google Books preserves American printings from the nineteenth century whose title pages explicitly identify the Old Testament as first published at Douay and the New Testament at Rheims while describing the whole as revised and compared with the Vulgate.14

These editions show why “the Douay-Rheims” should be approached as a textual family. Typography, spelling, notes, Challoner-derived readings, and later editorial decisions vary across printings. A scholar quoting a historically significant wording should therefore identify the edition whenever possible.

Catholic Biblical Scholarship Beyond the Vulgate Era

The Douay-Rheims remained deeply influential, but Catholic translation practice did not stop with it. Modern Catholic biblical scholarship increasingly returned directly to Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek critical texts. Pope Pius XII’s Divino Afflante Spiritu encouraged rigorous use of the original languages and textual criticism while reaffirming the Vulgate’s ecclesial importance. Later Catholic translations, including the New American Bible tradition, were produced directly from the biblical languages rather than by translating the Latin Vulgate into English.

This development helps place the DRB properly. Catholics need not choose between respecting the Vulgate tradition and recognizing the value of the original-language textual witnesses. The DRB is historically important precisely because it represents a particular stage in that history: the English rendering of a Latin Bible that had shaped Western Christianity for more than a millennium.

Strengths of the Douay-Rheims for Readers Today

The DRB remains valuable for several reasons. First, it preserves a direct connection with traditional Catholic biblical language. Readers of older catechisms, spiritual classics, theology, sermons, and devotional literature often encounter Scriptural phrases that correspond more naturally to the Douay-Rheims-Challoner than to a contemporary translation.

Second, its Vulgate orientation makes it useful for historical comparison. A reader can set the DRB beside a modern Hebrew- and Greek-based translation and observe how the Latin tradition mediated particular passages. This is especially fruitful when studying patristic and medieval theology, whose writers often knew Scripture through forms closely related to the Vulgate.

Third, the Challoner text possesses a dignified English style that many readers find suitable for prayer and memorization. Its “thee,” “thou,” and older vocabulary create distance from ordinary speech, but for some readers that distance contributes to a sense of reverence. The same feature, however, can make it less immediately accessible to newcomers or younger readers.

Limitations and Responsible Historical Use

A responsible appreciation of the DRB should also recognize its limits. The original translation was made primarily from the Vulgate rather than directly from the best Hebrew and Greek critical texts now available. Biblical textual criticism has advanced enormously since the sixteenth century through discoveries of manuscripts, better editions, improved knowledge of ancient languages, and comparison of witnesses such as the Dead Sea Scrolls.

The original Rheims-Douai English can be extremely Latinate; the Challoner text is much smoother but also substantially revised. Therefore statements about “what the Douay-Rheims says” should distinguish, where it matters, between the original and Challoner forms. Likewise, the DRB’s Psalm numbering and book names can confuse readers working with modern reference systems.

None of these facts diminishes the translation’s historical significance. They clarify what kind of Bible it is. The Douay-Rheims is a witness to the Vulgate tradition, English Catholic exile, Counter-Reformation scholarship, the cross-confessional development of English biblical prose, and centuries of Catholic devotion. It is strongest when read with awareness of that history.

Public-Domain Status and Modern Availability

The historic Douay-Rheims and Challoner texts are in the public domain in the United States, which is why they are widely reproduced in websites, apps, ebooks, print editions, and searchable databases. Public-domain status has helped give the DRB a renewed digital life: a translation born in exile and early modern print culture can now be consulted instantly around the world.

Modern editions may contain newly copyrighted introductions, typesetting, notes, artwork, or editorial apparatus, so the status of a particular publisher’s added material should be distinguished from the underlying historic Scripture text. The text itself, however, remains one of the major freely distributable historic English Catholic translations.

Why the Douay-Rheims Matters

The Douay-Rheims matters because it preserves more than an old style of English. It records a moment when Catholics in exile believed that the survival of their community required an English Bible faithful to their ecclesial tradition. It embodies the immense historical role of the Latin Vulgate. Its annotations preserve the intellectual conflicts of the Reformation era. Its vocabulary left traces in English religious speech. Its New Testament participated, directly and indirectly, in the world from which the King James Version emerged. Challoner’s revision then demonstrated that biblical language could cross confessional boundaries in the opposite direction.

For Catholic readers, it remains a link to generations who prayed, taught, defended the faith, and read Scripture through its pages. For Protestant readers, it offers a window into the parallel Catholic history of the English Bible. For historians, linguists, and students of translation, it shows how doctrine, textual authority, language, politics, publishing, and devotion converge in the making of a Bible.

The enduring lesson is that Bible translation is never merely the exchange of one word for another. Translators inherit manuscripts, churches, languages, controversies, literary traditions, and pastoral responsibilities. The Douay-Rheims makes those layers unusually visible. More than four centuries after the first Rheims New Testament appeared, it remains a remarkable testimony to the determination of Christians to hear and preserve the Holy Scriptures in the language of their people.
