Ruth

Read Ruth Now

Overview and Place in Scripture

Ruth is a short narrative of loss, loyalty, providence, and family restoration set “in the days when the judges ruled.” In Christian Bibles it follows Judges, creating a deliberate contrast: Judges ends with civil violence and social chaos, while Ruth tells a story of faithfulness among ordinary people. In the Jewish canon Ruth is one of the Five Scrolls and is traditionally read at Shavuot. The book follows Naomi, a Judean widow who returns from Moab to Bethlehem, and Ruth, her Moabite daughter-in-law, who refuses to abandon her. Through gleaning in the fields of Boaz, careful use of Israelite kinship customs, and the eventual birth of Obed, the story moves from emptiness to fullness. Its final genealogy connects Ruth to David, making a foreign woman part of Israel’s royal ancestry.

Literary Shape

Ruth is one of the Bible’s most crafted short stories. Chapter 1 moves from famine, migration, death, and Naomi’s bitter return to Bethlehem. Chapter 2 introduces Boaz and places Ruth in his field during harvest. Chapter 3 shifts to the threshing floor, where Naomi and Ruth seek a secure future through the responsibilities of a family redeemer. Chapter 4 brings the legal matter to the town gate, resolves the nearer relative’s claim, records Boaz’s marriage to Ruth, and ends with the birth of Obed and a genealogy leading to David. The story turns famine into harvest, widowhood into family, foreignness into belonging, and Naomi’s emptiness into renewed life. The narrative’s theological power lies largely in these ordinary transformations rather than in miracles or spectacular divine interventions.

Authorship, Date, and Historical Setting

Ruth does not name an author. Rabbinic tradition sometimes associated the book with Samuel, but modern scholars propose dates ranging from the monarchic period to the Persian era. The story is set before Israel’s monarchy, yet its language, genealogy, and treatment of intermarriage have led many scholars to see a later literary purpose. One influential proposal reads Ruth as a countervoice to postexilic restrictions on marriages with foreign women in Ezra and Nehemiah: Ruth is a Moabite whose loyalty, faith, and contribution to Israel’s future are beyond question. Whatever its date, the narrative knows Israelite agricultural practice, kinship obligations, local legal customs, and traditions concerning Moab. Its final form uses an ancient setting to ask enduring questions about family, belonging, loyalty, and God’s providential care.

Naomi, Grief, and Honest Faith

The book begins with Naomi’s suffering. Famine drives her family from Bethlehem to Moab; her husband Elimelech dies; her sons Mahlon and Chilion marry Moabite women and later die as well. Naomi returns to Judah convinced that the hand of the Lord has gone out against her. She asks to be called Mara, “bitter,” because she believes the Almighty has dealt bitterly with her. Ruth does not correct or rebuke Naomi’s grief. Instead, the narrative allows lament to stand while slowly changing her circumstances. That honesty makes Naomi a recognizable portrait of wounded faith. She still speaks about God, but she interprets her life through loss. The book’s movement toward restoration does not make her earlier grief unreal; it shows how hope can emerge without denying suffering.

Ruth’s Loyalty and the Meaning of Hesed

Ruth’s speech to Naomi is among the best-known declarations of loyalty in the Bible: “whither thou goest, I will go,” and “thy people shall be my people, and thy God my God.” In context, the words concern far more than romantic sentiment. Ruth leaves her homeland, kinship network, and prospects in Moab to accompany an older widow with little visible security. Her loyalty embodies the covenant virtue often described by the Hebrew word hesed: steadfast kindness, faithful love, or covenant loyalty. Ruth’s decision also complicates ethnic boundaries. She is repeatedly called “the Moabitess,” yet the book’s moral center rests on her faithfulness. She becomes an Israelite not through ancestry but through allegiance, shared life, and the community’s eventual recognition of her place among them.

Gleaning, Poverty, and Boaz

Ruth and Naomi survive through gleaning, the practice by which the poor could gather grain left behind in harvested fields. Torah laws in Leviticus and Deuteronomy instruct landowners not to reap every edge and to leave produce for the poor, widow, orphan, and foreigner. Ruth’s work in Boaz’s field therefore places the story at the intersection of charity, law, labor, and vulnerability. Boaz goes beyond the minimum requirement. He protects Ruth from harassment, orders workers to leave extra grain, invites her to eat, and recognizes her loyalty to Naomi. His generosity illustrates what covenant law looks like when practiced with compassion. At the same time, Ruth is not passive. She works, asks questions, takes initiative, and becomes an active participant in the restoration of the household.

Redeemer, Kinship, and the Threshing Floor

The Hebrew term goel, often translated “kinsman-redeemer,” refers to a close relative with responsibilities for protecting family interests, including property and vulnerable kin. Ruth combines this idea with customs related to preserving a deceased man’s family line, though the situation does not correspond exactly to the levirate marriage law of Deuteronomy 25 because Boaz is not the dead man’s brother. In chapter 3 Naomi instructs Ruth to approach Boaz at the threshing floor. The scene is intentionally charged with ambiguity and vulnerability, yet Ruth asks Boaz to “spread” his garment or wing over her because he is a redeemer. Boaz responds by praising her loyalty and promising to act honorably, while acknowledging that another relative has a prior claim. The chapter therefore balances risk, initiative, sexuality, and legal responsibility without depicting Boaz as simply taking what he wants.

The Gate, Marriage, and Restoration

In chapter 4 Boaz resolves the matter publicly at the town gate before elders. The nearer relative is willing to redeem family property but withdraws when Ruth and the preservation of the deceased line become part of the obligation. Boaz then acquires the right to marry Ruth and assumes responsibility for the family estate. The community blesses Ruth by invoking Rachel, Leah, and Tamar, placing the Moabite widow among important women in Israel’s ancestral story. When Ruth gives birth to Obed, the women of Bethlehem speak of the child as a restorer of Naomi’s life. The restoration is communal and intergenerational, not merely a romance between Ruth and Boaz.

Ruth, David, and Christian Reception

The genealogy at the end of Ruth identifies Obed as the father of Jesse and grandfather of David. This connection gives the story national significance. Israel’s celebrated king has a Moabite woman in his ancestry, a fact that challenges narrow assumptions about purity of descent. The Gospel of Matthew later includes Ruth by name in Jesus’s genealogy, along with Tamar, Rahab, and “the wife of Uriah.” Christian readers have often seen Boaz as a type of Christ, though this should not replace the story’s own social and legal meaning. Ruth is first a book about God working through the faithfulness of women, the generosity of a landowner, community law, and the survival of a vulnerable family. Its messianic significance grows from that concrete story rather than bypassing it.

Why Ruth Matters

Ruth offers a theology of providence in which God’s work is usually indirect. The book contains no sea crossing, prophetic miracle, or heavenly vision. Instead, “chance” brings Ruth to Boaz’s field; covenant law creates room for the poor to survive; courage sustains Naomi and Ruth; and ordinary acts of kindness become the means of restoration. The story honors grief, a foreign woman’s identity, and social responsibility as part of faithfulness to God. It also shows that the biblical story of redemption often advances through people at the margins rather than through obvious centers of power. For modern readers, Ruth remains a profound account of loyalty, migration, poverty, belonging, family obligation, and the quiet ways providence can work through human faithfulness.

Annotated Bibliography (Chicago Style)

  • Hubbard, Robert L., Jr. The Book of Ruth. New International Commentary on the Old Testament. Grand Rapids: Eerdmans, 1988. A substantial exegetical commentary on Hebrew language, kinship customs, literary structure, theology, and Ruth’s place in the canon.
  • Campbell, Edward F., Jr. Ruth: A New Translation with Introduction, Notes, and Commentary. Anchor Bible 7. Garden City, NY: Doubleday, 1975. A classic historical and philological commentary with careful attention to social customs, narrative art, and textual questions.
  • Bush, Frederic W. Ruth/Esther. Word Biblical Commentary 9. Dallas: Word Books, 1996. Detailed commentary combining textual criticism, linguistic study, literary structure, historical setting, and theological interpretation.
  • Block, Daniel I. Judges, Ruth. New American Commentary 6. Nashville: Broadman & Holman, 1999. An evangelical commentary particularly strong on covenant loyalty, the redeemer institution, social ethics, and canonical theology.
  • LaCocque, André. Ruth: A Continental Commentary. Minneapolis: Fortress Press, 2004. A literary and theological reading attentive to Ruth’s foreign identity, social marginality, gender, law, and the book’s challenge to exclusion.
  • Sasson, Jack M. Ruth: A New Translation with a Philological Commentary and a Formalist-Folklorist Interpretation. Sheffield: JSOT Press, 1989. Specialized study of the Hebrew text, storytelling technique, folklore patterns, and the book’s literary construction.
  • Yale Bible Study. “The Book of Ruth, Part 1” and “The Book of Ruth, Part 2.” In Ruth, Song of Songs, Lamentations, and Esther. yalebiblestudy.org. Accessed August 28, 2026. Free scholarly study guides on Ruth’s canonical location, gender, foreignness, Naomi, Boaz, social conventions, and subversion of expectations.
  • Bible Odyssey. “Bethlehem.” Society of Biblical Literature. bibleodyssey.org/dictionary/bethlehem/. Accessed August 28, 2026. Concise reference material on Bethlehem’s role in Ruth, Davidic tradition, and later biblical memory.
