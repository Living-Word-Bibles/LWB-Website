1 Corinthians

Read 1 Corinthians Now

Overview and the Corinthian Situation

First Corinthians is Paul’s most extensive surviving response to the practical and theological problems of a single early Christian community. Paul had founded the church in Corinth, a prosperous Roman colony and port city, and later received reports and a letter revealing deep divisions. The believers quarreled over leaders, tolerated serious sexual misconduct, sued one another, disagreed about marriage and singleness, argued over food associated with idols, displayed status competition in worship, mishandled the Lord’s Supper, competed over spiritual gifts, and disputed bodily resurrection. Paul addresses these issues one by one, but the letter is more than a collection of rulings. Its unifying center is the gospel of the crucified Christ. The cross overturns normal standards of wisdom, power, honor, freedom, and leadership. Christian maturity is measured not by impressive spirituality but by love that builds up the body.

Authorship, Date, and Paul’s Relationship with Corinth

First Corinthians is universally recognized as an authentic Pauline letter. Paul likely wrote it from Ephesus in the mid-50s CE during an extended period of ministry there. The letter itself reveals that it is not Paul’s first communication with Corinth: he refers to an earlier letter about sexual immorality, now lost. He has also received oral reports from “Chloe’s people” and a written set of questions from the Corinthians. These multiple channels explain the alternating structure of report, response, quotation, and correction. Paul’s relationship with the congregation is both authoritative and deeply personal. He calls himself their father through the gospel, but refuses to dominate them through rhetorical prestige. The later correspondence preserved in 2 Corinthians shows that conflict intensified after this letter. Together the Corinthian letters provide an unusually vivid window into the messy pastoral work of forming Christian communities in a competitive urban culture.

The Cross versus Status and Factionalism

The opening chapters confront slogans such as “I belong to Paul,” “I belong to Apollos,” and “I belong to Cephas.” Paul’s answer is not simply organizational unity. He reframes leadership through the cross. In a culture that prized rhetorical skill, patronage, honor, and public reputation, the message of a crucified Messiah appeared foolish and shameful. Paul insists that God’s wisdom is revealed precisely where conventional power does not expect it. Apostles are servants entrusted with God’s mysteries, not celebrities around whom factions should form. The Corinthians’ boasting therefore misunderstands both the gospel and ministry. Paul’s language can be sharp and ironic, especially when he contrasts their self-image as spiritually rich with apostolic weakness and suffering. The theological principle remains enduring: Christian leaders are accountable to Christ, gifts are received rather than owned, and no minister may replace the crucified Lord as the center of the church.

Bodies, Sexual Ethics, Marriage, and Singleness

Chapters 5–7 address the body with an intensity that counters the idea that spirituality makes bodily conduct irrelevant. Paul demands action regarding an incestuous relationship, criticizes believers for taking disputes before pagan courts, and rejects the notion that sexual behavior is merely private appetite. The body belongs to the Lord and is a temple of the Holy Spirit. Yet Paul’s treatment of marriage and celibacy is more nuanced than a simple hierarchy. Responding to Corinthian ascetic tendencies, he affirms mutual marital obligations and recognizes both marriage and singleness as gifts. His preference for remaining unmarried is shaped partly by an expectation of an impending crisis and by practical freedom for ministry, not by contempt for marriage. Some statements reflect first-century patriarchal assumptions and require careful interpretation, but Paul also uses strikingly reciprocal language about spouses’ authority over one another’s bodies. The chapter exemplifies pastoral theology responding to concrete circumstances.

Food Offered to Idols, Freedom, and Love

First Corinthians 8–10 develops one of the New Testament’s richest discussions of conscience and freedom. Some believers know that idols are nothing and therefore feel free to eat meat associated with pagan temples. Paul largely agrees with their theology but challenges the way they use knowledge. Knowledge can puff up; love builds up. If exercising a legitimate freedom destroys a fellow believer’s conscience, the stronger Christian should voluntarily limit that freedom. Paul then uses his own apostolic rights as an example: he has the right to receive support, yet sometimes relinquishes it for the gospel’s sake. At the same time, he draws a firm line at participation in idolatrous worship, using Israel’s wilderness failures as warning. Christian freedom is therefore neither legalistic fear nor autonomous self-expression. It is freedom ordered toward God’s glory, neighbor love, and the good of the community.

Worship, the Lord’s Supper, and Gender

Chapters 11–14 reveal a church whose gatherings expose social inequality and competing claims to spiritual authority. At the Lord’s Supper, wealthier members apparently eat and drink before poorer believers arrive or receive equal provision. Paul declares that such behavior contradicts the meal’s meaning. He passes on the tradition of Jesus’ words over bread and cup and insists that discerning the body includes recognizing the community for whom Christ died. The chapter on head coverings and gender is culturally complex: Paul assumes women pray and prophesy publicly, while arguing about appropriate signs of honor and sexual distinction. Later instructions about women’s silence appear difficult to reconcile with that assumption and have generated textual and interpretive debate. The broader section emphasizes ordered participation. Worship should make visible a body in which gifts serve one another rather than reproduce the status hierarchies of the wider society.

Spiritual Gifts and the Way of Love

The Corinthians prized spectacular manifestations of the Spirit, especially tongues, but Paul reframes spiritual gifts around the metaphor of one body with many members. No gift is self-generated, and no member can say to another, “I have no need of you.” Apostles, prophets, teachers, healings, tongues, and other gifts differ, yet all derive from the same Spirit and exist for the common good. First Corinthians 13, often read at weddings, originally interrupts this debate about charismatic competition. Love is patient and kind, does not boast or insist on its own way, and endures beyond partial gifts of knowledge or prophecy. Chapter 14 then prefers intelligible prophecy in gathered worship when tongues lack interpretation because edification, not private display, is the goal. Paul is neither anti-charismatic nor uncritical of charisma. His test is whether the Spirit’s gifts build up the church in love and confess Jesus as Lord.

Resurrection and the Gospel Tradition

First Corinthians 15 is the New Testament’s fullest sustained argument for bodily resurrection. Paul begins by rehearsing a tradition he had received: Christ died for sins, was buried, was raised on the third day, and appeared to Cephas, the Twelve, a larger group, James, all the apostles, and finally Paul. This creed-like material is earlier than the letter and is therefore an important witness to the early proclamation of Jesus’ death and resurrection. Some Corinthians apparently affirmed Christ’s resurrection while denying a future resurrection of the dead. Paul argues that the two claims stand or fall together. Using Adam and Christ, seed imagery, different kinds of bodies, and the language of transformation, he insists that resurrection is not mere survival of an immaterial soul but God’s victory over death. The chapter ends not in speculation but vocation: because resurrection hope is real, labor in the Lord is not in vain.

Why First Corinthians Matters

First Corinthians matters because it shows theology being forged in argument with ordinary communal problems. Leadership, sex, marriage, lawsuits, food, worship, class, gender, charisma, and death reveal whether the gospel has reshaped life. Paul’s answers are sometimes culturally specific and require historical interpretation, but his governing principles remain powerful: the cross judges boasting, the body belongs to God, freedom is limited by love, wealth must not humiliate the poor, gifts exist for the common good, and resurrection gives present work meaning. The letter also warns against idealizing the early church. Apostolic communities were conflicted and imperfect. Yet Paul does not abandon Corinth. He argues, rebukes, reasons, appeals, and hopes because sanctification is communal work. The church becomes Christ’s body by learning how to live together under the cross.
