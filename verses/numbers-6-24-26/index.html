Bible Study: Numbers 6:24-26

Numbers 6:24–26 contains the priestly or Aaronic blessing: “The LORD bless thee, and keep thee: The LORD make his face shine upon thee, and be gracious unto thee: The LORD lift up his countenance upon thee, and give thee peace.” These lines have closed Jewish and Christian worship for centuries. In Numbers, however, the blessing is not merely a beautiful liturgical ending. It is placed within Israel’s preparation to leave Sinai and journey through the wilderness.

Numbers 1–10 describes the organization of Israel around the tabernacle after the long stay at Sinai. Camps are arranged, Levites assigned duties, ritual instructions given, and the community prepares to move. Yale Bible Study identifies much of this material with priestly traditions concerned with sacred order, worship, and priestly authority.

Working Preacher emphasizes the striking placement of the blessing in this section. Israel is about to travel into uncertainty. The words therefore function as “a blessing for a journey.” Divine favor accompanies a people who cannot predict everything the wilderness will contain.

The blessing is given by God to Moses for Aaron and his sons to speak over Israel. Priests pronounce the words, but Numbers 6:27 makes the source clear: “they shall put my name upon the children of Israel; and I will bless them.” The priests do not manufacture blessing. God blesses through the words.

The three lines grow progressively longer in Hebrew. Each begins with the divine name. Working Preacher notes six verbs describing God’s action: bless, keep, shine, be gracious, lift up the face, and give peace. The movement is entirely God-centered.

“The LORD bless thee” is comprehensive. Biblical blessing includes life, fruitfulness, provision, well-being, and participation in God’s good purposes. It should not be reduced to financial prosperity.

“And keep thee” adds the dimension of protection. The verb appears repeatedly in Psalm 121, where God keeps Israel’s going out and coming in. For wilderness travelers, keeping was not theoretical. The journey involved danger, scarcity, and uncertainty.

“Make his face shine upon thee” uses an embodied metaphor. A shining face suggests favorable attention, warmth, and welcome. The opposite biblical image is God hiding the divine face, an expression of alienation or distress. The blessing imagines God turning toward the people with gracious favor.

“Be gracious unto thee” makes explicit that this favorable presence is gift. Israel does not purchase the shining face through merit. Grace comes from God’s disposition toward the people.

The third line says, “The LORD lift up his countenance upon thee.” The expression again describes God turning attentively toward the person or community. A lifted face is not indifference. The God of the blessing sees.

The final word is shalom, translated “peace.” Working Preacher stresses that shalom is much richer than absence of conflict. It includes safety, well-being, wholeness, health, fruitful relationships, and flourishing.

This prevents a shallow reading in which God’s blessing guarantees that nothing difficult happens. Israel receives these words precisely before a wilderness journey that will include conflict, fear, rebellion, death, and hardship. Divine blessing accompanies the journey; it does not erase the wilderness.

The singular “thee” is also notable. The blessing is spoken over the whole people, yet the grammar can allow each hearer to receive it personally. Biblical blessing is communal without becoming impersonal.

Archaeology gives the text unusual historical interest. Two small silver amulets discovered at Ketef Hinnom near Jerusalem contain forms of the priestly blessing and date to the late seventh or early sixth century BCE. They are among the earliest known witnesses to biblical wording.

Christian use of the blessing should respect its Israelite and Jewish identity. Christians did not invent these words. They inherit them from Israel’s Scriptures, and Jewish communities continue to use them in worship and family blessing.

For Christian discipleship, the passage can shape how believers bless others. Biblical blessing is not vague positive energy. It names concrete hopes under God: provision, protection, grace, attentive presence, and peace.

The words can be especially meaningful during transitions—travel, deployment, marriage, graduation, illness, new work, grief, or the beginning of a difficult season. Like Israel leaving Sinai, people often move forward without knowing everything ahead.

Numbers 6:24–26 therefore gives a theology of presence for the journey. God blesses, keeps, shines, shows grace, turns toward the people, and gives shalom. The wilderness remains real, but the people enter it carrying God’s name and God’s promise.

The blessing’s threefold use of the divine name also gives the poem its liturgical gravity. Every line begins again with God because every dimension of the blessing originates in God.

Bibliography & Further Reading

  • The Holy Bible, King James Version — Numbers 6:22–27. Primary priestly blessing and the concluding promise that God’s name is placed on Israel.
  • Working Preacher, “Commentary on Numbers 6:22–27”. Detailed treatment of the blessing as a wilderness-journey benediction, its six verbs, shining face, and shalom.
  • Yale Bible Study, “Numbers Part 1 — Study Guide”. Academic overview of Numbers 1–10, priestly traditions, authority, and Israel’s wilderness organization.
  • Jacob Milgrom, Numbers, JPS Torah Commentary (Jewish Publication Society, 1990). Major Jewish commentary on the priestly blessing, Hebrew structure, and Ketef Hinnom inscriptions.
  • Baruch A. Levine, Numbers 1–20, Anchor Bible 4A (Doubleday, 1993). Historical and philological commentary on Numbers 6 and priestly liturgy.
