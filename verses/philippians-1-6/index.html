Bible Study: Philippians 1:6

Philippians 1:6 expresses Paul’s confidence in God’s continuing work: “he which hath begun a good work in you will perform it until the day of Jesus Christ.” The verse is often quoted as reassurance that God will finish the process of spiritual growth in an individual believer. That application is meaningful, but Paul’s original wording is plural and embedded in a thanksgiving for the Philippian church’s partnership in the gospel.

Philippians is a prison letter. Yale Bible Study notes that Paul writes while confined, though the exact location of imprisonment remains debated. His circumstances are precarious enough that he contemplates the possibility of death. Yet the letter repeatedly speaks of joy, partnership, humility, perseverance, and hope.

The opening thanksgiving is warm. Paul remembers the Philippians with joy “for your fellowship in the gospel from the first day until now.” The Greek word koinōnia can mean sharing, participation, or partnership. The relationship is not merely emotional; the Philippians have materially supported Paul’s ministry.

Working Preacher notes that the “good work” of verse 6 can be heard in connection with this shared gospel ministry and financial partnership. God’s work is taking shape among a community whose generosity participates in the spread of the gospel.

This communal setting prevents the verse from becoming only a private promise about personal self-improvement. God is at work “among you” as well as within individuals. Churches themselves are unfinished works of grace.

The subject of the sentence is also important: God began the work, and God will bring it toward completion. Paul’s confidence rests in divine faithfulness rather than in the congregation’s flawless performance.

At the same time, Philippians does not teach passivity. Paul will urge the church to live worthily of the gospel, pursue unity, look to the interests of others, work out salvation, rejoice, pray, think on what is good, and practice what they have learned. God’s work empowers active faithfulness.

Philippians 2:13 later makes this relationship explicit: “it is God which worketh in you both to will and to do of his good pleasure.” Grace does not eliminate human action; it makes faithful action possible.

“Until the day of Jesus Christ” gives the verse an eschatological horizon. Paul expects God’s work to reach its goal at Christ’s future appearing. Completion is therefore larger than a moment of maturity in the present life.

This matters because Christians often become discouraged by slow growth. Habits return, churches disappoint, relationships remain difficult, and maturity develops unevenly. Philippians 1:6 allows hope without pretending the process is finished.

The verse should also not be used to excuse harmful behavior with “God is still working on me.” Paul’s confidence in divine grace is paired with serious ethical exhortation. Being unfinished is a reason for humility and repentance, not an exemption from accountability.

For churches, the communal dimension is especially encouraging. Congregations can experience conflict, leadership transitions, financial strain, and cultural change without concluding that God has abandoned them. The question becomes whether they remain open to God’s continuing work.

The verse also reframes ministry success. Paul writes from prison, not from obvious triumph. The “good work” of God can continue in circumstances that look weak by worldly standards. Imprisonment does not cancel vocation.

For discipleship, Philippians 1:6 invites believers to combine patience with participation. Trust God to complete what God began, and keep showing up for the practices through which that work takes shape: prayer, service, generosity, truth, repentance, worship, and love.

The verse can be especially helpful for people haunted by the fear that one failure has permanently ended their usefulness. Paul’s confidence is not grounded in human perfection but in God’s perseverance.

Yet the promise is not vague optimism. The goal is “the day of Jesus Christ.” God’s work is moving toward conformity to Christ and the fulfillment of the gospel, not merely toward whatever personal future a believer happens to prefer.

Philippians 1:6 therefore offers durable hope. Christians are works in progress, and so are Christian communities. The work begins with God, continues through shared faithfulness, and moves toward completion in Christ. Paul’s confidence is ultimately confidence in the God who does not abandon what grace has begun.

The plural address also means believers can become instruments in the good work God is completing in one another. Encouragement, correction, generosity, and prayer all belong to that communal formation.

The verse also encourages Christians to distinguish incompleteness from abandonment. A work can still be unfinished while genuinely belonging to God. Paul’s confidence gives room for patient formation: God’s grace can continue through correction, suffering, generosity, renewed love, and the ordinary endurance of a community that keeps participating in the gospel.

Bibliography & Further Reading

  • The Holy Bible, King James Version — Philippians 1:3–11. Primary thanksgiving context of partnership, love, discernment, and the day of Christ.
  • Working Preacher, “Commentary on Philippians 1:3–11”. Connects the ‘good work’ with the Philippians’ koinonia and concrete support of the gospel.
  • Yale Bible Study, “Paul’s Epistle to the Philippians”. Academic introduction to Philippians as a prison letter marked by friendship, suffering, joy, and communal exhortation.
  • Gordon D. Fee, Paul’s Letter to the Philippians, NICNT (Eerdmans, 1995). Major exegetical and theological commentary on Philippians.
  • Moisés Silva, Philippians, 2nd ed., Baker Exegetical Commentary on the New Testament (Baker Academic, 2005). Detailed commentary on Paul’s language, theology, ethics, and pastoral argument.
