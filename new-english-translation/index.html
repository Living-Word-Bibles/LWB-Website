The Holy Bible: New English Translation (NET)

An annotated history of the internet-first English Bible known for its unusually transparent translation process and more than 60,000 translators’ notes.

Read the NET Bible

About the NET Bible. The NET Bible is a modern English translation produced directly from Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek sources. Its defining feature is not simply its wording but its documentation: the full-notes edition places more than 60,000 translation, study, and text-critical notes beside the biblical text so readers can see why difficult passages were rendered as they were. Conceived for the internet age, the project posted working drafts online, invited review, and treated digital distribution as part of the translation’s mission from the beginning.

Overview & Distinctives

The New English Translation, normally identified simply as the NET Bible, occupies an unusual place among contemporary English Bibles. Many translations are best known for a particular reading level, church tradition, translation philosophy, or literary style. The NET is certainly a translation in its own right, but its most recognizable contribution is methodological transparency. Instead of asking readers to accept thousands of difficult translation decisions without explanation, the project attempted to expose those decisions to view.

That transparency is embodied in the notes. The first finished edition contained 60,932 of them. Some explain why a Hebrew or Greek construction was translated in a particular way. Others identify a legitimate alternate rendering, discuss a manuscript variant, supply historical background, or clarify an idiom that cannot be carried into English word for word. The result is a Bible that can be read devotionally like any other translation while also functioning as a bridge into the questions normally encountered in commentaries, critical editions, and original-language study.

The NET was also shaped by the technological moment in which it was born. Its creators did not merely convert a print Bible into a website after publication. They conceived the project around electronic access, public review, and rapid distribution. In that sense the NET belongs to the history of Bible technology as much as to the history of English translation: just as the codex and printing press changed how Scripture circulated, the internet allowed a translation-in-progress to be read and critiqued by people far outside the editorial room.

Origins of the Project: A Bible for the Internet Age

The NET project emerged in the mid-1990s, when the public internet was still young and most Bible publishing assumed a print-first model. W. Hall Harris III, who became project director and managing editor, later recalled that the working title was initially The Internet Study Bible. A proposal was accepted in January 1996, after early proof-of-concept work on the Johannine letters demonstrated how a new translation could be paired with a full apparatus of explanatory notes.

The idea was ambitious. The translators would work from the biblical languages; editors would review the drafts; and the project would publish material online during development rather than concealing every stage until a finished volume appeared. Drafts were therefore exposed to criticism from scholars, pastors, students, and ordinary readers. Public comments did not turn the NET into a translation by popular vote. The editorial team retained responsibility for the text. But the process created an additional layer of testing: readers could identify unclear English, question a rendering, or draw attention to an interpretive issue before the wording was permanently fixed in a print run.

This approach reflected a larger ministry concern. Bible.org wanted electronic distribution to reduce barriers to Scripture and study resources. The project therefore combined scholarship with a practical question: how could a serious modern translation take advantage of the internet’s capacity to distribute text almost instantly around the world? That question explains both the NET’s name and much of its later identity.

From Working Drafts to the 2005 First Edition

The translation developed in stages. By 1998 the New Testament had reached complete draft form. By 2001 a draft of the whole Bible existed, and the project released what it called a First Beta Edition. Borrowing the language of software development was deliberate: “beta” communicated that the text was usable but still open to further testing, correction, and refinement. A second beta followed before the final First Edition.

The completed First Edition appeared in 2005 after roughly a decade of development. Bible.org’s announcement described a team of more than twenty-five scholars working from Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek texts and emphasized the 60,932 notes attached to the translation. This was not simply a marketing accessory. The notes represented a record of the project’s reasoning and were integral to its stated purpose.

The First Edition also demonstrated that an internet-first translation could move successfully into conventional print. Full-notes editions were substantial volumes because the apparatus occupied so much space, while Reader’s, Pew, and Compact editions offered condensed note sets for readers who wanted a more portable Bible.

Translators, Editors & Scholarly Structure

The NET was produced collaboratively rather than by a single translator. Specialists were assigned biblical books or groups of books, while editors worked across sections to maintain consistency. The project’s published personnel lists identify scholars such as W. Hall Harris III, Michael H. Burer, Robert B. Chisholm, Daniel B. Wallace, Buist M. Fanning III, Gordon H. Johnston, Richard A. Taylor, and others in translation and editorial roles. The Second Edition added further editorial and consulting work.

This structure matters because Bible translation requires several forms of expertise at once. A scholar may know Hebrew poetry deeply but not specialize in Pauline Greek; another may be expert in textual criticism but not English literary style. Collaborative translation allows specialists to contribute where their training is strongest, while editorial review seeks consistency across sixty-six books written in different genres and historical settings.

The NET’s public documentation also makes the team easier to evaluate than an anonymous product. Readers can identify who served on the project, examine the notes, and compare the translation with the underlying textual evidence.

Translation Philosophy: Accuracy, Readability & Clarity

No responsible Bible translation is produced by mechanically substituting one English word for every Hebrew or Greek word. Languages organize grammar, idiom, emphasis, metaphor, and word order differently. A phrase that is transparent in ancient Greek may be awkward or misleading if reproduced too literally in English. On the other hand, a translation that paraphrases too freely can obscure details present in the source. The NET works within this unavoidable tension.

Its stated goals have often been summarized in terms such as accuracy, readability, and elegance. In practice, some passages remain relatively close to the form of the original language, while others render an idiom or implicit relationship more explicitly for modern readers. What distinguishes the NET is that the note frequently preserves the road not taken. A reader may see the more literal wording in a translator’s note even when the main text adopts a smoother English expression.

This makes the translation particularly useful for comparing formal and functional approaches. Instead of pretending that only one English rendering is possible, the NET often explains why two or more renderings are defensible and why the committee selected one for the main text. Such candor is valuable because many genuine translation questions are matters of judgment rather than simple right-versus-wrong choices.

The translation also pays attention to discourse and context. Words derive meaning from sentences, genre, and historical usage, not from dictionary glosses alone.

Textual Basis & Textual Criticism

Before translators can decide how to render a sentence, they must determine the Hebrew, Aramaic, or Greek wording being translated. The biblical books survive through manuscript traditions containing a large number of minor variants. Most differences are easy to recognize and do not affect the sense of a passage, but a smaller number require careful evaluation. The NET makes this stage of translation unusually visible.

For the Old Testament, the translation works primarily with the Masoretic Hebrew tradition while considering other ancient witnesses where they illuminate a difficult reading. These may include the Dead Sea Scrolls, the Greek Septuagint, the Samaritan Pentateuch for the Torah, and ancient versions. For the New Testament, the translators engage the modern critical Greek text and the broader manuscript evidence reflected in critical editions. The relevant notes may identify major manuscripts, explain why one reading appears earlier or stronger, and state when the translation follows a reading that differs from another familiar English Bible.

This is especially important in passages where readers notice brackets, missing words, or wording differences between translations. The NET does not treat such questions as secrets. Its text-critical notes—marked tc—introduce readers to the evidence behind the choice. They can be technical, but they demonstrate that textual criticism is not the invention of a new Bible; it is the disciplined comparison of surviving witnesses in order to identify the earliest attainable wording.

Why this matters: a translation can be transparent about textual uncertainty without implying uncertainty about the Christian faith as a whole. The immense majority of the biblical text is not in serious dispute. Where meaningful variants do occur, responsible editions identify them so readers can examine the evidence rather than being shielded from it.

The Signature Feature: More Than 60,000 Notes

The NET Bible’s notes are not all the same. Bible.org’s own abbreviation guide distinguishes several categories, and understanding them makes the edition much easier to use.

tn — Translator’s Note. Explains the rationale for a rendering, supplies a more literal expression, identifies an alternate translation, or discusses grammatical and lexical issues.
sn — Study Note. Supplies contextual, historical, cultural, literary, theological, or cross-reference information that helps explain the passage.
tc — Text-Critical Note. Discusses significant variant readings in Hebrew or Greek manuscripts and explains why a particular reading was adopted or mentioned.
map — Map Note. In editions using the map system, connects geographical references with map locations.

The most distinctive of these for translation study are the tn and tc notes. A translator’s note can reveal that the English main text is an idiomatic rendering while preserving the source-language form below. A text-critical note can explain why a phrase familiar from the King James Version is absent from many modern translations, or why two ancient witnesses preserve different forms of a verse.

This gives pastors, students, and serious lay readers access to information that would otherwise require several reference works. The notes do not replace Hebrew or Greek study, and they do not eliminate the need for commentaries. They do, however, help readers recognize where important decisions have been made. In that sense the NET invites readers to look over the translators’ shoulders.

An Internet-First Bible

Perhaps the NET’s most historically distinctive feature is that electronic publication was part of its design rather than an afterthought. Working drafts were placed online during development. Readers could respond while revision was still possible. By 2001 the complete Bible was available in beta form electronically, several years before the finished First Edition.

That sequence reversed the traditional publishing order. Earlier Bible translations normally moved from committee to typesetting to print and only later, in the digital era, into searchable software. The NET moved from collaborative digital development into print. Its notes were especially suited to this environment because electronic interfaces could reveal, hide, search, and link large amounts of supplementary information without forcing every reader to carry an enormous physical volume.

The digital approach also widened access for missionaries, pastors, students, and readers without large theological libraries. Searchable notes, original-language tools, cross-references, and downloadable editions could travel wherever an internet connection or stored file could go. Bible.org later developed integrated study environments that combined the NET with articles, language tools, and other resources.

The NET’s workflow anticipated the increasingly searchable, linked, and mobile character of modern Scripture study.

The 2019 Second Edition

Translations live in a changing linguistic and scholarly environment. English usage shifts; editors discover places where wording can be clearer; new studies sharpen understanding of grammar and vocabulary; and a large notes apparatus inevitably benefits from correction and refinement. The NET Bible Second Edition, copyrighted 2019, therefore revised both biblical text and notes while preserving the project’s fundamental identity.

The Second Edition should not be understood as a new translation replacing the NET’s original philosophy. It is a revision within the same project. The translation remains recognizable, the notes remain central, and the goal of exposing major interpretive and textual decisions continues. Current NET materials list Second Edition translators, editors, and consultants in addition to the original project personnel, reflecting the continuing stewardship of the text.

The revision also underscores a healthy principle in modern Bible publishing: careful translations can be stable without being frozen. The task is not continual novelty but responsible correction when editors judge that accuracy, clarity, or consistency can be improved.

What the NET Is Like to Read & Study

As continuous English prose, the NET generally aims for direct, contemporary language rather than the elevated archaism associated with the King James tradition. It can therefore work well for reading, teaching, and study, particularly when a reader wants a modern translation that does not hide technical questions.

Its greatest strength is also a potential distraction. A reader using the full-notes edition may find the apparatus so rich that attention repeatedly moves from Scripture to the bottom of the page. For close study that is precisely the point; for prayerful continuous reading, a reader may prefer a condensed-notes or text-focused presentation.

The NET is especially effective in comparison with other translations. When the ESV, NIV, KJV, NRSV, NASB, or another Bible differs substantially, the NET notes often explain the underlying grammatical or textual question. This means the NET can function as a companion translation even for Christians whose primary Bible is another version.

Readers should also distinguish the authority of Scripture from the authority of the notes. The biblical translation is the committee’s rendering of the source text; the notes are scholarly explanations and interpretations. They can be exceptionally useful without being infallible. The very transparency of the NET encourages readers to examine evidence, compare responsible scholarship, and return to the biblical text itself.

Influence, Reception & Lasting Contribution

The NET has earned attention beyond the circle of people who use it as their primary Bible. Translators and editors associated with other English versions have publicly noted the usefulness of its extensive notes.

Its broader contribution is a model of accountability. Bible translation inevitably involves judgments about syntax, vocabulary, manuscripts, idioms, literary style, and interpretation. Most printed Bibles reveal only a fraction of that process. The NET demonstrated that a translation could document an extraordinary number of decisions and still remain usable by ordinary readers.

It also belongs to the story of Christianity’s adaptation to new media. The manuscript codex made collections of biblical books easier to bind together; movable type multiplied Bibles in the early modern period; digital networks made global access and rapid searching possible. The NET deliberately entered that newest stage. Its history therefore illustrates an old Christian impulse expressed through a new technology: make Scripture available, make serious study more accessible, and explain the work carefully enough that readers can test what translators have done.

Copyright, Distribution & Permissions

The NET Bible is copyrighted and is not public domain, but its permissions have long reflected the project’s emphasis on access. Current official guidance permits substantial noncommercial use of the biblical text under stated conditions and provides separate procedures for commercial publication. The official copyright page also specifies preferred naming and attribution conventions and notes that NET Bible® is a registered trademark.

Because permissions can change and different rules may apply to text, notes, apps, audio, commercial products, and large-scale reproduction, publishers should consult the current NET Bible copyright page rather than relying on an old summary. For a website such as Living Word Bibles, the safest practice is to retain the required NET identification and copyright acknowledgment wherever NET Scripture is reproduced and to obtain additional permission whenever the intended use falls outside the published grant.
