2 Kings

Read 2 Kings Now

Overview and Place in Scripture

2 Kings continues without a break from 1 Kings in the Hebrew narrative tradition, carrying the story from the final ministry of Elijah to the destruction of Jerusalem and the exile of Judah. Its pages encompass the fall of the northern kingdom of Israel to Assyria in 722/721 BCE and the later fall of Jerusalem to Babylon in 586 BCE. The book therefore narrates catastrophe on a national scale. Yet its purpose is not simply to report military defeat. Kings interprets the collapse of both kingdoms through covenant theology, prophetic warning, idolatry, injustice, and the long consequences of failed leadership. Alongside that severe judgment stand stories of prophetic power, reform, mercy, and preservation. The closing scene, in which the exiled Davidic king Jehoiachin is released from prison and given a place at the Babylonian table, leaves a small but deliberate opening toward hope.

Literary Shape and the Deuteronomistic History

The first chapters complete the Elijah narrative and transfer prophetic authority to Elisha. A large central section follows Elisha’s ministry amid war, famine, dynastic violence, and ordinary human need. The book then moves through the reigns of Israel and Judah, using recurring formulas to evaluate kings. Northern rulers remain associated with Jeroboam’s cult, while southern kings are assessed in part by their relationship to the Jerusalem temple and “high places.” Modern scholarship commonly situates the book in the Deuteronomistic History, a larger theological work shaped by the language and ideals of Deuteronomy. The final edition is often connected with the exilic or early postexilic period, when writers sought to explain how the land, monarchy, and temple could be lost despite God’s promises. The answer is not that God failed, but that covenant warning had been repeatedly ignored.

Elisha and the Prophetic Imagination

Elisha’s stories range from international politics to the needs of unnamed households. He purifies water, provides oil for a widow, promises a child to the Shunammite woman, raises that child, feeds a community, and heals the Aramean commander Naaman. These episodes portray divine power as active beyond palace and battlefield. Naaman’s story is especially important because an outsider receives healing and confesses Israel’s God, while Elisha refuses payment. The narrative then exposes greed through Gehazi’s deception. Other stories involve kings, military intelligence, sieges, and regime change. The diversity is part of the point: prophetic authority cannot be confined to a religious corner. God’s word addresses household debt, sickness, food, enemy armies, kings, and foreign nations. Elisha’s ministry also resists the idea that miraculous power belongs to the prophet as personal possession; the prophet remains servant and witness.

The Fall of the Northern Kingdom

The political world of 2 Kings is dominated increasingly by Assyria, the major imperial power of the eighth century BCE. Israel’s rulers maneuver through tribute, alliances, rebellion, and internal instability. In 2 Kings 17, Samaria falls after a prolonged siege, and the northern kingdom ceases to exist as an independent state. The chapter offers a theological retrospective: Israel had worshiped other gods, rejected prophetic warnings, and imitated practices the covenant condemned. Modern historians also emphasize the geopolitical realities of Assyrian expansion, military superiority, taxation, deportation, and local resistance. The biblical explanation and historical reconstruction answer different kinds of questions. Kings is interested in why this disaster matters before God. Assyrian royal inscriptions and archaeological evidence confirm the empire’s massive role in reshaping the Levant, including deportations and the destruction of rebellious territories.

Hezekiah, Assyria, and Jerusalem

Hezekiah is portrayed as one of Judah’s strongest reforming kings. He removes cultic objects, centralizes worship, and trusts YHWH during the Assyrian crisis. The invasion of Sennacherib in 701 BCE is one of the best-attested events in biblical history, appearing in Kings, Isaiah, Assyrian inscriptions, and archaeology. Sennacherib’s prism boasts of shutting Hezekiah in Jerusalem “like a bird in a cage” while listing conquered Judean towns and tribute. The biblical narrative emphasizes Jerusalem’s deliverance and the prophetic role of Isaiah. Lachish, however, was captured, and reliefs from Sennacherib’s palace vividly depict its siege. Read together, these sources show both the historical severity of the campaign and the theological significance Judah attached to Jerusalem’s survival. Hezekiah’s later illness and diplomatic encounter with Babylon also foreshadow a future danger.

Josiah, Reform, and the Book of the Law

The reign of Josiah forms the last major moment of hope before Jerusalem’s fall. During temple repairs, a “book of the law” is discovered. The king responds with grief, covenant renewal, and a sweeping program of reform. Many scholars connect this text in some way with Deuteronomy or an early form of it, though the precise history remains debated. Josiah centralizes worship in Jerusalem and removes rival shrines and practices. Yet the narrative delivers a difficult verdict: reform does not erase the accumulated consequences associated with Manasseh and generations of disobedience. Josiah’s death at Megiddo is abrupt, and Judah soon becomes trapped between Egypt and Babylon. The story raises a theological tension that runs throughout Scripture: repentance matters deeply, yet historical consequences can outlast the generation that first produced them.

Jerusalem’s Destruction and Exile

The last chapters describe the rapid collapse of Judah under Babylonian pressure. Kings are installed and removed, tribute is demanded, rebellion brings renewed invasion, and Jerusalem is finally breached. The temple, palace, and city are burned; leaders are executed; treasures are carried away; and much of the population is deported. The book refuses to romanticize the event. The destruction of the temple is especially devastating because the temple had represented divine presence and Davidic continuity. Yet the theology of Kings had long insisted that sacred institutions could not shield a persistently unfaithful people from judgment. The exile becomes not evidence that Babylon’s gods defeated YHWH, but the fulfillment of prophetic warning. This interpretation would become foundational for later Jewish reflection on repentance, restoration, and life with God outside the land.

History, Sources, and Memory

2 Kings frequently cites royal annals, and many events intersect with external evidence. Assyrian inscriptions mention rulers such as Jehu, Menahem, Pekah, Hoshea, and Hezekiah; Babylonian chronicles help establish the chronology of Jerusalem’s final decades. Archaeological destruction layers at sites such as Lachish illuminate the violence of imperial conquest. At the same time, historians debate particular dates, synchronisms, and the extent to which the book’s speeches or prophetic episodes preserve earlier traditions. The text is best read neither as a modern transcript nor as invented theology detached from history. It is an ancient interpretation of a remembered political past, drawing on court materials, prophetic traditions, and communal memory. Its editorial shaping invites readers to see historical events through a covenantal moral lens.

Why 2 Kings Matters

2 Kings asks how faith survives when institutions collapse. Israel loses Samaria; Judah loses Jerusalem, temple, land, monarchy, and political independence. The book’s answer is severe but not nihilistic. Prophets had spoken before the disasters, so exile does not mean God has become absent or powerless. The word of God outlasts the structures that failed to heed it. The stories of Naaman, the Shunammite woman, Hezekiah, and Josiah also show that faithfulness, mercy, and reform remain meaningful even within a history moving toward judgment. For Christian readers, the book contributes to later biblical expectations of restoration, renewed kingship, and a faith not dependent on political control. Its final glimpse of Jehoiachin alive in Babylon suggests that judgment is not necessarily the final word. Hope survives in exile.

Annotated Bibliography (Chicago Style)

  • Cogan, Mordechai, and Hayim Tadmor. II Kings: A New Translation with Introduction and Commentary. Anchor Bible 11. Garden City, NY: Doubleday, 1988. A foundational commentary integrating Hebrew exegesis with Assyrian and Babylonian evidence for the political world of 2 Kings.
  • Sweeney, Marvin A. I & II Kings: A Commentary. Old Testament Library. Louisville: Westminster John Knox Press, 2007. Provides literary, theological, and historical analysis of the fall of Israel and Judah within the Deuteronomistic History.
  • Nelson, Richard D. First and Second Kings. Interpretation. Louisville: John Knox Press, 1987. A theological reading of royal failure, prophetic witness, reform, exile, and the enduring significance of covenant judgment.
  • Provan, Iain W. 1 and 2 Kings. New International Biblical Commentary. Peabody, MA: Hendrickson, 1995. Useful for tracing narrative structure, royal evaluations, prophetic material, and the movement toward exile.
  • Na’aman, Nadav. Ancient Israel and Its Neighbors: Interaction and Counteraction. Winona Lake, IN: Eisenbrauns, 2005. Historical studies that illuminate Assyrian, Babylonian, and Levantine contexts relevant to the events narrated in Kings.
  • Collins, John J. Introduction to the Hebrew Bible. 3rd ed. Minneapolis: Fortress Press, 2018. Provides an academic overview of Kings, Deuteronomistic theology, imperial history, and the problem of exile.
  • British Museum. “Sennacherib and the Siege of Lachish.” britishmuseum.org. Accessed August 28, 2026. Museum resources on Assyrian reliefs and imperial material culture that illuminate the historical setting of Hezekiah’s reign.
  • Hayes, Christine. Introduction to the Bible. New Haven: Yale University Press, 2012. Broad scholarly context for the fall of the kingdoms, prophetic interpretation, and the exilic theological framework reflected in 2 Kings.
