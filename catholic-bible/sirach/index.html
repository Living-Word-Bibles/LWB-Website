The Holy Bible: Douay-Rheims — Sirach (Ecclesiasticus)

Summary, History, Wisdom & Annotated Bibliography

Read Sirach 1:1

Overview

Sirach, also called the Wisdom of Ben Sira and traditionally Ecclesiasticus in the Douay-Rheims Bible, is one of the great wisdom books of the Old Testament. Unlike many ancient biblical works, it identifies its human author: Jesus, or Joshua, son of Sirach, commonly called Ben Sira. The book gathers instruction about worship, friendship, family, speech, education, wealth, poverty, work, leadership, medicine, death, free choice, and the fear of the Lord. The United States Conference of Catholic Bishops describes it as a wide-ranging compendium of Jewish wisdom written in Hebrew in the early second century B.C., probably completed by about 175 B.C. [1]

Ben Sira and His Historical Setting

Ben Sira lived in Jerusalem during the Hellenistic age, when Jewish society existed under strong Greek cultural influence. His wisdom is firmly rooted in Israel’s Scriptures and religious tradition, yet he writes in a world where Greek education, social ideals, politics, and philosophy were increasingly influential. Patrick W. Skehan and Alexander A. Di Lella’s Anchor Yale commentary emphasizes this setting: Ben Sira knew both Judaic and Hellenistic literary traditions but defended the distinctive religious values inherited by Israel. [5]

The author appears not merely as a collector of sayings but as a teacher. He repeatedly addresses students, urges disciplined study, and portrays wisdom as something to be pursued through prayer, reflection, experience, and instruction. Near the end of the book he even invites the unlearned into his “house of instruction.” Sirach therefore provides an unusually personal glimpse of Jewish scribal education before the Maccabean period.

Wisdom and the Fear of the Lord

Sirach begins with a sweeping theological claim: all wisdom comes from God. The opening chapter repeatedly joins wisdom to the “fear of the Lord,” meaning reverent devotion expressed through love, worship, obedience, humility, and keeping God’s commandments. The USCCB notes that this connection is programmatic for the entire book. [4] Wisdom is therefore not simply cleverness or intellectual achievement. It is a way of living rightly before God.

This idea reaches one of its fullest expressions in chapter 24, where Wisdom speaks in poetic, almost personal terms. Ben Sira ultimately identifies Wisdom with the law of Moses: divine wisdom has taken concrete form in the covenantal instruction given to Israel. The result is a distinctive synthesis of the older wisdom tradition with devotion to Torah. To seek wisdom is not to leave Israel’s faith behind; it is to enter more deeply into it. [1]

Practical Wisdom for Everyday Life

Much of Sirach concerns ordinary choices. Ben Sira speaks about controlling anger, guarding one’s speech, choosing friends carefully, avoiding gossip, honoring parents, dealing fairly with workers, giving alms, using wealth responsibly, showing mercy, and resisting pride. He warns that careless words can destroy relationships and that apparent prosperity is temporary. Friendship receives especially careful treatment: genuine friends are rare treasures, while false friends disappear when adversity comes.

The book also joins worship to ethics. Sacrifice without justice is empty, while care for the poor belongs to genuine service of God. Wealth is not condemned simply for existing, but greed, oppression, and misplaced confidence in riches are repeatedly attacked. Ben Sira’s teaching is intensely practical because wisdom must be demonstrated in conduct—in the home, marketplace, assembly, workplace, table fellowship, and sanctuary.

Human Freedom and Responsibility

One of Sirach’s most important theological passages appears in chapter 15. Ben Sira rejects the claim that God causes a person to sin. Human beings are presented as capable of genuine choice, with life and death set before them. The USCCB notes that the passage explicitly connects freedom of the will with moral responsibility: God sees every human deed but does not command wickedness. [1] This emphasis allows Sirach to hold together divine sovereignty and human accountability without making God the author of evil.

Creation, Worship, and the Praise of the Ancestors

Sirach’s vision expands beyond individual conduct. Chapters 42–43 celebrate the ordered beauty of creation—the sun, moon, stars, weather, sea, snow, and other works of God. Creation itself becomes evidence of divine wisdom. Then chapters 44–50 present the famous “Praise of the Ancestors,” recalling figures from Israel’s history including Enoch, Noah, Abraham, Moses, Aaron, Joshua, Samuel, David, Solomon, Elijah, Hezekiah, Josiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, and others. The survey culminates in praise of the high priest Simon, linking the inherited history of Israel with the worshipping community Ben Sira himself knew. [1]

From Hebrew to Greek

Sirach possesses one of the clearest ancient translation histories of any biblical book. Ben Sira wrote in Hebrew. His grandson later went to Egypt and translated the work into Greek, adding a prologue explaining both the project and the difficulty of carrying Hebrew expression into another language. The prologue places the grandson’s arrival in Egypt in the “thirty-eighth year” of King Euergetes, commonly identified as about 132 B.C.; the USCCB dates the Greek translation itself after 117 B.C. His preface is therefore an unusually valuable ancient witness to the transmission and translation of Jewish sacred literature. [1] John G. Snaith’s Cambridge treatment discusses the translator’s preface and the movement of the work from Hebrew into Greek. [7]

The Recovery of the Hebrew Text

For many centuries Christians chiefly knew Sirach through Greek and Latin versions, while the Hebrew original was largely lost. That changed dramatically beginning in 1896, when Hebrew manuscripts were identified among the Cairo Genizah discoveries. Further fragments followed. Additional ancient Hebrew witnesses were later recovered near the Dead Sea, including fragments at Qumran and a substantial scroll from Masada. Together the surviving Hebrew material now preserves more than two-thirds of the book and substantially confirms the antiquity of its Hebrew textual tradition. [6] [7]

The Israel Antiquities Authority’s Leon Levy Dead Sea Scrolls Digital Library catalogs the Masada manuscript as Mas 1h – Mas Sir, a Hebrew wisdom manuscript written on parchment. Its existence is especially important because it demonstrates that Hebrew Sirach circulated in antiquity rather than being merely a medieval reconstruction. [6]

Canon and Catholic Reception

Sirach is part of the Catholic Old Testament deuterocanon. The Council of Trent’s decree on canonical Scripture explicitly lists Ecclesiasticus among the books received as sacred and canonical. [3] It is also received in Eastern Orthodox and other ancient Christian traditions. The book was not included in the later Jewish biblical canon and is generally placed among the Apocrypha rather than the Old Testament canon in Protestant traditions.

Lasting Importance

Sirach preserves the voice of a Jewish sage trying to teach faithful living in a changing world. Its subject is not one isolated doctrine but the formation of an entire life. Wisdom begins with God, takes shape in obedience, and appears in speech, generosity, friendship, work, family responsibility, worship, self-control, and remembrance of Israel’s sacred history. The book’s recovery in Hebrew has also made it exceptionally valuable for understanding the development of Hebrew language and Jewish thought in the Second Temple period. For Catholic readers, Sirach remains both ancient wisdom literature and Scripture: a disciplined school of character in which reverence for God is meant to shape every ordinary decision. Its counsel can be practical, severe, poetic, and devotional in turn, but throughout the book Ben Sira insists that wisdom is proved in conduct. Theology is never separated from daily life; knowledge of God is repeatedly measured by justice, humility, disciplined speech, mercy, and faithfulness.
