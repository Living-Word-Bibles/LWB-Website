Bible Study: Acts 4:12

Acts 4:12 contains one of the New Testament’s strongest claims about Jesus: “Neither is there salvation in any other: for there is none other name under heaven given among men, whereby we must be saved.” Peter speaks these words not in a quiet classroom but under interrogation. He and John have been arrested after the healing of a man who had been unable to walk and after Peter’s public preaching about Jesus’ resurrection.

The authorities ask, “By what power, or by what name, have ye done this?” The question is about authority. Peter, “filled with the Holy Ghost,” answers that the healed man stands well “by the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth, whom ye crucified, whom God raised from the dead.” Verse 12 therefore grows directly out of the healing and resurrection proclamation. The “name” of Jesus represents His authority, identity, and saving power.

The word translated “saved” is closely related to the word used in the previous verse for the man being made whole. Acts intentionally links physical restoration with the larger salvation proclaimed in Jesus. The healing is a sign. It points beyond itself toward God’s work of restoration through the crucified and risen Christ.

Peter also quotes Psalm 118: “the stone which was set at nought of you builders, which is become the head of the corner.” Jesus’ rejection does not disprove His significance; resurrection reverses the human verdict. The authorities rejected Him, but God vindicated Him. Salvation is therefore tied to God’s action in the very One human power discarded.

The exclusive wording of verse 12 requires careful interpretation. Christian faith confesses Jesus as the decisive Savior, not merely one religious option among many. Acts repeatedly proclaims forgiveness and salvation through Him. At the same time, Christians have sometimes used this conviction arrogantly or coercively toward people of other religions. The manner of Peter’s witness matters. His boldness comes from the Spirit and from the healing of a vulnerable man, not from political control. Christian conviction should therefore be expressed with humility, love, and respect rather than contempt.

The verse also must be read within Luke’s larger story of expanding mission. The same Acts that makes this strong claim about Jesus also shows the gospel crossing boundaries the earliest disciples initially found difficult—Samaritans, an Ethiopian official, a Roman centurion, and Gentile communities across the Mediterranean. Confidence in Jesus does not narrow the circle of people Christians are willing to approach. It drives the mission outward.

Acts 4 emphasizes that Peter and John are ordinary men without elite rabbinic training. The council is astonished by their boldness and recognizes that they have been with Jesus. Their authority is not academic prestige or social rank. The Spirit equips them to testify to what God has done. This does not make learning unimportant; it means the gospel is not the private possession of an intellectual class.

The passage also presents salvation as something God “given among men.” It is gift before achievement. The risen Christ is offered as the One through whom forgiveness, healing, resurrection hope, and restored relationship with God come. Peter is not presenting a ladder by which people climb toward God. He is announcing what God has done in Jesus.

For discipleship, Acts 4:12 calls Christians to clarity about the center of their faith. Churches can become preoccupied with personalities, traditions, politics, institutions, or cultural identity. Peter redirects salvation to the name of Jesus Christ. Christian witness is strongest when it is both confident and Christlike: clear about Jesus, dependent on the Spirit, committed to mercy, and free from coercive pride. The healed man standing beside Peter is a fitting image of the claim. Salvation in Jesus is not merely a slogan; Acts presents it as God’s restoring power entering real human lives.

The immediate story also connects salvation with courage under pressure. Peter does not make the claim from a position of social power. He is a recently arrested disciple speaking before authorities capable of punishing him. His confidence comes from the Spirit and from the resurrection of Jesus, not from institutional dominance. That setting should influence how Christians use the verse today. Confessing the uniqueness of Christ is not permission for arrogance. The apostles’ boldness is costly, vulnerable, and centered on a man who was crucified. Christian witness is therefore most faithful when clarity about Jesus is joined to humility, mercy, and willingness to suffer rather than compel. The name proclaimed as the source of salvation is the name of the rejected and risen Christ.
