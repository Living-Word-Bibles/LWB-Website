Bible Study: Luke 1:37

Luke 1:37 concludes Gabriel’s reassurance to Mary with a sweeping statement: “For with God nothing shall be impossible.” The verse has become a favorite declaration of confidence, but its first meaning is tied to two extraordinary pregnancies—Elizabeth’s conception in old age and Mary’s promised conception of Jesus through the Holy Spirit.

Luke begins his Gospel by interweaving the birth stories of John the Baptist and Jesus. Zechariah and Elizabeth are elderly and childless; Mary is a young woman betrothed to Joseph. The angel Gabriel appears in both stories, and Luke deliberately invites comparison between the two announcements.

When Gabriel tells Mary she will conceive and bear a son, she asks, “How shall this be, seeing I know not a man?” Her question is not dismissed. Gabriel answers by speaking of the Holy Spirit, the power of the Most High, and the identity of the child as holy and Son of God.

Then Gabriel gives Mary a confirming sign: her relative Elizabeth, previously called barren, is already six months pregnant. Verse 37 follows immediately. “Nothing shall be impossible with God” is therefore not abstract optimism. It interprets concrete acts of divine initiative in the birth narratives.

The Greek wording can also be translated along the lines of “no word from God will be impossible” or “no word from God will fail.” That nuance connects possibility with the reliability of what God has spoken. The emphasis is not that human beings can imagine anything and require God to make it happen, but that God is able to fulfill God’s own promise.

Working Preacher describes Luke 1:37 as an interpretive key for the opening chapters. Elizabeth’s pregnancy, Mary’s calling, Jesus’ birth, and the unfolding story all witness to divine action exceeding ordinary expectation.

This distinction protects the verse from becoming a slogan for wish fulfillment. “Nothing is impossible with God” does not mean every personal dream, business plan, athletic goal, romantic desire, or financial ambition has divine guarantee. God’s unlimited power is not placed at the service of every human preference.

The verse also does not mean Christians should ignore ordinary limits or wise judgment. Faith does not require refusing medical care, denying financial reality, ignoring safety, or attempting reckless actions in order to prove trust.

Mary’s response in verse 38 shows the intended posture: “Behold the handmaid of the Lord; be it unto me according to thy word.” Faith is not control over divine power. It is trustful availability to God’s word.

Working Preacher also emphasizes that Mary’s response develops through fear, questioning, reassurance, and finally consent. Her faith is not portrayed as the absence of thought. She is troubled, she asks how, she receives further explanation, and then she responds.

This is significant for Christian discipleship because questions do not automatically equal unbelief. Mary can be a model of faithful inquiry. The Gospel allows her to think and speak before she says yes.

Luke also gives Mary social and theological significance. She is a young woman from Nazareth, not a priest in Jerusalem, yet she receives the announcement that changes the Gospel’s story. Luke repeatedly highlights divine action among people society may overlook.

The verse also anticipates Luke’s wider theme of reversal. The Magnificat soon celebrates the proud being scattered, the mighty brought down, the lowly lifted, the hungry filled, and the rich sent away empty. God’s “impossible” work often overturns established assumptions about power.

Christian tradition has understandably connected Luke 1:37 with the virgin conception and the incarnation. The God who creates life beyond ordinary expectation enters human history through a child whose identity and mission will transform the world.

Yet divine possibility does not spare Mary from cost. Favor does not mean ease. Pregnancy, social vulnerability, later misunderstanding, and eventually the suffering associated with Jesus’ mission all lie ahead. God’s power does not turn vocation into comfort.

This makes the verse useful in hardship without making it simplistic. Christians can trust that circumstances are never beyond God’s ability to redeem, transform, or work through. But they cannot predict exactly what form that work will take.

For discipleship, Luke 1:37 invites believers to hold human limitation and divine freedom together. People should plan wisely, tell the truth about obstacles, and acknowledge what they cannot do. Then they can also refuse to treat those limits as limits on God.

The verse can become a prayer of openness: “God, I do not see how, but Your word is not powerless.” That posture is different from demanding a predetermined outcome. It trusts God’s character and promise more than human ability to control the future.

Luke 1:37 therefore announces possibility rooted in divine promise. Elizabeth’s barrenness, Mary’s question, and the coming of Jesus all stand inside the same story: God can act where human expectation reaches its boundary. The faithful response is not fantasy but Mary’s courageous trust—“according to thy word.”

Bibliography & Further Reading

  • The Holy Bible, King James Version — Luke 1:26–38. Primary annunciation narrative and immediate context of Luke 1:37.
  • Yale Bible Study, “Luke 1–2: The Birth of the Anointed One — Study Guide”. Academic overview of Luke’s paired infancy narratives, literary design, John and Jesus, and messianic origins.
  • Working Preacher, “Commentary on Luke 1:26–38”. Explores Mary’s fear, questioning, transformation, Elizabeth’s supporting role, and trust amid uncertainty.
  • Joseph A. Fitzmyer, The Gospel According to Luke I–IX, Anchor Bible 28 (Doubleday, 1981). Classic historical and linguistic commentary on Luke’s infancy narrative and annunciation.
  • Joel B. Green, The Gospel of Luke, NICNT (Eerdmans, 1997). Major narrative and theological commentary on Mary, Gabriel, divine promise, reversal, and discipleship.
