The Holy Bible: Douay-Rheims — 2 Machabees

Summary, History, Theology & Annotated Bibliography

Read 2 Maccabees 1:1

Overview

2 Maccabees, called 2 Machabees in the Douay-Rheims Bible, is an independent account of the persecution under Antiochus IV Epiphanes and the early Maccabean struggle. It is not a sequel to 1 Maccabees. Rather, it covers a narrower period—from the high priesthood of Onias III and the reign of Seleucus IV, around 180 B.C., to Judas Maccabeus’s defeat of Nicanor in 161 B.C.—and interprets those events through a strongly theological lens. The work survives in Greek and presents itself as an abridgment of a five-volume history by Jason of Cyrene. [7]

Jason of Cyrene and the Greek Epitome

The compiler explains in 2 Maccabees 2 that Jason of Cyrene had written a five-book history, which the present author condensed into a single volume. Jason’s original work has been lost, so scholars cannot determine exactly how freely the epitomist reshaped it. The Jewish Encyclopedia notes that both Jason’s history and the surviving epitome were written in Greek rather than translated from Hebrew. [3]

The book opens with two letters to Jews in Egypt urging celebration of the Temple’s purification. The later letter is dated to 124 B.C., supporting a likely late-second-century B.C. date for the compiler’s edition. [7]

The Temple at the Center

More than 1 Maccabees, 2 Maccabees makes the Jerusalem Temple the theological center of the story. Chapter 3 recounts Heliodorus’s attempt to seize the Temple treasury, the city’s prayer, and a dramatic heavenly intervention that leaves him helpless. The USCCB says the episode stresses the Temple’s inviolability. [2]

The book later explains the Temple’s profanation not as proof that Israel’s God has been defeated, but as divine chastisement caused by the people’s sins. This theological pattern runs throughout the narrative: when covenant faithfulness collapses, suffering follows; when repentance and fidelity return, divine mercy and deliverance become possible.

Hellenization and Persecution

Chapters 4–6 portray a crisis beginning within Jerusalem itself. Rival high priests, corruption, and Hellenizing reforms weaken the religious order before Antiochus IV begins direct persecution. Jewish practices are suppressed, the Temple desecrated, and people forced toward pagan worship. The author treats the persecution as both political assault and spiritual test.

Michael W. Duggan’s Oxford Handbook of the Apocrypha chapter emphasizes the interaction of three groups in the book: priests who advance Hellenization, observant Jews who resist at the cost of their lives, and the militant force that eventually defeats the Seleucid armies. The narrative therefore joins martyrdom and military resistance rather than treating them as separate stories. [8]

Eleazar and the Martyrs

Chapter 6 tells of Eleazar, an elderly scribe who refuses even to pretend that he has eaten forbidden meat. He chooses death rather than leave younger Jews the impression that he abandoned the law. His martyrdom prepares for the famous account in chapter 7 of a mother and her seven sons tortured for refusing pork.

The brothers repeatedly confess confidence that God will raise the righteous. One expects the King of the universe to restore him because he dies for the law; another surrenders his hands hoping to receive them again from God. The USCCB identifies these verses as clear statements of future bodily resurrection for the just. [1]

The mother’s courage gives the episode its climax. She watches each son die and encourages the last to remain faithful, appealing to God as Creator and trusting that life can be restored beyond death. Jonathan A. Goldstein’s Anchor Yale commentary identifies martyrdom and resurrection among the book’s defining theological themes. [9]

Judas, the Temple, and Hanukkah

Judas Maccabeus then emerges as the military leader through whom the Temple is recovered. After victories over Seleucid forces, his followers cleanse the sanctuary, restore worship, and celebrate the purification for eight days. The annual observance becomes the feast of Dedication, or Hanukkah. [10]

Matthew Goff’s 2024 Oxford treatment stresses that 2 Maccabees presents the martyrs and the militants as parts of the same theological process: the willingness of faithful Jews to die rather than abandon the law contributes to the turning of divine wrath into mercy, after which Judas’s military victories become possible. [11]

Prayer and Sacrifice for the Dead

One of the book’s most important Catholic passages appears in chapter 12. After battle, Judas discovers that fallen soldiers had been wearing objects associated with idols. He and his men pray for forgiveness, and Judas sends money to Jerusalem for an expiatory sacrifice. The narrator explicitly connects this with resurrection hope and calls prayer for the dead a holy and pious thought. [4]

The USCCB notes that this is the earliest explicit statement that prayers and sacrifices for the dead can be efficacious. The passage became an important biblical witness in Catholic reflection on prayer for the departed, while the note carefully distinguishes the precise ancient belief expressed here from the later fully developed doctrine of purgatory.

Heavenly Intercession

Near the end of the book, Judas receives a vision of the deceased high priest Onias and the prophet Jeremiah. Onias is shown praying for the Jewish community, while Jeremiah is described as one who loves his people and prays for the holy city. Jeremiah then gives Judas a golden sword as a divine gift before the final battle with Nicanor. [6]

The USCCB notes that Jeremiah’s heavenly prayer has been understood in Catholic tradition as a biblical witness to the intercession of the saints. Together with the martyr narratives and prayer for the dead, this scene gives 2 Maccabees an unusually developed picture of communion between the faithful living and dead.

Historical and Literary Character

Second Maccabees has genuine historical value, especially for events before the revolt and material absent from 1 Maccabees, but it is not a detached chronicle. Its vivid rhetoric, speeches, apparitions, and arrangement emphasize moral and theological meaning.

Goldstein’s commentary stresses the book’s distinctive Hellenistic-Jewish style and its focus on miracles, Temple holiness, martyrdom, and resurrection. [9] Duggan likewise interprets its refined Hellenistic texture as well suited to a diaspora audience while still presenting Jerusalem and the Temple as the religious center uniting Jews throughout the Mediterranean world. [8]

2 Maccabees and 1 Maccabees

The two books overlap historically but were composed independently and have different aims. First Maccabees covers a longer period and emphasizes the rise of the Hasmonean family, military leadership, law, and national independence. Second Maccabees concentrates on the Temple, divine intervention, martyrdom, resurrection, and theological interpretation. It barely concerns itself with Mattathias and ends with Nicanor’s defeat rather than the later Hasmonean state.

Canon and Lasting Importance

The Catholic Church receives 2 Maccabees as deuterocanonical Scripture. The Council of Trent explicitly lists “the two books of the Machabees, the first and the second” among the canonical books of the Old Testament. [12] It is likewise received in Eastern Christian traditions, while Jewish and Protestant canons do not include it among their canonical books.

Its lasting importance lies in its fusion of history and theology. The Temple is sacred; persecution tests fidelity; martyrs die in resurrection hope; the living pray for the dead; righteous figures in heaven intercede; and victory is interpreted as divine mercy. Few Old Testament books state these themes so directly. Second Maccabees therefore remains indispensable for the Maccabean crisis, Hanukkah, the development of Jewish resurrection belief, and the theological world inherited by early Christianity.
