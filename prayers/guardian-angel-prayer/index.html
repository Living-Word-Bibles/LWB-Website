Common Christian Prayers

The Guardian Angel Prayer

Angel of God, my guardian dear, to whom God’s love commits me here, ever this day be at my side, to light and guard, to rule and guide. Amen.

About The Guardian Angel Prayer

The Guardian Angel Prayer, often called simply “Angel of God,” is a compact prayer asking for the care and guidance of the angel whom Christian tradition associates with God’s protection of an individual believer. Its language is especially familiar in Roman Catholic family devotion and religious education, where generations of children have learned it as a morning or bedtime prayer. The prayer is also known beyond Catholic settings, though Christian traditions differ in how explicitly they speak about personal guardian angels.

The Bible presents angels as created spiritual servants and messengers of God. They worship God, announce divine purposes, protect, guide, and minister according to God’s command. Psalm 91 speaks of God commanding angels concerning the faithful. Hebrews describes angels as “ministering spirits” sent to serve those who will inherit salvation. Jesus warns against despising “little ones” and refers to “their angels” who behold the Father’s face. These passages helped nourish later Christian reflection on angelic guardianship.

At the same time, biblical teaching directs worship to God, not to angels. In Revelation, when John falls before an angel, he is told to worship God. That distinction is crucial for understanding the Guardian Angel Prayer. The prayer does not treat the angel as an independent divine power. Its opening line explicitly grounds the angel’s care in God’s action: “to whom God’s love commits me here.” Any angelic protection is therefore understood as a ministry assigned by God.

Roman Catholic teaching gives personal guardian angels a defined place in its doctrine of creation and providence. The Catechism states that human life is surrounded by angelic care and intercession and cites early Christian testimony about an angel standing beside each believer as protector and shepherd. The Church’s official Compendium includes the “Angel of God” among common prayers, demonstrating its established place in Catholic devotion.

The prayer’s four verbs—“light and guard, to rule and guide”—summarize its spiritual vision. “Light” asks for illumination, not new revelation that supersedes Scripture, but help in seeing the path of faithfulness. “Guard” expresses trust in God’s providential protection. “Rule” reflects an older sense of directing or governing conduct rather than domination. “Guide” asks for help toward what is good. Together the verbs turn the prayer toward moral and spiritual formation, not curiosity about the hidden world of angels.

Christians should also avoid speculative angelology. Scripture gives glimpses of angelic ministry but does not encourage believers to discover an angel’s secret name, seek private messages, or build elaborate spiritual systems around angels. Christian devotion is safest when it remains centered on God and measured by Scripture. In traditions that do not practice prayer addressed to angels, the biblical passages about angelic ministry can still be received as testimony to God’s providence without adopting the devotional formula itself.

For families, the prayer’s simplicity is one reason for its endurance. It teaches children that they live under God’s care and that spiritual life includes both protection and guidance. For adults, the same lines can be prayed with greater depth: God’s providence does not promise freedom from every hardship, but it does place human life within a larger unseen order of divine service and care.

The Guardian Angel Prayer is therefore best understood not as fascination with angels for their own sake, but as a short expression of confidence that God’s love is active, ordered, and attentive.

Bibliography & Further Reading

  • The Holy Bible, Psalm 91:11–12; Matthew 18:10; Hebrews 1:14; Revelation 22:8–9. Key passages concerning angelic protection, ministry, and the command to worship God alone.
  • Catechism of the Catholic Church, §§328–336. Summarizes Catholic teaching on angels and specifically on guardian angels.
  • Compendium of the Catechism of the Catholic Church, Appendix A. Includes the official English and Latin forms of the “Angel of God” prayer.
  • United States Conference of Catholic Bishops, “Prayer to Your Guardian Angel.” Provides the prayer in current Catholic devotional use.
  • Peter Kreeft, Angels and Demons: What Do We Really Know about Them? (Ignatius Press, 1995). A popular theological treatment of angelology that distinguishes historic Christian teaching from speculation.
