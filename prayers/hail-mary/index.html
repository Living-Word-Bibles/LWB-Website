Common Christian Prayers

Hail Mary

Hail, Mary, full of grace, the Lord is with thee. Blessed art thou amongst women and blessed is the fruit of thy womb, Jesus. Holy Mary, Mother of God, pray for us sinners, now and at the hour of our death. Amen.

About Hail Mary

The Hail Mary, traditionally called the Ave Maria from its Latin opening, is among the best-known prayers of Western Christianity. Its first half is built directly from the Gospel of Luke. The angel Gabriel greets Mary at the Annunciation, and Elizabeth later blesses Mary and the child she carries. By joining these biblical greetings, the prayer places Jesus at its center: Mary is blessed because of God’s grace and because “the fruit” of her womb is Christ.

The prayer did not appear in its complete modern form all at once. Early Christian devotion honored Mary through biblical language, hymns, and liturgical antiphons. In the medieval West, the angelic greeting and Elizabeth’s blessing were increasingly joined as a devotional formula. The name “Jesus” became a customary conclusion to the first portion, sharpening the Christ-centered character of the prayer. The petition beginning “Holy Mary, Mother of God, pray for us sinners” developed later and reached a stable liturgical form in the early modern period.

The title “Mother of God” translates the ancient Christian term Theotokos, literally “God-bearer.” The title is not intended to make Mary the source of Christ’s divinity. Rather, it protects the confession that the one whom Mary bore is truly Jesus Christ, one person who is fully divine and fully human. The Council of Ephesus in 431 famously defended this language in the context of debates about Christ’s identity. Thus the Marian title ultimately serves a Christological claim.

The second half of the Hail Mary is a request for intercession: “pray for us sinners, now and at the hour of our death.” Roman Catholic and many other historic Christian traditions understand this as asking a fellow member of the communion of saints to pray with and for the believer. Many Protestant Christians, while honoring Mary as the mother of Jesus and “blessed among women,” do not invoke saints in prayer and therefore do not use the Hail Mary devotionally. A responsible Bible-study treatment should acknowledge that difference without caricaturing either tradition.

The Hail Mary is especially associated with the Rosary, where it is repeated while the worshiper meditates on events in the life, death, and resurrection of Christ. In that setting, repetition is intended to provide a steady rhythm for contemplation rather than to replace biblical meditation. The prayer also appears in other Catholic devotions and in various Anglican and ecumenical contexts.

For study, Luke 1 is the essential starting point. Gabriel’s greeting belongs to the story of the Incarnation, and Elizabeth’s words arise when she recognizes God’s work in Mary and the unborn Jesus. Reading the prayer against the Magnificat that follows also prevents Marian devotion from becoming detached from Mary’s own praise of God. Her song magnifies the Lord, celebrates divine mercy, and points toward God’s saving action in history.

The Hail Mary is therefore best understood as a prayer shaped by Scripture, Christology, and centuries of Christian devotion. Even Christians who do not pray it can study it profitably as a window into how different traditions have received Luke’s account of the Annunciation and Visitation.

Bibliography & Further Reading

  • The Holy Bible, Luke 1:26–56. The biblical source of Gabriel’s greeting, Elizabeth’s blessing, and Mary’s Magnificat.
  • Catechism of the Catholic Church, §§2675–2679. Explains the biblical and devotional structure of Marian prayer, especially the Ave Maria.
  • Paul VI, Marialis Cultus (1974), §49. Describes the Hail Mary within the structure and Christ-centered meditation of the Rosary.
  • “Hail Mary,” Catholic Encyclopedia. A historical reference article tracing the gradual development of the prayer’s wording.
  • Pope Francis, General Audience, 24 March 2021. A contemporary Catholic explanation of Marian intercession and its relation to Christ.
