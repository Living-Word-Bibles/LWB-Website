Bible Study: 1 Corinthians 6:19-20

First Corinthians 6:19–20 contains one of Paul’s most memorable statements about the human body: “your body is the temple of the Holy Ghost which is in you.” These words are sometimes quoted mainly in discussions of diet, exercise, smoking, or general health. Caring for health can certainly be a wise application of Christian stewardship, but Paul’s immediate subject is sexual ethics and the believer’s union with Christ. The larger passage, 6:12–20, responds to Corinthian slogans about freedom and the body. Paul argues that Christian freedom cannot be used to detach bodily actions from spiritual allegiance.

Corinthian culture was shaped by ordinary Greco-Roman assumptions about status, sexuality, household power, and religious life. Some members of the church appear to have reasoned that bodily appetites were morally indifferent. Paul answers that the body is “for the Lord; and the Lord for the body” (6:13). Christianity is not salvation of an inner soul from an irrelevant body. God raised the Lord and will also raise believers. The body therefore belongs within God’s redemptive purpose.

Paul develops this point through union with Christ. Believers’ bodies are “members of Christ” (6:15). Sexual union is not treated as a trivial physical transaction because bodily actions express real relationships. Paul quotes Genesis 2:24—“they two shall be one flesh”—to show that sexuality has covenantal and relational depth. He then contrasts becoming “one flesh” with another person and being “one spirit” with the Lord. His command to “flee fornication” follows from identity, not from contempt for the body.

The temple image intensifies the argument. In 1 Corinthians 3:16–17 Paul has already described the gathered church as God’s temple. Here the language is applied to believers’ embodied existence. The Greek pronouns are plural, which reminds readers that even this apparently personal statement has a communal dimension. The Holy Spirit dwelling in God’s people means that bodies are not private property in an absolute sense. What Christians do bodily affects their relationship to Christ and the holiness of the community.

“Ye are not your own” can sound threatening when detached from the gospel. Paul immediately explains it with redemption: “For ye are bought with a price.” The image evokes purchase or liberation, and elsewhere Paul describes Christ’s self-giving death as the decisive act by which believers belong to God. Christian moral claims therefore arise from grace. Paul is not saying that religious authorities own a believer’s body or may control it. He is saying that the Christian’s deepest allegiance has been transformed by Christ’s costly redemption.

The final command is positive: “therefore glorify God in your body.” Holiness is more than avoiding prohibited acts. The body becomes an arena of worship. Eating, resting, sexuality, work, speech, care, service, suffering, and physical presence can all be offered to God. This helps explain why the verse legitimately informs broader questions of health and stewardship, even though those are not its immediate topic. A body that belongs to God should not be treated with contempt, exploited, or deliberately enslaved to destructive appetites.

At the same time, responsible application requires care. This passage should not be weaponized against people with illness, disability, addiction, eating disorders, or bodies that do not match cultural ideals. Paul’s theology dignifies the body because of God’s Spirit and resurrection promise, not because the body achieves a certain appearance or level of performance. Christian communities should therefore resist both neglect of bodily life and the idolization of health, beauty, or physical control.

The text also has implications for sexual ethics that are relational rather than merely rule-based. Paul sees sexual behavior as involving persons who bear spiritual and bodily dignity. Christian chastity is not hatred of sexuality; it is the conviction that sexuality belongs within God’s purposes and cannot be separated from faithfulness, holiness, and care for another person. The command to flee sexual immorality is therefore paired with the larger summons to glorify God bodily.

First Corinthians 6:19–20 ultimately gives Christians a theology of embodied discipleship. The body is not disposable, morally irrelevant, or finally owned by appetite. It has been claimed by Christ, indwelt by the Spirit, and destined for resurrection. That identity places limits on Christian freedom, but those limits are the shape of belonging. Paul’s vision is not “your body is shameful.” It is nearly the opposite: your body matters so deeply to God that it is included in redemption and called to become a place where God is honored.

The resurrection horizon is crucial here. Paul’s body theology is not merely about present discipline; it is rooted in God’s promise to raise embodied people. Christian holiness therefore honors the body as part of the person God intends to redeem, not as a disposable shell around a more important soul.

Bibliography & Further Reading

  • The Holy Bible, King James Version — 1 Corinthians 6:12–20. Primary biblical context.
  • Working Preacher, “Commentary on 1 Corinthians 6:12–20”. Explores freedom, embodiment, and the individual and communal force of Paul’s temple language.
  • Yale Bible Study, 1 Corinthians course. Historical and literary context for Corinthian identity, ethics, and community conflict.
  • Gordon D. Fee, The First Epistle to the Corinthians, rev. ed., NICNT (Eerdmans, 2014). Major exegetical treatment of 1 Corinthians 6 and Pauline theology of the body.
  • Anthony C. Thiselton, The First Epistle to the Corinthians, NIGTC (Eerdmans, 2000). Detailed discussion of Corinthian slogans, sexual ethics, temple imagery, and Greek grammar.
