Bible Study: Matthew 28:19-20

Matthew 28:19–20 contains the words commonly called the Great Commission: “Go ye therefore, and teach all nations, baptizing them … teaching them to observe all things whatsoever I have commanded you.” Jesus then closes Matthew’s Gospel with the promise, “I am with you alway, even unto the end of the world.” The commission is missionary, but it is more than a command to travel or secure conversions. It summarizes Matthew’s entire vision of discipleship.

The scene takes place after the resurrection. Eleven disciples go to a mountain in Galilee where Jesus directed them. Mountains are important places of revelation in Matthew: Jesus teaches on a mountain, is transfigured on a mountain, and now commissions disciples on a mountain.

Matthew says the disciples worship Jesus, “but some doubted.” That small detail is important. The Great Commission is entrusted to a community that has encountered the risen Christ but is not portrayed as psychologically flawless. Mission begins in worship mixed with human limitation.

Jesus first declares, “All power is given unto me in heaven and in earth.” The commission rests on His authority rather than the disciples’ institutional power. Working Preacher highlights four “alls” in the paragraph: all authority, all nations, all Jesus commanded, and Jesus’ presence all the days.

The central imperative in Greek is “make disciples.” “Go,” “baptizing,” and “teaching” support that central command. Mission therefore aims at forming disciples, not merely generating decisions or expanding membership statistics.

“All nations” expands the scope of Matthew’s story. Earlier Jesus’ earthly ministry focuses primarily on Israel, yet Matthew also includes Gentile signals from the beginning: Gentile women in the genealogy, the Magi, Gentile encounters, and Jesus’ statements anticipating a wider mission.

Yale Bible Study notes that the Gospel’s ending neatly summarizes this universal trajectory. The risen Jesus sends the disciples beyond the boundaries that marked much of His earlier ministry.

“Baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost” places new disciples within a new identity and community. Later Trinitarian theology found this formula deeply significant, though Working Preacher cautions against pretending the first-century verse already contained every later creedal formulation in developed form.

Baptism is paired with teaching. The disciples are to teach people “to observe all things whatsoever I have commanded you.” Matthew therefore understands mission as ethical and formative. A disciple learns to live Jesus’ teaching.

That includes the Sermon on the Mount, mercy, reconciliation, enemy-love, prayer, generosity, truthfulness, care for the vulnerable, and obedience. The Great Commission cannot be separated from the actual content of Jesus’ commands.

This matters because Christian mission has sometimes been distorted by colonialism, coercion, cultural destruction, and political domination. “All authority” belongs to Jesus, not to missionaries or empires. The commission does not authorize forced conversion.

The manner of mission should reflect the teacher whose commandments are being taught. Jesus’ authority in Matthew is exercised through teaching, healing, mercy, service, confrontation of hypocrisy, suffering, and resurrection—not through coercive conquest.

Making disciples also differs from making cultural replicas. “All nations” means the gospel crosses cultures rather than erasing them. Christianity has always taken different linguistic, musical, artistic, and social forms. Faithfulness asks whether people are becoming disciples of Jesus, not whether they adopt every preference of the missionary.

The command is communal. Jesus addresses the gathered disciples, and baptism incorporates people into a people. Christian mission should create communities of learning, worship, mercy, and obedience rather than isolated religious consumers.

The promise at the end is essential: “I am with you alway.” Matthew’s Gospel began with Jesus named Emmanuel, “God with us.” It ends with the risen Jesus promising continuing presence. The entire Gospel is framed by divine companionship.

This promise also limits anxiety about mission. The church is responsible to witness faithfully, but it is not the savior of the world. Jesus remains present and authoritative.

For discipleship, Matthew 28:19–20 asks every Christian community how it participates in making disciples. Some are called to cross cultures geographically; others teach children, mentor believers, translate Scripture, serve neighbors, support missionaries, welcome newcomers, or embody Jesus’ commands locally.

The commission also turns inward. A church cannot teach others to obey everything Jesus commanded while ignoring those commands itself. Mission begins with ongoing discipleship among the missionaries.

Matthew’s final words therefore hold together authority, universality, baptism, teaching, obedience, and presence. Christians are sent because the risen Jesus reigns. They make disciples rather than subjects, teach obedience rather than mere information, and go with the assurance that Emmanuel remains with them to the end of the age.
