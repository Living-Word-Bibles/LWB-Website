The Holy Bible: Douay-Rheims — Baruch

Summary, History, Theology & Annotated Bibliography

Read Baruch 1:1

Overview

Baruch is a deuterocanonical book of the Old Testament associated by its opening verses with Baruch son of Neriah, the well-known scribe and companion of the prophet Jeremiah. Rather than unfolding as a single continuous narrative, the book gathers several distinct literary forms: a historical introduction, a confession of sin and prayer for mercy, a poem praising divine Wisdom, a prophetic poem of consolation addressed to Jerusalem and the exiles, and—in the Catholic arrangement—a sixth chapter known as the Letter of Jeremiah. The United States Conference of Catholic Bishops describes the work as a collection of four different compositions united by the experience of exile, repentance, wisdom, and promised restoration. [1]

Baruch, Jeremiah, and the Setting of Exile

The book places its opening scene in Babylon after the destruction of Jerusalem. Baruch reads a scroll before King Jeconiah—Jehoiachin—and the Judean exiles. They weep, fast, pray, collect money, and send gifts to Jerusalem for sacrifices and worship. The literary setting connects the community in exile with the ruined holy city and its temple, portraying repentance not as private emotion but as an act involving worship, memory, solidarity, and renewed allegiance to God. [1]

Modern scholarship distinguishes this literary setting from the date when the book reached its present form. The USCCB states that no certain date can be assigned and suggests final editing sometime in the last two centuries B.C. Karina Martin Hogan, writing in the Oxford Handbook of the Apocrypha, treats Baruch as an example of creative reuse of earlier Scripture by Jewish authors or editors in the Hellenistic or Roman period. [5] The traditional connection with Jeremiah remains essential to the book’s literary identity even when questions of composition are considered historically.

Confession, Sin, and Repentance

Baruch 1:15–3:8 develops an extended confession. Israel’s catastrophe is interpreted through a Deuteronomic pattern: the people disobeyed God, judgment followed, and restoration requires repentance. The community does not portray itself merely as an innocent victim of foreign power. It acknowledges its own covenant unfaithfulness and confesses that divine judgment has been just. Yet confession is directed toward mercy rather than despair.

The prayer appeals to God’s past acts of deliverance, especially the exodus, and asks him to remember his name, covenant, and people. The exiles recognize that they have inherited the consequences of earlier rebellion, but they also turn personally toward God. Hogan identifies alienation, sin, repentance, and restoration as themes linking Baruch’s different literary sections, while the USCCB notes strong similarities between this prayer and Daniel 9 as well as passages in Jeremiah. [5]

Wisdom Identified with Torah

At Baruch 3:9 the book shifts from confession to a poem about Wisdom. Israel is asked why it has grown old in a foreign land and is told that it has forsaken the fountain of wisdom. Kings, wealthy rulers, warriors, and nations cannot obtain Wisdom through power, wealth, exploration, or human achievement. God alone knows her way because he created and governs the universe.

The poem then makes its most important claim: God has revealed Wisdom to Jacob and Israel, and Wisdom is identified with the book of God’s commandments—the Law that endures forever. The USCCB explicitly notes the connection between Baruch’s Wisdom poem, Job 28, and Sirach 24. Wisdom is therefore not an esoteric possession reserved for philosophers or rulers. Israel possesses access to divine Wisdom through the Torah and is summoned to return to it. [2]

Jerusalem, Zion, and the Promise of Consolation

Baruch 4:5–5:9 changes tone again, moving into prophetic consolation. Jerusalem is personified as a grieving mother whose children have been carried away. She acknowledges that the exile came because of sin, but she tells her children to take courage and trust that the God who permitted their scattering can also bring them home. The language strongly recalls the consoling prophecies of Isaiah 40–66. [1]

Chapter 5 reaches a dramatic climax. Jerusalem is told to remove the clothing of mourning and put on the splendor of divine glory. Her scattered children will return from east and west, and God will level mountains and fill valleys so that Israel may advance securely. The imagery transforms exile into a new procession of restoration. Mourning becomes glory, displacement becomes gathering, and humiliation gives way to justice and worship. [7]

The Letter of Jeremiah: Baruch 6

In the Douay-Rheims and Latin Vulgate tradition, the Letter of Jeremiah forms Baruch chapter 6. In major Greek manuscripts and many Orthodox Bibles it circulates as a separate work. The letter presents itself as instruction to people facing exile and repeatedly warns them not to fear or worship the idols of the nations. Its argument is deliberately concrete: idols must be carried, cleaned, guarded, clothed, and repaired by human hands, yet they cannot speak, rescue anyone, judge, or save themselves. [1]

The USCCB identifies ten recurring warnings built around the conclusion that these idols are not gods and therefore are not to be feared. The polemic resembles Jeremiah 10, Isaiah 44, Psalms 115 and 135, and the anti-idolatry sections of Wisdom. Sean A. Adams’s Brill commentary studies both Baruch and the Letter of Jeremiah directly from the Greek text of Codex Vaticanus, the oldest extant manuscript containing the books, and emphasizes how Greek-speaking readers would have encountered them. [6]

Language and Textual History

The textual history of Baruch is complex. The USCCB notes that the original language may have been Hebrew, although the preserved text is known through Greek and other ancient versions. Scholars have long argued that portions of Baruch reflect translation from a Semitic source, while the exact history of the individual sections remains debated. Adams’s commentary deliberately approaches the surviving Septuagint form as a Greek literary text rather than treating it only as evidence for a lost Hebrew original. [6]

The Letter of Jeremiah has its own transmission history. A small Greek fragment from Qumran Cave 7, designated 7Q2, has been proposed as containing part of the Letter, though Adams cautions that the identification is not absolutely certain because the fragment is extremely small. The Greek textual tradition nevertheless demonstrates the antiquity and wide circulation of the work within Jewish and Christian scriptural collections. [6]

Canon and Christian Reception

Baruch belongs to the Catholic Old Testament deuterocanon. The Council of Trent’s decree on canonical Scripture formally includes Baruch in the received books of the Old Testament. [3] It is also received as Scripture in Eastern Orthodox and Oriental Orthodox traditions. The book is not part of the Jewish biblical canon and is generally placed among the Apocrypha in Protestant traditions. Its long association with Jeremiah helped secure its place in Christian biblical manuscripts and theological interpretation. [5]

Lasting Importance

Baruch’s lasting power comes from the movement of its theology: from catastrophe to confession, from confession to wisdom, and from wisdom to hope. The exiles are not told to deny their guilt or pretend that Jerusalem’s destruction did not happen. They are called to interpret suffering truthfully, return to God, rediscover Wisdom in the divine Law, reject the false security of idols, and trust that judgment need not be the end of the story. Zion’s mourning can become joy because the God who judges is also the God who remembers, gathers, consoles, and restores. In that sense Baruch is a book for communities living between loss and renewal—a meditation on how repentance, Scripture, worship, and hope can preserve identity when the visible structures of religious and national life appear to have collapsed.
