Bible Study: Deuteronomy 31:6

Deuteronomy 31:6 speaks courage at a moment of major transition: “Be strong and of a good courage, fear not, nor be afraid of them: for the LORD thy God, he it is that doth go with thee; he will not fail thee, nor forsake thee.” Moses is nearing death, Israel is preparing to cross the Jordan, and the generation that has depended on Moses must continue without him. The promise does not pretend that nothing frightening lies ahead. It grounds courage in the presence of God.

The chapter begins with Moses acknowledging his age and the end of his leadership. He will not cross the Jordan. The people face nations and fortified cities on the other side, and Joshua will assume leadership. In Deuteronomy’s narrative world, this is a vulnerable moment. Leadership changes, uncertainty increases, and the future cannot be controlled. Moses responds by directing Israel’s confidence away from himself and toward the Lord.

“Be strong and of a good courage” is repeated in Deuteronomy 31 and then echoed in Joshua 1. The repetition shows that courage is not merely a personality trait belonging to naturally fearless people. It is a covenant posture. Israel can move forward because the God who acted in Egypt and the wilderness remains present. Courage grows from remembered faithfulness.

The command “fear not” should not be misunderstood as a prohibition against every emotional experience of fear. Biblical characters regularly feel afraid. The issue is whether fear becomes the governing power that prevents obedience. Israel must not let the intimidating strength of other nations erase its memory of God. Christian readers can apply the same principle without assuming every challenge is a conquest God has promised them personally.

The promise “he will not fail thee, nor forsake thee” is especially powerful because Moses himself is leaving. Human leaders are temporary. God’s covenant presence is not. That distinction protects people from making any pastor, parent, mentor, political figure, or institution indispensable. Faithful leaders should prepare communities to depend on God rather than on the leader’s permanence.

The conquest context also requires ethical care. Deuteronomy’s commands concerning Canaan are part of Israel’s ancient national story and have been used disastrously when later peoples treated themselves as a new Israel authorized to seize land or destroy enemies. Modern Christians should not turn Deuteronomy 31:6 into permission for conquest, colonialism, or violence. Its enduring theological claim is about God’s faithfulness to His people amid fearful transition, not a blank authorization for religious warfare.

The surrounding chapter emphasizes Torah as well as courage. Moses writes down the law and commands that it be read publicly so the people may hear, learn, fear the Lord, and observe His words. God’s presence does not eliminate the need for obedience. Courage and covenant instruction belong together. Israel is not simply told to feel confident; it is called to continue faithfully under God’s word.

The verse also speaks to communal transitions. Churches change pastors, families lose elders, organizations face succession, and individuals enter seasons where familiar guides are absent. Fear can intensify when a trusted human presence disappears. Deuteronomy 31:6 does not deny grief over that loss. It insists that God is not leaving simply because Moses is.

For discipleship, the verse offers a durable basis for courage. Strength is not pretending to be invulnerable. It is taking the next faithful step while trusting the God who goes with His people. Believers can acknowledge danger, prepare wisely, seek counsel, and still refuse to be ruled by fear. The promise is not that every plan will succeed exactly as hoped. The promise is about God’s presence: He does not abandon His people. Courage grows when the future is placed in the hands of the One whose faithfulness outlasts every human transition.

Remembered history is therefore part of courage. Israel is not asked to trust an unknown deity but the Lord whose acts have already shaped its story. Christian courage similarly grows through remembering God’s faithfulness in Scripture and in the life of the community, especially when the next stage of the journey feels uncertain.

The wording also teaches that courage can be communal. Moses addresses all Israel before he turns personally to Joshua. The people must move forward together, remembering that God’s presence belongs to the covenant community and not only to one heroic leader. Fear often isolates, convincing people that they alone must solve what lies ahead. Deuteronomy answers with a shared identity and a shared promise. Israel enters the next season together under the God who goes with them.
