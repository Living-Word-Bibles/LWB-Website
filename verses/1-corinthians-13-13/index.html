Bible Study: 1 Corinthians 13:13

First Corinthians 13:13 closes one of the most familiar passages in the New Testament: “And now abideth faith, hope, charity, these three; but the greatest of these is charity.” In the King James Version, “charity” translates the Greek word agapē, a word normally rendered “love” in modern English. The older English term should not be reduced to charitable giving. Paul is describing the self-giving love that seeks another person’s good, refuses rivalry and self-display, and reflects the character of Christ. Verse 13 gathers the chapter into three enduring realities—faith, hope, and love—and then gives love a distinctive priority.

The verse is often read at weddings, but its original setting is a conflicted congregation. First Corinthians addresses divisions over leaders, status, knowledge, worship, spiritual gifts, sexuality, lawsuits, food, and the Lord’s Supper. Chapters 12–14 focus especially on spiritual gifts. Some Corinthians appear to have prized spectacular gifts, particularly tongues, in ways that could become competitive. Paul responds by describing the church as one body with many members and then announces “a more excellent way” (12:31): love. Chapter 13 therefore is not an interruption between discussions of gifts. It is the theological center of Paul’s argument about how gifts must be used.

Faith, hope, and love form a memorable triad elsewhere in Paul’s letters, especially 1 Thessalonians 1:3 and 5:8. Faith is trust in and allegiance to God through Christ. Hope looks toward God’s promised future, including resurrection and the completion of redemption. Love is the pattern of life created by God’s love and embodied in relationships. These are not competing virtues. They belong together. Faith without love can become empty religious confidence; hope without love can become private escape; love detached from faith and hope can lose the distinctly Christian story that gives it shape.

Why, then, is love “the greatest”? The preceding verses help answer. Prophecies, tongues, and partial knowledge belong to the present age and will reach their goal when believers know fully and see “face to face.” Love, however, is not merely a temporary instrument. It belongs to the character of the life toward which God is bringing his people. Christian hope anticipates communion with God and with one another, and love is already the moral reality of that coming life. In this sense, love participates now in what will remain when partial gifts have fulfilled their purpose.

Paul’s claim also reflects the way love fulfills the purpose of spiritual gifts. Gifts are not badges proving that one Christian is more advanced than another. They are given “to profit withal,” for the common good (12:7). The person who speaks brilliantly, understands mysteries, possesses extraordinary faith, gives away possessions, or even endures heroic suffering gains nothing if such actions are severed from love (13:1–3). Love is greatest because it determines whether power, knowledge, sacrifice, and spirituality are directed toward the good of another or toward the glorification of self.

The KJV’s word “charity” can be spiritually useful when readers understand its older meaning. Contemporary English often uses “love” for attraction, preference, sentiment, family attachment, or romance. Paul’s agapē certainly includes affection, but 13:4–7 describes it primarily through disciplined actions: patience, kindness, truthfulness, endurance, and refusal of envy, boasting, arrogance, selfishness, and resentment. The love of verse 13 is therefore costly and concrete. It appears in how Christians speak, forgive, share attention, exercise gifts, handle disagreement, and honor people who possess less visible status.

Faith and hope remain indispensable in Christian life. Paul is not saying they are unimportant. Rather, love is the sphere in which faith and hope become recognizable as Christian virtues. Galatians 5:6 similarly speaks of “faith which worketh by love.” Hope also produces patience and endurance for the sake of God and neighbor. The three mutually reinforce one another: faith receives God’s grace, hope expects God’s future, and love lives the life of that future in the present.

For modern disciples, 1 Corinthians 13:13 asks a searching question about spiritual maturity. Churches can measure success by attendance, knowledge, influence, doctrinal precision, eloquence, or visible gifts. Paul does not dismiss truth or gifts, but he insists that the greatest mark is love shaped by Christ. The verse therefore belongs not only on wedding programs but in ordinary congregational life. Where faith trusts God, hope refuses despair, and love seeks another’s good, the church begins to display the life of the kingdom that will endure.

The triad also gives believers a durable framework for seasons of uncertainty. Faith looks to the trustworthy God, hope refuses to let present circumstances define the future, and love asks what faithfulness requires toward the person in front of us. Paul’s ordering keeps Christian spirituality from becoming merely private or speculative.

Bibliography & Further Reading

  • The Holy Bible, King James Version — 1 Corinthians 12–14. Primary biblical context for spiritual gifts, love, and orderly worship.
  • Working Preacher, “Commentary on 1 Corinthians 13:1–13”. Explains Paul’s description of love as concrete action and locates chapter 13 within congregational practice.
  • Yale Bible Study, 1 Corinthians course. Academic study of the letter’s historical setting, rhetoric, conflict, gifts, and communal theology.
  • Gordon D. Fee, The First Epistle to the Corinthians, rev. ed., NICNT (Eerdmans, 2014). Influential commentary on Paul’s theology of gifts, love, and the church.
  • Anthony C. Thiselton, The First Epistle to the Corinthians, NIGTC (Eerdmans, 2000). Detailed exegetical treatment of 1 Corinthians 13 and the semantic range of agapē.
