Overview

The Berean Standard Bible (BSB) is a modern English translation produced through the Berean Bible project in cooperation with Bible Hub, Discovery Bible, OpenBible.com, and the Berean Bible Translation Committee. The project describes the BSB as a completely new English translation based on Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek sources, designed for public reading, study, memorization, and evangelism. Its distinguishing idea is transparency: the translation is connected to interlinear and literal tiers so that readers can trace English wording back toward the original-language data rather than encountering the polished English text in isolation.

The name comes from Acts 17:11, where the Bereans are commended for receiving the message eagerly and examining the Scriptures daily. That verse functions almost as a mission statement for the project. The BSB aims to be readable enough for ordinary church use while remaining closely connected to the linguistic and textual evidence behind the translation.

Brief History

  1. 2015–2016
    Early public use and New Testament circulation. By 2015 the Berean Study Bible had been selected by the World Bible Quiz Association for its 2016–2017 program, and the New Testament was circulating publicly under the Berean Study Bible name.
  2. 2020
    Complete Bible era. The project’s own pages preserve 2016/2020 copyright-era references, reflecting the movement from the earlier New Testament phase into a complete Old and New Testament text.
  3. Early 2022
    Name changed to “Berean Standard Bible.” Software distributors record the publisher’s change from “Berean Study Bible” to “Berean Standard Bible,” reducing confusion with conventional study Bibles that contain extensive commentary.
  4. Apr 30, 2023
    Public-domain dedication. The Berean Bible and Majority Bible texts were officially dedicated to the public domain. All uses are freely permitted; attribution is appreciated but not required.
  5. 2024–2026
    Third-printing text and wider distribution. Official downloads remain labeled “Current, updated to 3rd Printing.” The project now reports that the third physical printing has sold out while additional print editions and related Berean translation projects continue in production.

Why “Berean”?

The project’s name deliberately points to the Bereans of Acts 17. In the biblical account, they are praised not for accepting a teacher uncritically but for receiving the message eagerly and then examining the Scriptures daily. The translation project repeatedly returns to that verse in its official pages. The emphasis fits the BSB’s architecture: the English text is meant to be read devotionally and publicly, but readers are also encouraged to inspect interlinear data, literal renderings, cross-references, translation tables, and textual notes.

This gives the name a methodological significance. “Berean” is not simply a brand label; it describes the kind of reading the project wants to encourage—receptive, careful, comparative, and willing to examine the evidence behind a translation.

From “Study Bible” to “Standard Bible”

The earlier title Berean Study Bible sometimes suggested a conventional study Bible—a translation surrounded by large blocks of commentary. The project itself explains that this was never quite what the name meant. The printed text included the Bible, footnotes, section headings, and cross-references, while deeper translation tables, lexicons, outlines, and related tools were provided separately online. By early 2022 the title had shifted to Berean Standard Bible, emphasizing the translation itself rather than the idea of a commentary-heavy study edition.

Translation Philosophy: The Tiered Model

The Berean project is organized around multiple translation tiers rather than one English rendering being expected to do everything at once. The official tier description begins with an interlinear that follows the Hebrew and Greek closely, then a literal translation aimed at exposing core lexical meaning. The BSB occupies the modern-English tier, where the wording is shaped for fluent reading while remaining traceable to the earlier tiers. A further emphasized or annotated tier expands meaning and intensity for study.

This structure reflects a basic translation reality: ancient languages do not map onto English one word at a time. A rigidly literal line may preserve form while sounding unnatural; a smoother line may communicate the sense but hide lexical or grammatical relationships. The Berean model therefore places “word for word” and “thought for thought” work on connected levels. The BSB is the point at which those layers are styled into contemporary English suitable for reading aloud, memorization, teaching, and evangelism.

An Open Translation Process

The translation process is unusually public. Official project pages say that translation tables have been made available for comment and evaluation and that hundreds of questions, suggestions, and corrections have been received from pastors, teachers, students, seminarians, professors, and lay readers. The core translation and coordination work was carried out by the Bible Hub and Discovery Bible teams under an advisory committee.

The advisory editors named by the project include Dr. Gary Hill, Dr. Grant Osborne, Dr. Eugene H. Merrill, Dr. Maury Robertson, Dr. Ulrik Sandborg-Petersen, and Dr. Baruch Korman. The project identifies Osborne with New Testament leadership and Merrill with Old Testament leadership. Bible Hub contributed base-text and interlinear work, while Discovery Bible contributed language support and expanded translation notes associated with Dr. Gleason Archer. The committee describes the Bible as being translated as a single unit rather than assigning independent books to isolated teams, with the goal of improving consistency across parallel passages and repeated terminology.

Greek, Hebrew & Aramaic Sources

The BSB does not identify one printed Greek or Hebrew edition as the sole source behind every decision. Instead, its official source list documents a broad range of textual witnesses and critical editions and footnotes significant differences among them. For the New Testament, the project lists Nestle-Aland, the SBL Greek New Testament, the Editio Critica Maior, Eberhard Nestle, Westcott and Hort, the Byzantine Textform, the Greek Orthodox text, and forms of the Textus Receptus.

For the Old Testament, the project lists the Westminster Leningrad Codex and Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia within the Masoretic tradition, together with the Dead Sea Scrolls, the Septuagint in Rahlfs-Hanhart and Swete forms, and the Samaritan Pentateuch. This approach lets the notes signal places where major textual traditions differ instead of presenting every verse as though the manuscript evidence were identical.

Translation Character

In practice, the BSB usually reads as a middle-to-formal modern translation. It is not an interlinear and does not try to reproduce Hebrew or Greek syntax mechanically. At the same time, its wording frequently stays close enough to the source structure to support detailed comparison with the literal and interlinear tiers. The official project describes this balance with three ideas: authenticity, because wording is connected to original-language sources; elegance, because the English is deliberately styled for literary quality and public reading; and availability, because the text and study resources are intended for broad distribution.

The BSB therefore fits naturally in churches and study settings where readers want contemporary English without moving as far toward paraphrase as some functionally equivalent translations. Like every translation, it still involves interpretive decisions. Its special value is that the project publishes much of the underlying translation infrastructure so readers can examine those decisions more easily.

Footnotes, Cross-References & Textual Variants

The standard BSB text includes footnotes, section headings, and cross-references rather than extensive doctrinal commentary. The source abbreviations in the notes are especially useful for readers comparing manuscript traditions. A variant can be marked as belonging to the Masoretic Text, Dead Sea Scrolls, Septuagint, Byzantine tradition, Textus Receptus, or one of the modern Greek critical editions. That does not turn the BSB into a full textual commentary, but it gives the reader more visibility into why some translations differ.

BSB and the Majority Standard Bible

The Berean Standard Bible should not be confused with the related Majority Standard Bible (MSB). Bible Hub describes the MSB as pairing the BSB Old Testament with a New Testament rooted in the Robinson-Pierpont Byzantine Majority Text, with notes identifying important variants from modern critical editions and the Textus Receptus. The BSB itself uses the broader source-comparison approach described above. Both texts were included in the April 2023 public-domain dedication, but they represent different New Testament textual projects.

Public Domain & Reuse

On April 30, 2023, the Berean Bible and Majority Bible texts were officially dedicated to the public domain. The project’s terms say simply that all uses are freely permitted. A suggested attribution is offered, but the project explicitly says it is appreciated rather than required. This makes the BSB unusually flexible among recent English Bible translations.

Optional attribution: “The Holy Bible, Berean Standard Bible (BSB), produced in cooperation with Bible Hub, Discovery Bible, OpenBible.com, and the Berean Bible Translation Committee. This text of God’s Word has been dedicated to the public domain.”

Why the Public-Domain Decision Matters

Most major contemporary English Bible translations are distributed under copyright licenses that regulate quotation, republication, software use, or large-scale reproduction. The BSB took a different route. By placing the text in the public domain, the committee removed ordinary licensing barriers for churches, missionaries, publishers, educators, programmers, and individual readers. A ministry can print substantial portions, a developer can build the text into an application, and a publisher can create a new edition without paying Bible-text royalties.

The decision also fits the project’s theological explanation of availability. Its official materials appeal to the early Christian circulation of apostolic letters and describe Scripture as something meant to be examined, copied, shared, and proclaimed. The legal structure of the modern BSB therefore reflects part of its stated ministry philosophy rather than functioning merely as a technical licensing choice.

Digital Distribution & Current Availability

The open-use policy is matched by unusually broad technical distribution. The official download page currently provides the BSB as Word documents, spreadsheets, plain text, ePub, USFM, USJ, USX, verse-by-verse files, and downloadable translation tables, along with multiple print-ready PDFs. Bible Hub also distributes the text online beside interlinear, literal, lexical, and parallel-translation tools.

As of 2026, the official site says the third physical printing has sold out, while its downloadable files continue to be identified as current and updated to that third-printing text. The project is also expanding into audio, software, other-language work, the Majority Standard Bible, updated literal resources, and additional interlinear projects.

Strengths & Responsible Use

The BSB’s chief strengths are its readable modern style, access to the translation process, broad documentation of textual sources, and unrestricted reuse. It is particularly useful for pastors, students, software developers, publishers, and ministries that want a current English translation without conventional licensing barriers.

Readers should still distinguish the BSB from its companion tiers. The BSB itself is the polished modern-English translation; the interlinear and literal forms are separate tools designed to expose more of the source-language structure. Used together, the tiers make the Berean project distinctive: the reader can move from ordinary English back toward lexical and textual evidence without leaving the same translation family.
