1 John

Read 1 John Now

Overview and Place in Scripture

First John is one of the most pastorally direct and theologically concentrated writings in the New Testament. Though traditionally called an epistle, it lacks the normal opening greeting and closing salutation of an ancient letter and reads more like a sermon, pastoral tract, or circular exhortation intended for a network of churches. Its language closely resembles the Gospel of John: light and darkness, truth and falsehood, abiding, eternal life, new birth, love, testimony, the Father and the Son, and the command to believe in Jesus Christ. Yet 1 John speaks from a moment of painful division. Some people have left the community, rival teachers make claims about Christ and spiritual knowledge, and the writer seeks to reassure those who remain that they truly possess eternal life.

Authorship, Date, and Johannine Setting

The work does not name its author. Christian tradition associated it with John the son of Zebedee, the apostle traditionally linked to the Fourth Gospel. Modern scholarship generally emphasizes the close relationship among the Gospel of John and the three Johannine letters while debating whether they came from one author, several authors within a Johannine circle, or successive stages of a shared community tradition. The vocabulary and theology are unmistakably related, but differences of genre and emphasis make precise reconstruction difficult. Many scholars place 1 John near the end of the first century or beginning of the second, in a setting where Christians formed by Johannine teaching were struggling over the meaning of Jesus’ identity and the ethical consequences of faith.

Attempts to label the opponents precisely should remain cautious. The letter says that “many antichrists” have appeared and that some former members “went out from us.” It condemns denial that Jesus is the Christ and insists that Jesus Christ has come “in the flesh.” These statements suggest a serious christological conflict, perhaps involving teachers who separated the divine Christ from the human Jesus or minimized the significance of his embodied life and death. Later theological categories such as “Docetism” can be useful comparisons, but 1 John does not provide enough information to identify the opponents neatly with a later named sect.

Literary Shape and Repeated Tests

First John is difficult to outline as a straight-line argument because it circles repeatedly around the same themes. The prologue begins with sensory language: what was heard, seen, looked upon, and handled concerning the Word of life is proclaimed so that readers may share fellowship with the apostolic witness, with the Father, and with the Son. The letter then revisits several interlocking tests of authentic Christian life: right confession of Jesus, obedience to God’s commands, love for fellow believers, rejection of hatred and worldliness, discernment of false spirits, and confidence in God’s gift of eternal life.

This cyclical style is part of the book’s pastoral force. John does not offer one abstract definition of Christianity. He returns to belief, love, righteousness, and assurance from different angles, showing that they belong together. The community is not told to choose between doctrine and ethics. Confessing Christ truthfully matters; so does walking as Christ walked. Love is not sentiment detached from truth; truth is not doctrinal precision detached from love.

God Is Light: Sin, Confession, and Advocacy

The first great theological declaration is that “God is light, and in him is no darkness at all.” Fellowship with God therefore cannot be reconciled with a settled life of darkness. Yet the letter refuses perfectionist denial of sin. “If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves.” Christians are instead called to confession, trusting God’s faithfulness and justice to forgive and cleanse. Jesus Christ is described as the righteous advocate with the Father and as the atoning sacrifice for sins. Different Christian traditions explain the language of atonement in distinct ways, but the immediate pastoral point is clear: acknowledgement of sin belongs with confidence in God’s provision through Christ.

The letter also contains strong language about those born of God not continuing in sin. Read beside its explicit admission that believers still sin, these statements are best understood as describing the characteristic direction of life rather than claiming that Christians attain sinless perfection. The contrast is between practicing righteousness and making peace with rebellion, not between ordinary believers and an imaginary class of people who never fail.

God Is Love

The best-known declaration of 1 John is that “God is love.” The letter defines that love christologically: God’s love is revealed in sending the Son so that people might live through him. Christian love therefore imitates divine self-giving. “We love him, because he first loved us.” Love for God cannot be separated from love for brothers and sisters, and the writer gives this teaching concrete economic force: if someone sees another believer in need and closes the heart, the claim to possess God’s love is contradicted by conduct. Love is to be enacted “in deed and in truth.”

This emphasis makes 1 John central to Christian ethics and spirituality. Its own standard for truth and love is the self-giving love revealed in Jesus.

Christology, Spirit, and Assurance

First John repeatedly anchors Christian identity in confession that Jesus is the Christ and the Son of God. The “spirits” are to be tested rather than trusted merely because they sound spiritual. The confession that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh becomes a criterion of discernment, protecting faith from forms of spirituality that detach salvation from the concrete incarnation. The Spirit also bears witness, and believers are described as having an “anointing,” language that has been interpreted in relation to the Holy Spirit and the community’s knowledge of apostolic truth.

Assurance is another major purpose. The letter explicitly states that it is written so believers may know that they have eternal life. This knowledge is not grounded in spiritual elitism. It rests in God’s testimony concerning the Son and is confirmed through faith, obedience, and love. The famous statement that “perfect love casteth out fear” does not deny reverence for God; it addresses fear of judgment by locating confidence in God’s completed and maturing love.

A Note on 1 John 5:7–8

First John also contains one of the best-known textual issues in the King James tradition. The longer Trinitarian wording often called the Comma Johanneum—the reference to the Father, Word, and Holy Ghost bearing witness in heaven—appears in some later manuscripts and entered the Textus Receptus tradition, but it is absent from the earliest and strongest Greek witnesses and from many ancient versions. Most modern critical editions therefore do not print it as part of the earliest recoverable text. The doctrine of the Trinity does not depend on this disputed wording; it rests on the broader biblical witness. The passage is a useful example of why modern Bibles sometimes differ and why textual notes can serve rather than threaten careful reading.

Why 1 John Matters

First John remains a profound guide to Christian assurance and discernment. It refuses a faith made only of claims. Authentic Christianity confesses the incarnate Son, walks in the light, acknowledges sin, receives forgiveness, obeys God, tests spiritual claims, loves concretely, and rests in the gift of eternal life. Its repeated contrasts—light and darkness, truth and lie, love and hatred, life and death—are severe because the author believes the stakes are real. Yet the center of the letter is not fear. It is divine initiative: God loved first, sent the Son, gave the Spirit, forgives confessed sin, and grants life. For readers unsettled by competing voices or uncertain of their standing before God, 1 John answers with a faith that is doctrinal, ethical, communal, and deeply anchored in love.

Annotated Bibliography (Chicago Style)

  • Brown, Raymond E. The Epistles of John. Anchor Bible 30. New York: Doubleday, 1982. A landmark commentary on authorship, community conflict, Christology, ethics, and the literary relationship of the Johannine letters to the Gospel.
  • Kruse, Colin G. The Letters of John. 2nd ed. Pillar New Testament Commentary. Grand Rapids: Eerdmans, 2020. A current exegetical treatment emphasizing Christology, assurance, love, atonement, the Spirit, and eternal life.
  • von Wahlde, Urban C. The Gospel and Letters of John, Volume 3: The Three Johannine Letters. Eerdmans Critical Commentary. Grand Rapids: Eerdmans, 2010. Detailed analysis of the composition, historical setting, and theology of 1–3 John.
  • Lieu, Judith M. I, II, & III John: A Commentary. New Testament Library. Louisville: Westminster John Knox, 2008. A major literary and theological study attentive to identity, boundaries, ethics, and the social world of the Johannine communities.
  • Bennett, Thomas Andrew. 1–3 John. Two Horizons New Testament Commentary. Grand Rapids: Eerdmans, 2021. Combines verse-by-verse exegesis with theological reflection on Christology, love, church, salvation, and eschatology.
  • Brown, Sherri, and Francis J. Moloney. Interpreting the Gospel and Letters of John: An Introduction. Grand Rapids: Eerdmans, 2017. An accessible guide to Johannine literature that situates the letters within their Jewish and early Christian contexts.
  • Yale Bible Study. “The Johannine Epistles.” Yale Divinity School. yalebiblestudy.org. Accessed August 28, 2026. Study material on 1–3 John, community division, love, sin, eschatology, and the letters’ relationship to the Fourth Gospel.
  • Metzger, Bruce M., and Bart D. Ehrman. The Text of the New Testament: Its Transmission, Corruption, and Restoration. 4th ed. New York: Oxford University Press, 2005. Essential background for the textual history of passages such as the Comma Johanneum in 1 John 5.
