Genesis

Overview and Place in Scripture

Genesis opens the Bible with an account of beginnings: creation, the fracture of human community, the spread of nations, and the calling of one family through whom God promises blessing to the earth. Its English title comes from the Greek genesis, “origin,” while the Hebrew title, Bereshit, comes from the opening words, “In the beginning.” As the first book of the Torah or Pentateuch, Genesis supplies the narrative foundation for what follows. Exodus assumes Jacob’s family has gone down to Egypt; later prophets appeal to Abraham, Jacob, Sodom, and creation; and the New Testament repeatedly returns to Adam, Abraham, Sarah, Isaac, Jacob, and Joseph. Genesis is therefore more than a collection of ancient stories. It is Scripture’s opening exploration of God, humanity, sin, covenant, promise, and blessing.

Literary Shape

Genesis falls into two broad movements. Chapters 1–11, often called the primeval history, move from creation to Eden, Cain and Abel, the flood, the covenant with Noah, and Babel. The scope is universal. Beginning in chapter 12, the focus narrows to Abraham and his descendants: Abraham and Sarah, Isaac and Rebekah, Jacob and his household, and Joseph and his brothers. A repeated formula often translated “these are the generations of” helps organize the book. The shift from all humanity to one chosen family is central to its theology. God’s purpose is not simply to favor Abraham privately, but to make his family a vehicle of blessing to “all the families of the earth.” The book ends in Egypt, preparing the crisis of Exodus.

Authorship, Tradition, and Composition

Jewish and Christian tradition has long associated the Torah with Moses, and later biblical writings speak of the “law of Moses.” Genesis itself, however, does not name an author. Modern scholarship generally understands the book as the result of a long process in which ancient stories, genealogies, family traditions, priestly interests, and theological reflection were transmitted and edited into the Pentateuch. Older scholarship commonly described major sources such as J, E, and P; current scholars debate their dates, extent, and relationships. Genesis contains repetitions and differing literary emphases, yet its final form is carefully shaped. A Christian reading need not treat literary history as a threat to inspiration: Scripture can be confessed as God’s Word while also being received through human memory, composition, editing, and transmission.

Creation, Humanity, and Sin

Genesis 1–3 establishes themes that echo throughout the Bible. Genesis 1 portrays God creating by sovereign speech and repeatedly declaring creation good. Humanity, male and female, is made in the “image of God” and entrusted with fruitful stewardship. Genesis 2 focuses more closely on the human creature, the garden, work, companionship, and command. Genesis 3 then describes disobedience, alienation, shame, and exile from Eden. The consequences spread quickly: Cain kills Abel, violence multiplies, and corruption precedes the flood. Yet judgment is repeatedly accompanied by mercy. Cain receives protection, Noah’s family is preserved, and after the flood God establishes a covenant with Noah and every living creature. Genesis presents sin as personal and social while insisting that divine judgment does not cancel God’s commitment to creation.

Covenant and the Patriarchs

With Abraham, Genesis’s covenantal pattern comes into focus. God calls Abram to leave his land and promises land, descendants, a great name, and worldwide blessing. Those promises are repeatedly threatened by barrenness, famine, family conflict, danger, and human failure. Abraham sometimes acts in remarkable faith and sometimes in fear. Sarah moves from infertility to the birth of Isaac. Hagar, an enslaved Egyptian woman drawn painfully into Abraham’s household, encounters God in the wilderness and receives promises concerning Ishmael. Genesis 15 dramatizes divine commitment to Abraham, and circumcision in Genesis 17 becomes the covenant sign. The near-sacrifice of Isaac in Genesis 22 is among Scripture’s most influential and difficult episodes. Across these stories, election is not presented as a reward for flawless heroes but as God’s persistent work through complicated people.

Jacob, Israel, and Joseph

The Jacob cycle develops the theme of contested blessing. Jacob obtains Esau’s birthright and receives Isaac’s blessing through deception, flees, encounters God at Bethel, and then experiences years of deception in Laban’s household. His family becomes the foundation of Israel’s tribes. On returning to Canaan, Jacob wrestles through the night with a mysterious figure and receives the name Israel. The narrative does not idealize his household: rivalry, favoritism, sexual violence, vengeance, and grief all appear. Yet reconciliation also appears, especially between Jacob and Esau. Genesis 37–50 then follows Joseph, sold by his brothers into slavery and taken to Egypt. He rises from imprisonment to authority under Pharaoh, preserves many during famine, and eventually reconciles with his brothers. His statement that they meant evil while God meant it for good expresses the book’s mature theology of providence without excusing wrongdoing.

Historical World and Ancient Context

Genesis reflects the ancient Near Eastern world. Creation and flood traditions have points of contact with Mesopotamian works such as Enuma Elish, Atrahasis, and the Epic of Gilgamesh, while differing significantly in theology and moral vision. Patriarchal stories assume customs involving kinship, inheritance, household servants, treaties, pastoral movement, and burial. Archaeology illuminates this setting but cannot simply prove individual patriarchal episodes. Earlier attempts to date Abraham precisely from particular customs have often proved less secure because many practices persisted for centuries. Responsible historical reading therefore distinguishes cultural plausibility from direct verification. Genesis is ancient theological narrative rooted in recognizable places and social worlds, but its purpose is larger than modern biography: it interprets origins, identity, and promise for the community that received it as Scripture.

Genesis in the Canon

Genesis has an immense history of Jewish and Christian interpretation. Jewish tradition continually returns to creation, the patriarchs and matriarchs, the binding of Isaac, Jacob, and Joseph in worship and commentary. Christian writers read Genesis in conversation with the gospel: Paul appeals to Abraham in Romans and Galatians and contrasts Adam and Christ in Romans and 1 Corinthians. Christian theology has also drawn on Genesis in discussing creation, sin, providence, sacrifice, election, marriage, and the image of God. These later readings should not erase the book’s Jewish identity. A responsible Christian approach first hears Genesis in its own literary world and then traces how later Scripture receives its language and themes.

Why Genesis Matters

Genesis asks foundational questions: Who created the world? What is humanity for? Why are human life and families marked by violence, estrangement, mortality, and longing? How can blessing emerge from people capable of betrayal and fear? What does it mean for God to bind himself by promise? Its answer is given through story. God creates, judges, preserves, calls, promises, accompanies, and works across generations. Familiar episodes become richer when read closely: the heroes are flawed, outsiders receive divine attention, women repeatedly move the story forward, and promises unfold through delay. Genesis establishes a biblical grammar of creation, fall, covenant, faith, providence, and blessing that continues to shape Jewish and Christian reading across the canon. Its closing image—Joseph’s coffin in Egypt—also leaves the promise unfinished and draws readers forward toward the deliverance narrated in Exodus.

Annotated Bibliography (Chicago Style)

  • Sarna, Nahum M. Genesis: The Traditional Hebrew Text with New JPS Translation. JPS Torah Commentary. Philadelphia: Jewish Publication Society, 1989. A major Jewish commentary attentive to Hebrew language, ancient Near Eastern context, literary structure, and the history of Jewish interpretation.
  • Wenham, Gordon J. Genesis 1–15. Word Biblical Commentary 1. Waco, TX: Word Books, 1987; and Genesis 16–50. Word Biblical Commentary 2. Dallas: Word Books, 1994. Detailed exegetical volumes combining literary analysis, source-critical discussion, theology, and engagement with comparative ancient material.
  • Hamilton, Victor P. The Book of Genesis, Chapters 1–17. New International Commentary on the Old Testament. Grand Rapids: Eerdmans, 1990; and The Book of Genesis, Chapters 18–50. Grand Rapids: Eerdmans, 1995. Substantial evangelical commentaries with close attention to Hebrew grammar, narrative detail, theology, and canonical connections.
  • Alter, Robert. Genesis: Translation and Commentary. New York: W. W. Norton, 1996. A literary translation and commentary especially valuable for narrative art, repetition, wordplay, characterization, and the stylistic qualities of biblical Hebrew.
  • Baden, Joel S. The Composition of the Pentateuch: Renewing the Documentary Hypothesis. New Haven: Yale University Press, 2012. A major modern treatment of Pentateuchal composition and source criticism, useful for understanding scholarly debates behind Genesis’s literary formation.
  • Collins, John J. Introduction to the Hebrew Bible. 3rd ed. Minneapolis: Fortress Press, 2018. A widely used academic introduction that situates Genesis within the Torah, ancient Near Eastern literature, Israelite religion, and modern critical scholarship.
  • Yale Bible Study. “Genesis.” Yale Divinity School. yalebiblestudy.org/courses/genesis/. Accessed August 28, 2026. Free scholarly lectures and study guides addressing creation, patriarchal narratives, literary formation, historical questions, and theological themes.
  • The Jewish Publication Society. “The JPS Torah Commentary: Genesis.” jps.org/books/jps-torah-commentary-genesis/. Accessed August 28, 2026. Publisher overview of Sarna’s commentary and the JPS Torah Commentary method, including Hebrew text, Masoretic notes, classical sources, and modern scholarship.
