Bible Study: Exodus 20:15

Exodus 20:15 says simply, “Thou shalt not steal.” The brevity is striking. No object is specified, which gives the command broad reach. In the ancient world, theft could involve property, livestock, money, land, or even persons through kidnapping. Some scholars have proposed that the command originally focused especially on stealing persons, but in its canonical form it functions as a general prohibition against taking what rightly belongs to another.

The command belongs to a covenant given to people who have just been freed from slavery. That background matters. Israel has experienced what it means to have labor, bodies, and freedom controlled by others. A liberated community is now commanded not to build its life around taking from neighbors.

The prohibition assumes that possessions and boundaries can carry moral significance. Biblical faith does not teach that material goods are unimportant. Food, shelter, tools, land, wages, and resources can be essential to human flourishing. Theft violates trust and can place real burdens on those who lose what they need.

Yet the biblical treatment of property is never absolute. The same Torah that forbids theft also commands care for the poor, debt relief, gleaning, fair wages, and limits on exploitation. Private possession exists within responsibility to God and neighbor. The command against stealing therefore cannot be used to defend greed while ignoring systems that deprive workers or vulnerable people of what justice requires.

This broader context expands modern application. Theft includes obvious acts such as burglary, shoplifting, fraud, and embezzlement. It can also include wage theft, deceptive contracts, corruption, plagiarism, identity theft, and the misuse of resources entrusted for another purpose. The form changes, but the moral issue remains taking or withholding what is not rightly ours.

Employers and institutions can steal as well as individuals. Scripture repeatedly condemns withholding wages and exploiting vulnerable workers. A company that manipulates records to avoid paying employees what they earned violates the same basic principle of justice even if the act looks more sophisticated than ordinary theft.

The command also challenges small acts of dishonesty people may excuse because “everyone does it.” Taking office resources for private use, falsifying expenses, knowingly keeping money given by mistake, or using someone else’s work without credit can reveal the same underlying assumption: another person’s property or labor matters less than my convenience.

Christian interpretation moves beyond prohibition toward generosity. Ephesians 4:28 tells the thief not merely to stop stealing but to work honestly “that he may have to give to him that needeth.” The transformation is remarkable: the person who once took becomes a person who contributes. Grace does not simply restrain wrongful acquisition; it produces open-handed stewardship.

The same principle applies to public resources. Corruption steals not only from an abstract institution but from the people whose taxes, services, pensions, schools, roads, or assistance depend on those funds. Biblical justice therefore treats stewardship of shared resources as a neighbor-love issue.

This does not mean Christians must give away everything without wisdom. Scripture recognizes household responsibility, planning, and stewardship. The point is that possessions are gifts to manage rather than idols to guard at all costs. People who trust God can respect others’ property while also using their own resources for good.

For discipleship, Exodus 20:15 asks believers to examine how they acquire, use, and account for resources. Are wages fair? Are debts and obligations handled honestly? Is intellectual work credited? Are vulnerable people being exploited? Are personal desires being financed by taking what belongs to someone else?

The command protects trust within community. When people can rely on neighbors, institutions, and leaders not to take what is theirs, social life becomes safer and more humane. “Thou shalt not steal” therefore belongs to a larger biblical vision in which justice, honest work, generosity, and stewardship replace exploitation. The God who delivered Israel from a house of bondage calls His people to build relationships where people are not treated as sources of wealth to be used.

Christian stewardship also includes returning what has been taken when possible. Repentance may require restitution, corrected records, repayment, or acknowledgment of another person’s work. Grace forgives, but grace also teaches the former thief to become trustworthy.
