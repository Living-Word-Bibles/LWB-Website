Bible Study: Titus 3:5

Titus 3:5 says, “Not by works of righteousness which we have done, but according to his mercy he saved us, by the washing of regeneration, and renewing of the Holy Ghost.” The verse is one of the New Testament’s clearest statements that salvation originates in divine mercy rather than human achievement. Yet the surrounding chapter also shows that grace is meant to produce a transformed way of life.

Titus 3 begins with practical instruction. Believers are to respect authorities, avoid slander and quarrels, show gentleness, and demonstrate consideration toward everyone. The theological explanation begins in verse 3: Christians themselves were once foolish, disobedient, enslaved to destructive desires, living in malice, envy, and hatred.

Verse 4 then introduces the turning point: “But after that the kindness and love of God our Saviour toward man appeared.” Salvation is grounded in God’s kindness, love of humanity, and mercy.

Working Preacher emphasizes the sharp contrast in verse 5. Salvation comes “not because of any works of righteousness that we had done” but because of God’s own mercy. Human moral effort does not force or purchase divine favor.

This is important because religious people can turn even good works into a platform for superiority. If salvation were earned by outperforming others, pride and contempt could simply become new forms of the old destructive life described in verse 3.

The phrase “he saved us” places initiative with God. The saving action is completed enough to be spoken of in the past tense, yet the surrounding verses still look toward the future hope of eternal life.

“The washing of regeneration” uses language strongly associated by many interpreters with baptism. Working Preacher notes that the Greek word for “washing” can refer to a bath and that early Christian readers naturally heard baptismal resonance here.

“Regeneration” or rebirth refers to new beginning. The old life marked by envy, hatred, and enslavement is not treated as the believer’s final identity.

The phrase “renewing of the Holy Ghost” adds continuing divine action. Salvation is not merely cancellation of guilt; the Spirit renews human life so that conduct can begin to resemble the mercy that saved it.

Working Preacher also connects this renewal with the wider biblical hope of new creation. The same Spirit associated with God’s renewing work in the world is poured out richly through Jesus Christ.

The verse should therefore not be used to create an opposition between grace and good works as though Christians should avoid moral effort. Titus immediately says believers should devote themselves to good works because they are good and profitable for others.

The order is the key. Good works are fruit, not purchase price. Mercy saves; renewed people then learn to act mercifully.

This distinction protects both humility and responsibility. Christians cannot boast that their conduct earned salvation, but neither can they claim grace while remaining indifferent to how they treat others.

The context of Titus is especially practical. The letter repeatedly emphasizes self-control, good conduct, household relationships, public witness, and deeds that benefit neighbors.

Verse 5 can therefore speak strongly against religious hypocrisy. A community that celebrates grace while practicing cruelty, slander, quarrels, or exploitation contradicts the renewal the verse describes.

The verse also offers hope to people burdened by the past. Working Preacher stresses that rebirth and renewal mean the past does not have to determine the present or future. God’s mercy opens the possibility of real change.

That change may be gradual. The text does not promise that every destructive habit disappears instantly. Renewal can involve repentance, accountability, spiritual discipline, counseling, community, and repeated dependence on the Spirit.

The baptismal language also makes salvation communal. Baptism is not merely private inspiration but incorporation into a people whose life should increasingly display God’s kindness and mercy.

The sequence from mercy to renewal also gives Christian ethics its proper emotional center. Good works become grateful participation in God’s new life rather than anxious attempts to accumulate enough righteousness to be accepted.

For discipleship, Titus 3:5 invites two confessions held together: “I did not save myself” and “the Spirit is renewing me.” The first destroys pride. The second destroys fatalism.

Titus 3:5 therefore gives a compact theology of salvation: not earned righteousness, but divine mercy; not merely pardon, but rebirth; not merely a new label, but renewal through the Holy Spirit. Grace begins the new life, and the Spirit continues shaping that life so that the kindness of God becomes visible in the people God has saved.
