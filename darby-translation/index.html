The Holy Bible: Darby Translation (DBY)

The Holy Scriptures: A New Translation from the Original Languages by J. N. Darby

Read Now →

Overview. The Darby Translation is a highly literal English Bible associated with John Nelson Darby (1800–1882), one of the most influential early leaders of the Plymouth Brethren. Darby’s English New Testament was issued in parts beginning in 1865 and completed in 1867; revised editions followed in the early 1870s and, from his corrected copy, posthumously in 1884. After his death, associates prepared an English Old Testament by collating the common Hebrew text with Darby’s French and German translations. The first complete English New Translation appeared from G. Morrish in 1890.

Rights: Public Domain (U.S.). The historic text may be quoted and reprinted freely.

John Nelson Darby and the Brethren Background

John Nelson Darby was born in 1800, studied at Trinity College Dublin, was called to the Irish bar, and later entered ordained ministry in the established church in Ireland. By the late 1820s he had separated from that ecclesiastical structure and joined Christians meeting outside ordinary denominational lines. These gatherings became associated with the movement later known as the Plymouth Brethren. Darby became a major teacher, traveler, organizer, and controversialist within the movement and spent much of his life preaching and writing across Britain and continental Europe.

His Bible translation grew out of this wider ministry. Darby did not initially believe English Christianity lacked a usable Bible; he regarded the King James Version as suitable for normal public use and commonly used it in his own oral ministry. His concern was narrower and more scholarly: readers without Hebrew or Greek needed a translation that brought them closer to the grammatical and textual details he encountered in the original languages.

A Study Bible Rather Than a Public-Reading Bible

Darby stated this purpose directly in the revised preface to his New Testament. He did not want primarily to produce a literary or ecclesiastical Bible for public reading. He wanted to place before an English reader, as closely as he could, the results of his own study of Greek manuscripts, critical editions, translations, grammars, and commentaries. The goal was therefore study and private use, especially for Christians who valued the original text but could not read it themselves.

That explains the characteristic texture of the DBY. It often preserves distinctions that smoother translations absorb into natural English. Sentence structure can be stiff, repeated Greek or Hebrew terms may be rendered consistently, and explanatory brackets or notes are used where the English requires a supplied word or where manuscripts differ. Darby accepted a certain roughness because transparency mattered more to him than rhetorical polish.

The English New Testament: 1865–1884

Darby first published portions of his English New Testament separately, beginning with Matthew in 1865. The complete New Testament appeared in 1867 under G. Morrish. A revised second edition followed in 1871/1872; bibliographies vary because the revised preface is dated 1871 while surviving edition records commonly identify the publication as 1872. A third edition appeared in 1884, two years after Darby’s death.

The introductory notice to the 1884 edition states that it was printed from a corrected copy of the second edition that Darby had completed before his death and then revised from his own notes while it was going through the press. The wording changed relatively little, but selected readings formerly confined to notes were introduced into the text and the textual apparatus was expanded.

New Testament Textual Criticism

Darby’s New Testament is not a simple reworking of the King James Version or a translation of the Textus Receptus alone. He worked in the world of nineteenth-century textual criticism and consulted the major editors available to him, naming scholars such as Griesbach, Lachmann, Scholz, and Tischendorf. His notes frequently discuss manuscript evidence and alternative readings.

This gave the DBY an unusually visible text-critical character for an English Bible of its period. Darby did not merely choose a reading and hide the alternatives. The notes often allowed readers to see where evidence was divided. He could be critical of the theories of leading textual critics and did not believe age or numerical manuscript support alone settled every question. His aim was to evaluate evidence carefully while keeping the reader informed.

The Old Testament and the 1890 Complete Bible

Darby died in 1882 before a complete English Old Testament bearing his name had been published. The 1890 introductory notice explains how associates completed the task. They worked from the common Hebrew text and collated Darby’s mature French and German translations, noting that he had personally revised the first few English books shortly before his death.

The editors regarded Darby’s French Old Testament in particular as preserving his mature judgment on Hebrew passages and sought to transfer the value of that work into English. They also retained familiar Authorized Version vocabulary where it served exactness, while accepting that close adherence to the French or German could sometimes produce noticeable roughness.

The complete English Bible was published by G. Morrish in 1890 as The Holy Scriptures: A New Translation from the Original Languages by J. N. Darby. A later 1939 edition condensed portions of the notes, and the 1961 edition restored and checked much of the explanatory material. Its introductory notice explicitly says that the 1961 text reprints the wording of the 1890 complete edition.

Divine Names and “Jehovah”

One of the most recognizable Old Testament features is the treatment of the divine names. The editors wanted English readers to distinguish forms that the Authorized Version often represented simply as LORD, GOD, or “Lord.” The DBY therefore uses “Jehovah” for the Tetragrammaton and “Jah” where that shorter Hebrew form occurs, while normally rendering Adonai as “Lord.”

“Jehovah” reflects the conventional English form inherited from earlier Christian scholarship rather than the pronunciation “Yahweh” favored by most modern scholars. Historically, however, Darby’s usage serves an important translation purpose: it allows readers to see immediately when the underlying text uses the personal divine name rather than a title.

Brackets, Notes, and Linguistic Transparency

The DBY’s apparatus is integral to the translation. In later editions, square brackets can indicate words supplied to complete the English sense or places where original manuscripts vary. Footnotes identify literal renderings, alternative translations, Septuagint evidence, Masoretic reading traditions, and distinctions among Hebrew and Greek words. The 1961 editors preserved transliterated Hebrew and Greek terms and explained abbreviations such as “LXX,” “Lit.,” and “Cf.”

This apparatus makes the Darby Bible especially useful for comparative study. It is less effective for readers seeking uninterrupted literary flow, but that was never its primary purpose. Darby wanted the non-specialist to have access to information normally confined to scholarly tools.

French and German Translation Work

Darby’s English Bible cannot be separated from his multilingual work. During his ministry on the European continent he collaborated with French- and German-speaking Christians on new Bible translations. In Germany he participated in the work that became the highly literal Elberfelder Bible; in French he produced a translation often associated with his mature exegetical judgments.

These projects cross-pollinated. Darby revised readings across languages, and the posthumous English Old Testament drew consciously on his French and German versions. The complete English DBY is therefore not merely an English-language project but the final English expression of a broader international translation effort.

Translation Character: Strengths and Trade-Offs

  • Formal equivalence: DBY stays unusually close to Hebrew and Greek structure, making it valuable for close comparison.
  • Text-critical visibility: the notes expose manuscript variants and editorial decisions rather than concealing them.
  • Lexical distinctions: Darby often preserves differences among related terms and divine names.
  • Study orientation: the translation was deliberately designed for private study rather than polished public reading.
  • Trade-off: its literalness can result in awkward, archaic, or foreign-sounding English and should not be mistaken for a word-for-word substitute for knowing the original languages.

Influence and Legacy

The Darby Translation never became a dominant English church Bible, but its influence exceeds its circulation. Bible historian Michael Marlowe notes that the English Revised Version translators consulted Darby’s New Testament, evidence that his work was taken seriously within nineteenth-century revision scholarship. Within Brethren circles, the DBY became a durable study resource and remains associated with Darby’s extensive biblical exposition.

Its larger historical importance lies in the model it represents: a translation openly designed to give ordinary readers access to textual criticism and linguistic detail. In that respect it anticipates features now familiar in study Bibles, translators’ notes, interlinear tools, and digital comparison platforms.

Public-Domain Status and Modern Use

The historic Darby text is in the public domain in the United States. BibleGateway therefore permits quotation and reprinting of its hosted DBY text, and public-domain editions circulate widely online. Modern publishers may add copyrighted formatting or editorial matter, but the underlying historic translation itself is freely reusable in the United States.

For modern Christians, DBY works best as Darby intended: beside another Bible rather than necessarily in place of one. Comparing its wording with the KJV, ASV, ESV, NET, or another modern translation can highlight grammatical and textual questions worth investigating. Its value lies not in being smoother than other English Bibles, but in making the interpretive work of translation unusually visible.

Concise Timeline

  • 1800 — John Nelson Darby is born.
  • 1865 — English New Testament begins appearing in separate parts.
  • 1867 — Complete English New Testament published by G. Morrish.
  • 1871/1872 — Revised second edition of the English New Testament.
  • 1882 — Darby dies.
  • 1884 — Third New Testament edition issued posthumously from Darby’s corrected copy and notes.
  • 1890 — First complete English DBY published, combining the New Testament with the posthumously prepared Old Testament.
  • 1939 — Stow Hill edition appears with condensed notes.
  • 1961 — Edition reprints the 1890 wording while critically reviewing and expanding the note apparatus.
