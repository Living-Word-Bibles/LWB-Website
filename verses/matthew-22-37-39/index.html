Bible Study: Matthew 22:37-39

Matthew 22:37–39 records Jesus’ answer to a question about the greatest commandment: love God with all heart, soul, and mind, and “love thy neighbour as thyself.” The wording is familiar, but Matthew’s setting gives the exchange a sharper edge than Mark’s parallel. Here a lawyer asks the question specifically to test Jesus during a series of Jerusalem controversies.

Jesus’ answer begins with Deuteronomy 6:5, part of the Shema tradition at the heart of Jewish confession. Love of God is rooted in Israel’s covenant faith and in the declaration that the Lord alone is God.

“With all thy heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy mind” expresses comprehensive devotion. The terms overlap rather than dividing human nature into isolated parts. Love of God involves desire, identity, thought, loyalty, and action.

Matthew’s wording emphasizes “mind,” reminding Christian readers that intellectual life can belong to worship. Thinking carefully, studying Scripture, examining evidence, and seeking wisdom need not compete with faith.

Jesus identifies this as “the first and great commandment,” but He immediately adds a second from Leviticus 19:18: “Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself.” Working Preacher emphasizes that Jesus refuses to compress religion into one vertical goal while neglecting human relationships.

The two commands are “like” one another because love of God and love of neighbor mutually interpret faithful life. A person cannot use devotion to God as an excuse for mistreating people created by God.

The Old Testament context of neighbor-love makes the command concrete. Leviticus 19 discusses honesty, justice, care for the vulnerable, fair judgment, wages, gleaning, speech, and vengeance. Love is therefore not merely affection.

Matthew’s version ends the teaching with verse 40: “On these two commandments hang all the law and the prophets.” Jesus does not say the Law and Prophets are abolished. He says their faithful interpretation depends upon the love these commandments express.

This is especially important in Matthew, a Gospel that strongly values Jesus as interpreter of Torah. In the Sermon on the Mount Jesus insists He has not come to destroy the law and prophets but to fulfill them. Matthew 22 continues that interpretive mission.

Christians should therefore avoid presenting the passage as though Judaism consisted of loveless legalism until Jesus introduced compassion. Jesus is citing Jewish Scripture to answer a Jewish legal question. The great commandments arise from Torah itself.

“Love thy neighbour as thyself” does not require identical treatment in every situation. Love seeks genuine good. A child, stranger, friend, victim, employee, and dangerous aggressor may need different responses.

Neighbor-love also does not forbid boundaries or justice. Jesus’ broader teaching includes accountability and truth. Protecting a vulnerable person from harm can be a form of neighbor-love toward both the victim and the wrongdoer, because love does not require allowing sin to continue unchecked.

The command to love oneself is not stated independently, but the analogy assumes that normal concern for one’s own life can become a model for concern for others. Christianity need not demand self-contempt in order to be sacrificial.

The testing context also shows Jesus refusing a false choice. The lawyer asks for the greatest commandment, but Jesus provides a second command that cannot be ignored. Religious debates often ask people to choose between devotion and ethics, truth and love, worship and justice. Jesus binds them together.

For discipleship, Matthew 22:37–39 provides a framework for evaluating Christian life. Does worship lead toward neighbor-love? Does theology produce honesty and mercy? Does love for people remain rooted in God rather than merely in convenience?

The commands also challenge selective love. It is easy to love neighbors who share beliefs, class, ethnicity, politics, or preferences. Jesus’ wider teaching in Matthew includes enemy-love, pushing disciples beyond comfortable circles.

For churches, the passage becomes a test of priorities. Programs, traditions, budgets, doctrinal controversies, and institutional survival should all be evaluated by whether they serve love of God and neighbor.

Matthew 22:37–39 therefore does not simplify faith into sentiment. It concentrates the Law and Prophets into demanding covenantal love: the whole person directed toward God and active concern directed toward other people. According to Jesus, everything else must hang from those two commitments rather than replacing them.

The command’s totality also means devotion cannot be compartmentalized. Worship, economics, sexuality, speech, politics, work, and relationships all fall within love of God and neighbor.

The pairing also prevents a false separation between devotion and public ethics. Love of God that never changes how power, money, speech, or neighbor-relations are handled has not yet become the integrated obedience Jesus describes.

Bibliography & Further Reading

  • The Holy Bible, King James Version — Matthew 22:34–40. Primary context of the lawyer’s test and Jesus’ two great commandments.
  • Working Preacher, “Commentary on Matthew 22:34–46”. Explores the complementary relationship between love of God and love of neighbor.
  • Yale Bible Study, “The Gospel of Matthew”. Academic overview of Matthew’s Jewish scriptural setting, Torah interpretation, discipleship, and community ethics.
  • W. D. Davies and Dale C. Allison Jr., Matthew 19–28, ICC (T&T Clark, 1997). Major critical commentary on Matthew’s Jerusalem controversies and the great commandments.
  • Ulrich Luz, Matthew 21–28, Hermeneia (Fortress, 2005). Historical and theological commentary on Matthew 22, Torah, love, and conflict.
