Bible Study: Exodus 20:17

Exodus 20:17 is unique among the Ten Commandments because it moves explicitly into the realm of desire: “Thou shalt not covet thy neighbour’s house … wife … servant … ox … ass, nor any thing that is thy neighbour’s.” Earlier commands prohibit murder, adultery, theft, and false witness. The final command reaches beneath outward acts and asks what happens in the heart before those acts occur.

To covet is more than noticing or admiring something. The Hebrew term describes a desire that becomes possessive—wanting what belongs to another in a way that threatens the neighbor’s good. Desire itself is part of human life, but desire can become disordered when another person’s possessions, relationships, status, or opportunities become objects we believe we must obtain.

The biblical narratives provide vivid examples. David sees Bathsheba, desires her, and eventually abuses royal power in a chain of adultery, deception, and killing. Ahab wants Naboth’s vineyard; Jezebel then uses false witnesses to secure the property through Naboth’s death. In both stories, outward violations grow from a prior refusal to accept a boundary around what belongs to another.

This explains why the command against coveting belongs at the end of the Decalogue. It functions almost like an interior commentary on several earlier commandments. The person who learns contentment is less likely to steal, commit adultery, lie to obtain an advantage, or harm another person in pursuit of something desired.

The ancient wording reflects a patriarchal household in which a wife, servants, animals, and property appear in the same list. Modern readers should acknowledge that social reality rather than pretend the list reflects contemporary ideas of equality. The wider biblical canon nevertheless affirms the dignity of women and servants beyond property categories, and Christian interpretation should not reproduce the ownership assumptions of the ancient household.

Coveting is especially relevant in consumer cultures built on comparison. Advertising often works by teaching people that their current lives are inadequate until they possess something someone else has. Social media intensifies the effect by displaying edited versions of other people’s homes, bodies, travel, careers, families, and achievements. Desire can be manufactured faster than gratitude can respond.

The command does not forbid ambition, planning, or the desire to improve one’s circumstances. Wanting meaningful work, adequate housing, healthy relationships, or financial stability is not automatically coveting. The ethical question is whether the desire depends on taking, resenting, or diminishing what belongs to another. Contentment and aspiration can coexist when another person’s good is not treated as a threat.

Paul later writes that covetousness can become a form of idolatry. That connection is significant. Coveting tells the heart that life will finally be secure if it possesses a particular object, relationship, or status. The desired thing begins to promise what only God can ultimately give: identity, worth, security, and meaning.

Christian contentment therefore is not resignation. It is freedom from the belief that another person’s life must become mine before I can be whole. Gratitude trains attention toward gifts already received, while generosity loosens the grip of possession. Prayer helps expose the difference between a legitimate desire and an envy that is beginning to control behavior.

Contentment also makes generosity possible. When people stop interpreting another person’s good as evidence of their own deprivation, resources can be shared more freely. Gratitude loosens the grip of comparison and creates space to celebrate a neighbor rather than compete with them.

The command also protects the neighbor. Coveting often objectifies people. A neighbor becomes an obstacle, a competitor, or a source from which something can be taken. Love reverses that orientation. Instead of asking, “How can I get what they have?” love asks, “How can I seek their good?”

For discipleship, Exodus 20:17 invites examination before action. What desires are growing? What comparison is producing resentment? What possession or relationship has begun to seem essential to identity? The command does not ask Christians to stop wanting anything. It asks them to bring desire under love of God and neighbor. Contentment becomes a form of freedom: the ability to rejoice in another person’s good without needing to possess it.

The final command also reveals why spiritual formation cannot stop at outward compliance. A person may avoid theft while living in envy, or avoid adultery while continually desiring another’s spouse. God’s covenant reaches the inner life from which behavior grows.

Bibliography & Further Reading

  • The Holy Bible, King James Version — Exodus 20:17. Primary text of the command against coveting.
  • Yale Bible Study, “Exodus → Sinai — Study Guide”. Academic discussion of the final commandment and its relationship to adultery, theft, property, and desire.
  • Working Preacher, “Commentary on Exodus 20:17”. Explores coveting as disordered desire that can generate theft, adultery, false witness, and violence.
  • Patrick D. Miller, The Ten Commandments (Westminster John Knox, 2009). Theological study of the Decalogue and the interior moral dimension of coveting.
  • Nahum M. Sarna, Exodus, JPS Torah Commentary (Jewish Publication Society, 1991). Major Jewish commentary on Exodus 20 and the moral logic of the Decalogue.
