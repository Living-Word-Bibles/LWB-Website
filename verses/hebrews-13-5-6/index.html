Bible Study: Hebrews 13:5-6

Hebrews 13:5–6 joins contentment with confidence in God’s presence: “Let your conversation be without covetousness; and be content with such things as ye have: for he hath said, I will never leave thee, nor forsake thee.” The next verse answers fear with a quotation from the Psalms: “The Lord is my helper, and I will not fear what man shall do unto me.” These statements belong to the final ethical exhortations of Hebrews and are addressed to a community that has experienced pressure, loss, and social vulnerability.

The KJV word “conversation” here means conduct or manner of life, not merely speech. The writer warns against a life shaped by “the love of money.” The Greek wording is closely related to the same concern found elsewhere in the New Testament: possessions can become a false source of security. Contentment is offered as an alternative.

Contentment does not mean poverty is inherently virtuous or that people should accept exploitation. Hebrews 13 also commands hospitality, care for prisoners, solidarity with those mistreated, and generosity. Christian contentment is not indifference to material need. It is freedom from making possessions the foundation of identity and safety.

The reason given is striking: “for he hath said, I will never leave thee, nor forsake thee.” The writer draws language from Israel’s Scriptures, especially promises associated with Deuteronomy and Joshua. The logic is not “you do not need anything.” It is “you are not abandoned.” God’s presence becomes the deepest ground for security.

This is important because the original audience appears to have suffered material loss. Earlier, Hebrews recalls that believers accepted the plundering of their possessions because they knew they had a better and enduring possession. The final warning about money is therefore not addressed to people unfamiliar with loss. Contentment is taught in a context where possessions have already proved unstable.

Verse 6 quotes Psalm 118: “The Lord is my helper, and I will not fear what man shall do unto me.” Again, this does not mean people cannot cause real harm. Hebrews speaks openly about persecution, imprisonment, violence, and death. The confidence is that human power is not ultimate. God remains helper even when circumstances are threatening.

The statement “I will not fear” should not be used to shame people who experience anxiety. Biblical courage does not require the absence of emotion. The Psalms themselves contain fear, lament, and pleading. The point is that fear does not become the final authority over faithfulness. The believer can feel threatened and still confess that God is helper.

The verse also connects money and fear more closely than modern readers sometimes notice. People often pursue wealth because they fear insecurity, dependence, aging, loss, or lack of control. Hebrews does not condemn prudent planning, saving, work, or provision for family. It challenges the moment when money becomes the answer to the question, “What will keep me safe if everything else fails?”

The surrounding ethics keep contentment communal. Believers are to love one another, welcome strangers, remember prisoners, honor marriage, resist greed, remember leaders, and offer praise and generosity. Contentment is not withdrawal from responsibility; it releases resources and attention for the good of others.

For discipleship, Hebrews 13:5–6 invites Christians to examine both desire and fear. What possessions have become necessary for a sense of worth? What fear drives the need for more? What would it mean to trust that God’s presence is not measured by financial abundance?

The promise “I will never leave thee” does not guarantee that income will never change, jobs will never be lost, or people will never suffer. It gives something deeper: God’s faithfulness does not fluctuate with material circumstances. Christian contentment grows from that presence. Because the Lord is helper, believers can hold possessions gratefully rather than desperately and face human threats without surrendering ultimate trust.

The promise of presence also creates generosity. When possessions are not treated as the final source of security, they can be shared more freely. Contentment is therefore not a closed fist around what one has; it is the freedom to receive gratefully and give responsibly because God remains the deeper source of help.

There is also a strong connection between contentment and memory. The promise “I will never leave thee” comes from Israel’s scriptural tradition, where God’s presence sustains people at moments of transition and uncertainty. Hebrews teaches its readers to borrow courage from that remembered history. Contentment grows when the believer remembers that God’s faithfulness has outlasted changing leaders, changing lands, and changing circumstances before. The spiritual alternative to greed is therefore not merely owning less; it is remembering more clearly where lasting help comes from.
