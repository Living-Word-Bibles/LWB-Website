Daniel

Overview and Place in Scripture

Daniel combines court tales about faithful Jews living under foreign empires with symbolic visions of kingdoms, persecution, heavenly conflict, judgment, and resurrection. Chapters 1–6 tell stories such as the fiery furnace, Nebuchadnezzar’s dreams, the writing on the wall, and Daniel in the lions’ den. Chapters 7–12 shift into apocalyptic visions filled with beasts, horns, angels, numbers, and coded historical sequences. The book is also linguistically unusual: it begins in Hebrew, moves into Aramaic from 2:4b through chapter 7, and returns to Hebrew for chapters 8–12. In the Hebrew Bible Daniel belongs to the Writings rather than the Prophets; Christian Bibles place it among the Major Prophets. Catholic and Orthodox traditions also receive additional Greek material associated with Daniel. The book’s central concern is how God’s people can remain faithful when imperial powers dominate public life and when history appears controlled by violent rulers.

Daniel as Character and the Question of Date

The stories present Daniel as a Judean exile taken to Babylon in the sixth century BCE who serves rulers while refusing compromise in matters of ultimate loyalty. Traditional Jewish and Christian interpretation often treated Daniel as the author or central prophetic source of the whole book. Modern scholarship commonly dates the final form of chapters 7–12 to the second century BCE, especially the crisis under the Seleucid ruler Antiochus IV Epiphanes, because the visions describe the sequence of Hellenistic kingdoms with increasing specificity up to that period. The stories in chapters 1–6 may preserve older diaspora traditions that were collected and adapted into the final work. The book therefore uses a revered exile figure to address a later generation facing persecution. This literary strategy is characteristic of apocalyptic writing, where revelation is placed in an earlier setting and “unsealed” for readers in a present crisis.

Faithfulness in the Imperial Court

The opening stories explore life inside empire without requiring either total assimilation or total withdrawal. Daniel and his companions learn Babylonian language and literature, receive foreign names, and serve in the royal administration, yet they draw boundaries around worship and covenant identity. The food test in chapter 1 introduces this pattern. Daniel can cooperate professionally while refusing what he understands as defilement. The same tension intensifies in chapter 3 when Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego refuse the golden image and in chapter 6 when Daniel continues praying despite a royal decree. These narratives have long strengthened minority religious communities because faithfulness is portrayed as intelligent, disciplined, and courageous rather than merely oppositional. The stories also satirize imperial pretensions. Kings demand impossible interpretations, construct monuments, issue absurd decrees, and discover that their power has limits. God can grant wisdom within the court without surrendering sovereignty to the court.

Nebuchadnezzar, Pride, and the Limits of Empire

Nebuchadnezzar dominates the first half of Daniel as both terrifying monarch and object lesson in pride. His dream of the statue in chapter 2 presents successive kingdoms as temporary before God establishes a kingdom that will not be destroyed. Chapter 4 tells of a cosmic tree cut down until the king learns that “the Most High rules the kingdom of mortals.” Whether read historically, theologically, or as court satire, the story attacks the illusion that imperial achievement makes a ruler divine or self-sufficient. Chapter 5 repeats the theme with Belshazzar, whose feast profanes temple vessels before the handwriting announces that his kingdom has been weighed and found wanting. The lesson is not that every political regime can be mapped confidently onto Daniel’s symbols by modern interpreters. It is that empires claiming permanence are inherently temporary. Human sovereignty is derivative, morally accountable, and ultimately subject to divine judgment.

Apocalyptic Vision and Symbolic History

Daniel 7 retells the logic of chapter 2 through four monstrous beasts rising from the sea. Instead of glorious metals, empire is now pictured as predatory animal power. The Ancient of Days sits in judgment, and “one like a son of man” receives dominion. In its immediate literary setting, this humanlike figure is closely related to the “holy ones of the Most High,” representing the vindication of God’s persecuted people, though later Jewish and Christian traditions also interpreted the figure messianically. Subsequent visions employ a ram, goat, horns, and angelic interpreters to depict conflicts involving Medo-Persian and Greek power. Apocalyptic symbolism allows oppressed communities to see political events from a heavenly perspective. The bizarre imagery is not a puzzle designed mainly to predict twenty-first-century headlines. It unveils the theological character of empire, persecution, and judgment for readers whose visible world seems to contradict divine sovereignty.

Antiochus IV and the Crisis Behind the Visions

The later visions fit especially closely with events surrounding Antiochus IV Epiphanes in the 160s BCE, when Seleucid policies and internal Jewish conflicts culminated in suppression of traditional worship, desecration of the Jerusalem temple, and persecution. Daniel 11 tracks struggles among Hellenistic kingdoms with unusual detail before moving beyond clear historical correspondence at its end, a major reason scholars locate the composition of the final visions during the crisis itself. The book’s symbolic periods—such as “a time, two times, and half a time”—communicate that persecution is intense but limited. Evil does not receive endless time. Daniel’s audience is therefore encouraged to endure rather than calculate an exact modern timetable. The heavenly interpretation says that present suffering belongs within a larger conflict and that oppressive rulers who appear invincible are already bounded by divine decree. Apocalyptic hope is resistance against the claim that the persecutor owns the future.

Resurrection and Final Vindication

Daniel 12 contains one of the Hebrew Bible’s clearest statements of resurrection: many who sleep in the dust will awake, some to everlasting life and others to shame. This hope emerges in a context where faithful people can be killed before justice is visible in history. Earlier biblical traditions often emphasize national restoration, long life, or Sheol; Daniel articulates a more explicit form of postmortem vindication. The wise who lead many to righteousness will shine like stars. This development became foundational for later Jewish apocalyptic thought and for early Christian belief about resurrection and final judgment. The New Testament repeatedly draws on Daniel’s language, including “Son of Man,” the “abomination of desolation,” resurrection, heavenly judgment, and kingdom imagery. Revelation is especially indebted to Daniel’s beasts and angelic visions. Christian interpretation should recognize this continuity while respecting Daniel as a Jewish work of resistance and hope before it became Christian Scripture.

Why Daniel Matters

Daniel matters whenever believers must negotiate life under powers they cannot control. Its court tales teach faithful presence: one can learn, work, serve, and even prosper within a foreign system without granting that system ultimate allegiance. Its visions teach faithful resistance: brutal empires may appear permanent, but from heaven they are beasts whose time is limited. The book also warns against turning apocalyptic hope into sensational date-setting. Daniel’s symbols arose to sustain communities under real persecution, not to provide an endless codebook for identifying modern politicians. Its deepest claims are simpler and more demanding: God judges arrogant power, faithful witness can be costly, prayer and wisdom matter, and death itself will not prevent final vindication. For Christians, Daniel provides essential language for Jesus’ kingdom proclamation and the New Testament’s eschatology. Read responsibly, it produces endurance and courage rather than panic, conspiracy, or speculative certainty.

Annotated Bibliography (Chicago Style)

  • Collins, John J. Daniel: A Commentary on the Book of Daniel. Hermeneia. Minneapolis: Fortress Press, 1993. Standard critical commentary on language, court tales, apocalyptic visions, Antiochus IV, and resurrection.
  • Goldingay, John E. Daniel. Word Biblical Commentary 30. Dallas: Word Books, 1989. Major exegetical commentary combining literary, historical, linguistic, and theological analysis.
  • Newsom, Carol A., with Brennan W. Breed. Daniel: A Commentary. Old Testament Library. Louisville: Westminster John Knox Press, 2014. Contemporary literary and historical commentary attentive to empire, identity, and apocalyptic imagination.
  • Seow, C. L. Daniel. Westminster Bible Companion. Louisville: Westminster John Knox Press, 2003. Accessible scholarly commentary emphasizing diaspora faithfulness, symbolism, and historical context.
  • Hartman, Louis F., and Alexander A. Di Lella. The Book of Daniel. Anchor Bible 23. Garden City, NY: Doubleday, 1978. Detailed commentary on Hebrew and Aramaic texts, historical setting, additions, and apocalyptic traditions.
  • Lucas, Ernest C. Daniel. Apollos Old Testament Commentary 20. Downers Grove, IL: InterVarsity Press, 2002. Evangelical scholarly commentary integrating historical research, literary analysis, and theological interpretation.
  • Hayes, Christine. “Visions of the End: Daniel and Apocalyptic Literature.” Open Yale Courses, RLST 145, Lecture 23. oyc.yale.edu. Accessed August 28, 2026. Academic introduction to Daniel’s setting, apocalyptic genre, historical symbolism, and eschatological hope.
  • Collins, John J. The Apocalyptic Imagination: An Introduction to Jewish Apocalyptic Literature. 3rd ed. Grand Rapids: Eerdmans, 2016. Broad study of apocalyptic genre and worldview that places Daniel within Second Temple Jewish literature.
