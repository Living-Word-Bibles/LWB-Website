Exodus

Overview and Place in Scripture

Exodus is the second book of the Torah and one of the Bible’s defining narratives of deliverance, covenant, worship, and divine presence. Its English title comes from the Greek word for “departure,” referring to Israel’s escape from slavery in Egypt. The Hebrew title, Shemot, “Names,” comes from the book’s opening words. Exodus begins by recalling the sons of Jacob who entered Egypt and then moves quickly to a new Pharaoh who enslaves their descendants. The book follows Moses from his birth and calling through the plagues, Passover, crossing of the sea, covenant at Sinai, golden calf, and construction of the tabernacle. Later biblical writers, Jewish liturgy, Christian theology, and the language of salvation repeatedly return to this defining memory of deliverance.

Literary Structure

Exodus can be read in three large movements. Chapters 1–15 narrate Israel’s oppression, Moses’s call, the confrontation with Pharaoh, the plagues, Passover, and the crossing of the sea. Chapters 16–24 describe the wilderness journey to Sinai and the establishment of covenant, including the Ten Commandments and the Covenant Code. Chapters 25–40 focus heavily on the tabernacle, priestly worship, the golden calf, covenant renewal, and the completion of the sanctuary. The book therefore moves from liberation to covenant and from covenant to worship: Israel is freed from Pharaoh to become a people ordered around God’s presence.

Authorship, Tradition, and Composition

Jewish and Christian tradition associates the Torah with Moses, and Exodus presents Moses as the central mediator of revelation, law, and covenant. The book also contains passages in which Moses writes particular words or records stages of the journey. Exodus as a whole, however, does not identify a single author. Modern scholarship generally sees the book as a composite work shaped from older traditions and literary sources over time. Classic source criticism identified Yahwistic, Elohistic, and Priestly materials, while more recent scholars have revised or challenged older reconstructions. Repeated call scenes, differing divine names, variations in plague traditions, and distinct styles in narrative and priestly instruction contribute to these discussions. Whatever model is adopted, the final form of Exodus intentionally joins liberation, law, covenant, and sanctuary into one theological narrative.

Moses and the Divine Name

Moses dominates Exodus, but the book repeatedly emphasizes that the true actor is God. Moses is born under a royal decree intended to kill Hebrew boys, preserved through the courage of women, raised in Pharaoh’s household, and driven into Midian after killing an Egyptian. At the burning bush he encounters the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob and receives the commission to return to Egypt. Exodus 3 is especially important for the revelation associated with the divine name YHWH and the enigmatic declaration often rendered “I AM THAT I AM” in the KJV. Moses repeatedly objects that he is inadequate, and Aaron is appointed to assist him. His calling therefore becomes a pattern of reluctant leadership sustained by divine promise rather than personal confidence.

Plagues, Passover, and the Sea

The conflict with Pharaoh is both political and theological. Pharaoh refuses to release an enslaved people, and the plague narratives display escalating judgment against Egypt. The hardening of Pharaoh’s heart is described in several ways: Pharaoh hardens his own heart, his heart becomes hard, and God hardens it. The narrative thus wrestles with human obstinacy and divine judgment without reducing the tension to a simple formula. The final plague, the death of the firstborn, is linked with the institution of Passover, in which Israel marks its doors with blood and eats a hurried meal of lamb, unleavened bread, and bitter herbs. The crossing of the sea then becomes the decisive act of deliverance. Exodus 15 celebrates the victory in poetic form, praising God as the one who overthrows oppressive power and brings his people toward his dwelling.

Sinai, Covenant, and Law

At Sinai the liberated slaves are constituted as a covenant people. Exodus 19 describes Israel as a “kingdom of priests” and a “holy nation,” language that later Scripture will reuse. The Ten Commandments set out core obligations toward God and neighbor, while Exodus 21–23 applies covenant order to cases involving servants, property, violence, restitution, courts, festivals, and social responsibility. Some regulations assume ancient institutions, including slavery and patriarchal household structures, that Christians today do not simply reproduce. Yet the laws repeatedly limit power and protect the resident foreigner, widow, orphan, and poor. In Exodus, law is given after deliverance; obedience is the covenant response to grace, not the cause of Israel’s rescue.

The Golden Calf and Covenant Renewal

The golden calf episode in Exodus 32–34 interrupts the sanctuary instructions and exposes the fragility of Israel’s covenant loyalty. While Moses remains on the mountain, the people ask Aaron to make a visible god or divine image, and Aaron fashions a calf. Moses intercedes, the tablets are broken, judgment follows, and the future of the covenant appears uncertain. Yet the story moves toward renewal rather than abandonment. Moses asks to know God’s ways and to see God’s glory, and Exodus 34 presents a foundational description of the Lord as merciful and gracious, patient, abundant in steadfast love and faithfulness, yet also just. That confession becomes one of the Old Testament’s most frequently echoed statements about God’s character.

Tabernacle, Priesthood, and Presence

Modern readers sometimes find the detailed tabernacle instructions in Exodus 25–31 and 35–40 difficult, but they are essential to the book’s purpose. The tabernacle is a portable sanctuary organized around the conviction that the holy God intends to dwell among Israel. Its ark, curtains, altar, lampstand, priestly garments, incense, and ritual furnishings are described with deliberate precision. The repeated statement that the people built the sanctuary “as the LORD commanded Moses” contrasts with the disobedience of the golden calf. When the work is complete, the divine glory fills the tabernacle so fully that Moses cannot enter. Exodus therefore ends with the problem of God’s presence answered, but not fully resolved: God is among the people, yet holiness creates boundaries that the sacrificial and priestly system of Leviticus will address.

Historical Questions and Ancient Context

The historical setting of the Exodus remains debated. Scholars have proposed dates in the fifteenth or thirteenth centuries BCE and have compared the narrative with Egyptian geography, Semitic populations in Egypt, forced labor, New Kingdom building projects, and later Israelite memory. No Egyptian inscription has been found that simply narrates the biblical Exodus, and archaeology does not provide a straightforward confirmation of a mass migration on the scale a literal reading might suggest. At the same time, the story preserves authentic Egyptian names, settings, and social features, and Israel’s own tradition consistently remembers a deliverance from Egypt. Historical study should distinguish the power of Israel’s remembered deliverance from claims that exceed the available evidence.

Exodus in Jewish and Christian Faith

In Judaism, the Exodus stands at the heart of Passover and Israel’s identity as a people redeemed from slavery. The command to remember Egypt becomes an ethical reason to protect the stranger and the vulnerable. Christian writers read the Exodus typologically: Jesus’s death occurs in a Passover setting, Paul calls Christ “our passover,” baptism is compared with passage through the sea, and Revelation borrows imagery from the plagues and the song of Moses. These Christian readings do not erase the Jewish meaning of Exodus; they arise from a Jewish scriptural world in which deliverance from Egypt already defined God’s saving character. For modern readers, Exodus remains a book about liberation and responsibility, divine holiness and mercy, worship and justice. Its enduring question is not only how a people gets free, but what freedom is for: covenant faithfulness, holy community, and life in the presence of God.

Annotated Bibliography (Chicago Style)

  • Sarna, Nahum M. Exodus: The Traditional Hebrew Text with New JPS Translation. JPS Torah Commentary. Philadelphia: Jewish Publication Society, 1991. A foundational Jewish commentary emphasizing Hebrew philology, the ancient Near Eastern background, Passover, covenant, law, and the tabernacle.
  • Childs, Brevard S. The Book of Exodus: A Critical, Theological Commentary. Old Testament Library. Philadelphia: Westminster Press, 1974. A classic commentary that combines historical-critical study with attention to the canonical and theological shape of Exodus.
  • Dozeman, Thomas B. Commentary on Exodus. Eerdmans Critical Commentary. Grand Rapids: Eerdmans, 2009. A comprehensive modern commentary covering literary development, historical traditions, legal material, ritual texts, and theological interpretation.
  • Meyers, Carol. Exodus. New Cambridge Bible Commentary. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2005. Particularly useful for social history, gender, archaeology, household life, and the cultural setting of Israel’s remembered deliverance from Egypt.
  • Propp, William H. C. Exodus 1–18: A New Translation with Introduction and Commentary. Anchor Bible 2. New York: Doubleday, 1999; and Exodus 19–40. Anchor Bible 2A. New York: Doubleday, 2006. Extensive philological and historical commentary on the full book.
  • Collins, John J. Introduction to the Hebrew Bible. 3rd ed. Minneapolis: Fortress Press, 2018. Provides a concise academic orientation to Exodus within the Pentateuch and to modern discussion of the Exodus tradition, law, and covenant.
  • Yale Bible Study. “The Book of Exodus.” Yale Divinity School. yalebiblestudy.org/courses/exodus/. Accessed August 28, 2026. Free scholarly material on the patriarchal transition, Moses, the plagues, source traditions, Sinai, law, and the golden calf.
  • Hoffmeier, James K. Israel in Egypt: The Evidence for the Authenticity of the Exodus Tradition. New York: Oxford University Press, 1996. A detailed archaeological and Egyptological argument for taking the Egyptian setting and historical memory of Exodus seriously, useful alongside more skeptical historical reconstructions.
