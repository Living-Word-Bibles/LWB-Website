Bible Study: John 1:1

John 1:1 opens the Fourth Gospel with words designed to echo the beginning of Genesis: “In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God.” Unlike Matthew and Luke, John does not begin with Jesus’ birth. Unlike Mark, it does not begin with John the Baptist. John begins before creation itself, placing Jesus’ identity inside a cosmic frame.

The phrase “In the beginning” immediately recalls Genesis 1:1. Yale Bible Study emphasizes that the Prologue uses God’s creative word to frame the significance of Jesus. In Genesis, God creates by speaking. In John, the “Word” through whom all things come into being is eventually identified as the one who “became flesh” and lived among us.

The Greek term translated “Word” is logos. It can mean word, speech, message, account, or reason. Greek philosophical traditions used logos in discussions of rational order, while Jewish Scripture and wisdom traditions spoke of God creating by His word and of divine Wisdom present in God’s works. John’s language can resonate with both worlds without being reducible to either one.

“The Word was with God” preserves distinction. The Word is related to God rather than simply identical with the Father as a person. The Greek phrase can suggest orientation toward God—an intimate relationship. John’s later narrative repeatedly presents Jesus as coming from the Father, speaking what He hears from the Father, and returning to the Father.

Then the Prologue makes its strongest claim: “the Word was God.” The Gospel does not wait until the resurrection to introduce a high view of Christ. From the first sentence, the reader is told that the one who becomes Jesus of Nazareth participates in divine identity.

This verse became foundational in Christian debates about the Trinity and the divinity of Christ. Early Christians wrestled with how the Word could be “with God” and also “was God.” The Nicene tradition eventually confessed the Son as truly divine without collapsing Father and Son into one person. John 1:1 did not by itself produce later creedal formulas, but it was central to the church’s theological reflection.

The grammar has also been debated, especially the final clause. Because the Greek word for God lacks the article in that clause, some have argued that the Word should be translated “a god.” Mainstream Greek scholarship generally rejects that conclusion. The grammatical construction emphasizes the quality or nature of the Word rather than identifying the Word as the same person as “the God” mentioned in the previous clause.

John’s claim is not abstract metaphysics for its own sake. The rest of the Prologue explains why the Word matters: all things come through Him, life is in Him, His life is light, He enters the world, He becomes flesh, and He makes God known. Christology serves revelation and salvation.

The verse also establishes continuity between creation and redemption. The one Christians meet in Jesus is not a late divine response to a failed world. The Word belongs to God’s creative purpose from the beginning. Salvation in John is therefore not an escape from creation but the Creator’s life entering creation.

This helps explain the Gospel’s recurring signs involving material life—water, wine, bread, eyes, bodies, graves, fish, gardens. The Word becomes flesh rather than remaining distant from matter. John’s cosmic theology becomes embodied.

For discipleship, John 1:1 calls Christians to let Jesus define their understanding of God. The Gospel will later say that the Son “declares” or makes the Father known. Christian faith does not begin with an abstract deity and then add Jesus as one teacher among others. John presents Jesus as the incarnate Word through whom God is revealed.

The verse also calls for humility. If the Word through whom creation exists has entered history, Christian worship concerns a mystery larger than human categories. Yet John insists that this mystery has become knowable through relationship, witness, signs, words, death, and resurrection.

John 1:1 therefore functions as the Gospel’s theological doorway. Before any miracle is performed or disciple is called, the reader knows who stands behind the story: the eternal Word, in relationship with God, fully sharing divine identity, and soon to enter the world in flesh. Everything that follows asks what it means to encounter that Word in Jesus.

The Prologue’s first line also gives readers information most characters in the Gospel do not yet possess. That dramatic advantage shapes every encounter that follows.

Nicodemus, the Samaritan woman, the crowds, and even the disciples often struggle to understand who Jesus is. The reader, by contrast, begins with the cosmic claim that the one speaking and acting in the narrative is the eternal Word. That dramatic advantage shapes the entire Gospel.

Bibliography & Further Reading

  • The Holy Bible, King James Version — John 1:1–18. Primary text of the Johannine Prologue.
  • Yale Bible Study, “The Gospel of John → Prologue — Study Guide”. Academic treatment of Logos, creation, light and darkness, incarnation, and Jesus as revealer.
  • Working Preacher, “Commentary on John 1:[1–9], 10–18”. Explores creation, life, light, incarnation, and John’s theology of abundant life.
  • Raymond E. Brown, The Gospel According to John I–XII, Anchor Bible 29 (Doubleday, 1966). Classic historical and theological commentary on the Fourth Gospel and its Prologue.
  • Craig S. Keener, The Gospel of John: A Commentary, 2 vols. (Baker Academic, 2003). Extensive commentary on John’s Jewish and Greco-Roman background, Logos language, and Christology.
