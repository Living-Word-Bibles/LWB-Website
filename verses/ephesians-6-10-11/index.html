Bible Study: Ephesians 6:10-11

Ephesians 6:10–11 opens the letter’s famous description of the armor of God: “Finally, my brethren, be strong in the Lord, and in the power of his might. Put on the whole armour of God, that ye may be able to stand against the wiles of the devil.” The passage is often imagined as a solitary believer preparing for private spiritual combat. Yet the commands are plural. Ephesians is addressing the church as a body. The strength believers need is shared strength rooted in the Lord rather than individual bravado.

“Be strong in the Lord” sets the theological order. Christians are not instructed to generate spiritual power from within themselves. Earlier in Ephesians, divine power is the power that raised Christ from the dead, seated Him above every rule and authority, made spiritually dead people alive, and created one reconciled people from Jews and Gentiles. The final exhortation draws on that same power. Christian strength is participation in what God has already accomplished in Christ.

The phrase “in the power of his might” intensifies the emphasis. Ephesians has repeatedly spoken about power, but it has also redefined what power looks like. Christ’s victory comes through the cross, the place of apparent weakness and shame. The church therefore must not read this verse as permission to imitate worldly domination. The power of God in Ephesians creates reconciliation, peace, forgiveness, truthfulness, and a new humanity.

“Put on the whole armour of God” echoes earlier clothing imagery in the letter. Ephesians 4 tells believers to “put off” the old self and “put on” the new self created in righteousness and holiness. The armor is therefore not an unrelated collection of mystical equipment. It is another way of describing the new life already commanded: truth, righteousness, peace, faith, salvation, God’s word, and prayer.

The source of the imagery is also important. Ephesians draws on Isaiah, especially passages where God Himself appears as a divine warrior clothed in righteousness and salvation. The church is not inventing its own weapons. It is taking up qualities that belong to God’s righteous rule. This is why the armor is moral and theological rather than violent.

The purpose is “that ye may be able to stand.” Standing is repeated throughout the paragraph. The emphasis is defensive and persevering. The church is not told to conquer human opponents in Christ’s name. It is told to resist forces that would make it abandon truth, peace, love, holiness, and faith. Christian warfare in this passage is primarily the struggle to remain faithful.

The opponent’s “wiles” are schemes, strategies, or deceptive methods. Evil does not always appear as obvious brutality. It can work through lies, fear, resentment, pride, division, despair, exploitation, and the misuse of authority. Ephesians has spent several chapters teaching the church how to live as one reconciled body; the devil’s schemes therefore include anything that tears that body apart or replaces Christ’s pattern with hostility.

This passage has sometimes been misused to demonize human opponents. Verse 12 immediately warns against that error by saying the struggle is “not against flesh and blood.” Christians should be especially cautious about labeling political rivals, other religions, neighbors, or difficult people as agents to be spiritually attacked. The armor of God does not authorize contempt. It equips believers to resist evil without becoming evil.

The plural address also reminds believers that different members may be strong in different moments. One person may need the community’s truth when confused, another its prayer when exhausted, and another its peace when angry. Shared worship, confession, teaching, service, and mutual encouragement are not secondary to spiritual warfare; they are among the means by which the church is strengthened in the Lord.

For discipleship, verses 10–11 offer a balanced approach to spiritual struggle. Christians should not be naïve about evil, but neither should they become obsessed with it. Their attention is directed first to the Lord, then to the armor He provides, then to standing firm. Strength means continuing to tell the truth, practice righteousness, pursue peace, trust God, receive salvation, live by Scripture, and pray. The church stands not because it is invulnerable, but because the risen Christ is stronger than the forces that seek to deform its life.

This passage also belongs to the larger argument of the book, so faithful interpretation should keep the immediate verse connected to the gospel, the community, and the surrounding commands. Reading it in context protects the text from becoming a slogan and helps its theological force shape ordinary Christian practice.

  • Yale Bible Study, “A Militant Metaphor”. Yale Divinity School discussion of Ephesians 6:10–20, cosmology, martial imagery, incarceration, and power.
