Bible Study: Deuteronomy 6:6-7

Deuteronomy 6:6–7 belongs to one of the most important passages in Jewish and Christian Scripture. It follows the Shema: “Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God is one LORD: and thou shalt love the LORD thy God with all thine heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy might.” Then Moses says that these words are to be in the heart and taught diligently to children, spoken about at home, on the road, at bedtime, and upon rising. Faith is meant to inhabit everyday life.

The first movement is inward: “these words … shall be in thine heart.” In biblical anthropology, the heart is the center of thought, intention, desire, and decision. Deuteronomy does not envision faithful teaching as the transfer of information by people whose own lives are untouched. The words must first shape the teacher. Formation precedes instruction.

The next command is generational: “thou shalt teach them diligently unto thy children.” The Hebrew language can carry the sense of sharpening or impressing the words through repetition. Covenant memory must be intentionally passed on. Israel’s future depends on a new generation knowing who God is, what He has done, and how His people are called to live.

The verse then moves through the rhythms of an ordinary day: sitting in the house, walking by the way, lying down, and rising up. The list is comprehensive. Torah instruction is not limited to formal worship or specialized religious classrooms. Faith is discussed while life is happening. Meals, travel, family conversations, work, rest, and daily routines become settings for remembering God.

This is especially important in Deuteronomy because Israel is preparing to enter a land of prosperity. Moses repeatedly warns that comfort can produce forgetfulness. People may enjoy houses they did not build, wells they did not dig, and vineyards they did not plant and then forget the Lord who delivered them from Egypt. Teaching children is therefore part of resistance to spiritual amnesia.

Jewish tradition has embodied Deuteronomy 6 in the regular recitation of the Shema and in practices associated with mezuzot and tefillin. Christians should respect that living Jewish context rather than treating the passage only as raw material for Christian application. Jesus Himself quotes the Shema as the greatest commandment, showing continuity with Israel’s confession of covenant love.

For Christian families, the passage has often encouraged parents to teach Scripture at home. That remains a strong application, but the text should not be used to shame parents whose children struggle with faith or whose circumstances are complicated. Faithful teaching is responsibility, not control over another person’s eventual choices. Nor is spiritual formation the task of parents alone. Churches, extended families, mentors, and whole communities participate in passing on faith.

The passage also challenges churches that outsource discipleship entirely to programs. Formal classes can be useful, but Deuteronomy imagines formation through a way of life. Children learn not only from what adults say about God but from how adults handle money, conflict, hospitality, work, forgiveness, grief, and gratitude. The “words” are taught when the household’s patterns align with them.

Modern technology makes the command newly relevant. Daily life is filled with voices competing for attention. Screens, advertising, entertainment, and social media can occupy the moments once left for conversation and reflection. Deuteronomy does not prescribe one modern method, but its principle is clear: communities must create repeated practices that keep God’s word present amid competing narratives.

For discipleship, Deuteronomy 6:6–7 asks whether Scripture has a home in the ordinary schedule. The goal is not nonstop religious speech or forced conversation. It is a life in which God’s word naturally appears because it has taken root in the heart. Teaching then becomes more than recitation. It is memory, conversation, example, worship, and repeated attention—faith woven into waking, walking, resting, and rising.

The command therefore joins memory and love. Israel is not asked to teach disconnected facts but the words of the covenant relationship summed up in loving the Lord with heart, soul, and might. Christian teaching is strongest when knowledge of Scripture and visible love of God remain together.

The passage also reveals that formation depends on repetition. Modern readers sometimes assume repetition is shallow, but Deuteronomy treats repeated hearing as essential because forgetfulness is one of Israel’s great spiritual dangers. Repetition does not mean mindless recitation. It means returning often enough to God’s words that they begin to shape instinct, vocabulary, priorities, and memory. Families and churches can practice this through regular Scripture reading, prayer, worship, conversation, and simple habits that make the biblical story familiar over a lifetime.

Bibliography & Further Reading

  • The Holy Bible, King James Version — Deuteronomy 6:1–9. Primary text of the Shema and commands to teach.
  • Yale Bible Study, “Deuteronomy Part 2 — Study Guide”. Discusses the Shema, covenant loyalty, love language, and treaty context.
  • Jeffrey H. Tigay, Deuteronomy, JPS Torah Commentary (Jewish Publication Society, 1996). Major Jewish commentary on the Shema and Deuteronomy’s covenant theology.
  • Patrick D. Miller, Deuteronomy, Interpretation (Westminster John Knox, 1990). Theological commentary on covenant love, memory, teaching, and obedience.
  • Moshe Weinfeld, Deuteronomy 1–11, Anchor Bible 5 (Doubleday, 1991). Foundational historical and literary study of Deuteronomy’s first eleven chapters.
