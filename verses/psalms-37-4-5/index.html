Bible Study: Psalms 37:4-5

Psalm 37:4–5 says, “Delight thyself also in the LORD; and he shall give thee the desires of thine heart. Commit thy way unto the LORD; trust also in him; and he shall bring it to pass.” These verses are sometimes quoted as though God promises to deliver whatever a believer strongly desires. Psalm 37’s larger argument is different. It addresses the frustration of watching wrongdoers prosper and teaches the righteous not to become consumed by envy, anger, or imitation of their methods.

Yale Bible Study classifies Psalm 37 as a wisdom psalm. Like Proverbs, it reflects on the fate of the righteous and wicked and offers instruction about how to live when experience seems to challenge the moral order.

The psalm begins, “Fret not thyself because of evildoers, neither be thou envious against the workers of iniquity.” The problem is not merely private desire. The speaker is wrestling with apparent injustice: people doing wrong seem to flourish.

Working Preacher highlights this context and explicitly warns against prosperity readings of verse 4. Read alone, “he shall give thee the desires of thine heart” can sound like a formula for material gain. Read within the psalm, it belongs to instruction about trusting God rather than copying the wicked.

“Delight thyself also in the LORD” therefore reorders desire. The command comes before the promise. The heart is not treated as a fixed source of perfectly reliable wishes. Desire itself is being formed through delight in God.

This matters because human beings can desire things that would harm themselves or others. Wealth, revenge, recognition, control, or a particular relationship can become consuming. Psalm 37 does not promise divine fulfillment of disordered desire.

Instead, delight in God gradually reshapes what the heart values. The person who learns God’s justice and faithfulness may begin to desire truth, peace, generosity, courage, and righteousness more deeply.

Verse 5 continues, “Commit thy way unto the LORD.” Like Proverbs 16:3, the language of committing suggests entrusting the course of life to God. The person still has a “way,” but does not treat that way as sovereign.

“Trust also in him; and he shall bring it to pass” again places agency with God. The psalmist is trying to free readers from frantic attempts to secure justice through envy or imitation of evil.

The following verses speak of God bringing righteousness to light and judgment into visibility. The promise is therefore connected with vindication and moral order more than with private acquisition.

Psalm 37 repeatedly tells the reader to wait. “Rest in the LORD, and wait patiently for him.” Waiting is difficult precisely because the wicked appear successful now. Wisdom refuses to judge God’s faithfulness solely by the present moment.

This should also be read with humility. The psalm contains strong confidence that wrongdoing will not finally prosper, but wisdom literature elsewhere, especially Job and Ecclesiastes, complicates simplistic retribution. Scripture allows believers to confess trust while admitting that justice is not always visible within a lifetime.

The verse therefore should not be used to tell suffering people that every righteous desire will eventually be granted in the exact form imagined. Faithful people sometimes die with hopes unfulfilled.

Nor should it become a technique for “manifesting” desires through positive emotion. The power in the text belongs to the Lord, not to the psychological intensity of the believer.

For discipleship, Psalm 37:4–5 asks Christians to examine what they envy. When people who act dishonestly gain money, influence, or attention, resentment can slowly make their methods attractive. The psalm offers another path: delight in God, commit the way, trust, wait, and continue doing good.

The passage also invites believers to bring desires honestly before God. Trust does not require pretending not to want things. Prayer can name desire while remaining open to transformation: “Lord, this is what I want. If the desire is disordered, change it. If the path is wrong, redirect me.”

Delight itself can be practiced through worship, gratitude, Scripture, prayer, service, and attention to God’s character. Over time, what a person repeatedly delights in shapes what the person wants.

For churches, the psalm is a warning against measuring faithfulness by visible success. Large budgets, crowds, influence, or cultural prestige do not prove righteousness. A community can prosper outwardly while learning the methods of the very evildoers the psalm says not to envy.

Psalm 37:4–5 therefore is not a blank check for human desire. It is a wisdom path for people tempted by the apparent success of wrongdoing. Delight in God. Entrust the road to God. Trust instead of imitating injustice. As desire becomes formed by that relationship, the heart learns to want what belongs to a faithful life.

Bibliography & Further Reading

  • The Holy Bible, King James Version — Psalm 37:1–11. Primary context of envy, evildoers, trust, delight, commitment, patience, and meekness.
  • Working Preacher, “Commentary on Psalm 37:1–9”. Explicitly rejects prosperity-gospel use of Psalm 37:4 and interprets it within the problem of wicked prosperity.
  • Yale Bible Study, “Wisdom Psalms — Study Guide”. Classifies Psalm 37 as wisdom and discusses its reflection on the apparent prosperity of the wicked.
  • James L. Mays, Psalms, Interpretation (Westminster John Knox, 1994). Theological commentary on the Psalms as prayer, praise, lament, trust, and instruction.
  • John Goldingay, Psalms, 3 vols. (Baker Academic, 2006–2008). Detailed literary, historical, and theological commentary on the Psalter.
