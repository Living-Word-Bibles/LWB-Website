Bible Study: Luke 6:31

Luke 6:31 gives the saying commonly called the Golden Rule: “And as ye would that men should do to you, do ye also to them likewise.” The sentence is easy to memorize and appears simple enough to stand alone. Yet Luke places it in one of Jesus’ most demanding teachings: love your enemies, do good to those who hate you, bless those who curse you, pray for those who mistreat you, give generously, and imitate the mercy of God.

The verse belongs to Luke’s “Sermon on the Plain” or “Sermon on the Level Place.” Jesus has just pronounced blessings on the poor, hungry, grieving, and rejected, followed by woes to the rich, full, laughing, and well-spoken-of. The ethical teaching that follows describes how the community of Jesus should live inside a fractured world.

Working Preacher emphasizes that Luke 6:27–38 is not a random collection of moral maxims. The instructions are examples of mercy in action. Jesus grounds the entire section in the character of God: “Be ye therefore merciful, as your Father also is merciful.”

The Golden Rule therefore belongs to a theology of imitation. Disciples treat others well not merely because reciprocity is socially useful but because God’s mercy gives their relationships a new pattern.

Versions of the Golden Rule existed in other ancient traditions, sometimes in negative form: do not do to another what you would not want done to you. Jesus’ positive wording emphasizes action. Disciples do not merely avoid harm; they actively seek another person’s good.

The immediate context makes the rule especially demanding because the “others” include enemies. It is easy to treat friends as we would like friends to treat us. Jesus explicitly says even sinners love those who love them. Kingdom-shaped mercy appears when believers refuse to let hostility determine the moral quality of their response.

This does not mean all relationships become identical. Treating another person as you would want to be treated requires wisdom about what genuinely serves the other person’s good. A parent, child, employee, stranger, friend, and dangerous aggressor may require different forms of response.

The verse also does not require enabling abuse. If a person were harming others, most people would ultimately want someone to interrupt that destructive behavior. Setting boundaries, seeking help, reporting violence, or limiting contact can therefore be compatible with the deeper logic of the rule.

Nor does love of enemies eliminate justice. Luke’s Gospel contains prophetic criticism of exploitation and misuse of wealth. Mercy seeks the good of the wrongdoer, but genuine good can include accountability, repentance, restitution, and protection of victims.

The Golden Rule also challenges self-centered moral imagination. It asks a person to leave the narrow world of personal desire long enough to imagine life from another’s position. What would I want if I were the one who was hungry, ignored, insulted, powerless, frightened, or misunderstood?

At the same time, the phrase “as ye would” requires mature self-understanding. People can desire unhealthy things for themselves and therefore cannot make personal preference the only ethical standard. The wider teaching of Jesus, especially God’s mercy, love of neighbor, truth, justice, and the kingdom, helps define what “good” treatment means.

The communal setting is also important. Working Preacher reads Luke’s sermon as instruction for a witnessing community. When Christians practice mercy in conflict, they display the character of God’s kingdom before the surrounding world.

That makes the verse relevant to political and social disagreement. Christians may hold strong convictions without deciding that opponents deserve contempt, humiliation, or lies. The Golden Rule asks how believers would want their own motives represented, their own words quoted, and their own dignity preserved during disagreement.

The same principle applies economically. If believers would want generosity during genuine need, fair wages for their labor, truthful contracts, and humane treatment, then they should seek those realities for others rather than protecting them only for themselves.

For discipleship, Luke 6:31 can become a daily question before speech and action: If I were in the other person’s place, what response would reflect truth, mercy, and dignity? That question slows impulsive retaliation and opens space for neighbor-love.

The verse is not a complete ethical system, but within Jesus’ sermon it becomes a powerful summary of relational mercy. Christians treat others in kingdom-shaped ways because they themselves live by mercy. The Golden Rule is therefore more than politeness. It is one practical expression of becoming “merciful, as your Father also is merciful.”

Because Luke places the rule beside enemy-love, its most radical use may occur precisely where reciprocity has broken down. The disciple seeks another’s good without first demanding that the other prove worthy.

Bibliography & Further Reading

  • The Holy Bible, King James Version — Luke 6:27–38. Primary context of enemy-love, mercy, the Golden Rule, non-retaliation, and generosity.
  • Working Preacher, “Commentary on Luke 6:27–38”. Places the Golden Rule within the Sermon on the Level Place and God’s mercy toward enemies.
  • Yale Bible Study, “The Gospel of Luke — Jesus Teaches and Heals”. Academic treatment of Luke’s teaching ministry and the formation of a distinctive disciple community.
  • Joseph A. Fitzmyer, The Gospel According to Luke I–IX, Anchor Bible 28 (Doubleday, 1981). Major commentary on Luke 6, enemy-love, mercy, and the Golden Rule.
  • Joel B. Green, The Gospel of Luke, NICNT (Eerdmans, 1997). Narrative and theological commentary on Luke’s social ethics and disciple formation.
