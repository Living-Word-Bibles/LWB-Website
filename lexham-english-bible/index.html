Overview & Distinctives

The Lexham English Bible (LEB) is a modern English translation produced in the digital Bible-study environment associated with Logos Bible Software and Lexham Press. It occupies a distinctive place among contemporary English Bibles because its central goal is not merely to give readers a polished English rendering, but also to make the relationship between that rendering and the Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek texts unusually visible. The LEB therefore belongs naturally beside interlinears, reverse interlinears, lexicons, textual notes, and other tools used for close biblical study.

The editors describe the translation as one intended to communicate the meaning of the original languages clearly while allowing readers to see how the English text gets there. That purpose explains several of the LEB's most recognizable features: relatively close correspondence to the wording and structure of the source languages, extensive notes, visible marking of idioms, italics for words supplied for English sense, and a willingness to preserve features that other translations sometimes smooth away. Its prose is generally readable, but the LEB does not hide the fact that translation involves interpretation.

This makes the LEB particularly useful for readers who want something more transparent than a highly idiomatic translation but who do not want to read an interlinear as their principal English text. It can function as a normal Bible for reading and devotion, yet its design continually invites comparison with the underlying text. In that respect, the LEB is best understood not simply as another entrant in the English Bible marketplace, but as a translation shaped by the possibilities of digital biblical scholarship.

Origins & Development

The LEB emerged from work on the Lexham Greek-English Interlinear New Testament. According to the LEB preface, that interlinear ancestry was fundamental to the project. The editors wanted an English Bible that could stand alongside the original-language text and remain sufficiently close to it for meaningful comparison. Existing English translations often serve other goals exceptionally well, but their phrasing may move far enough from Hebrew or Greek syntax that a student using an interlinear or original-language tool can have difficulty seeing why a particular English wording was chosen.

The New Testament phase appeared first, with the translation circulating in the Logos ecosystem around 2010. Work then expanded to the Old Testament, and the translation developed through successive digital editions. The surviving front matter available through Biblia identifies the Lexham English Bible, Fourth Edition, published by Lexham Press in 2013, and lists both the editorial team and the translators involved. Earlier copyright lines preserve 2010 and 2012 dates, reflecting the staged development of the text rather than a single one-time publication event.

That development history matters because it reflects the LEB's digital character. A print translation is often experienced through major named editions separated by years or decades. A digital Bible can be corrected, synchronized with reverse interlinears, and redistributed more fluidly. The LEB was born in that environment. Its history therefore resembles the history of software-assisted scholarship as much as the history of a conventional print Bible. The text was meant to live inside searchable libraries where a reader could move directly from an English word to a Greek or Hebrew form, open a lexicon, compare translations, inspect textual notes, and trace grammatical relationships.

Editors, Translators & the Interlinear Background

W. Hall Harris III is closely associated with the LEB and is identified in early material as its general editor. The Fourth Edition credits a broader editorial group including Harris, Elliot Ritzema, Rick Brannan, Douglas Mangum, John Dunham, Jeffrey A. Reimer, and Micah Wierenga. Its list of translators includes Harris, Michael S. Heiser, Jeremy Penner, David M. Fouts, Eugene E. Carpenter, Gordon H. Johnston, H. Daniel Zacharias, William D. Barrick, Michael A. Grisanti, Chip McDaniel, Israel Loken, Ken M. Penner, Dorian G. Coover-Cox, and Amy L. Balogh/Pfeister in surviving edition records.

The interlinear background helps explain the division of labor. Interlinear work demands decisions at a very granular level: what English gloss corresponds to a particular inflected form, how a clause should be segmented, what information belongs in the running English line, and what should remain in notes or annotations. When that material is transformed into a readable Bible, translators must make a second set of judgments about English syntax, idiom, discourse, and style.

The LEB is therefore not a mechanical word-substitution exercise. No responsible Bible translation can be. Hebrew and Greek do not map word-for-word onto English in a consistent way, and even the most formally oriented translation must decide how clauses relate, how ellipsis should be filled out, when an idiom should be rendered idiomatically, and how grammatical information should be represented. What distinguishes the LEB is that it frequently makes those decisions more visible than translations designed primarily for uninterrupted literary reading.

Translation Philosophy: Literal Where Possible, Readable Where Necessary

The LEB is often described as a relatively formal or “literal” translation, but the word literal can mislead if it suggests that translation is simply replacing each source-language word with one English equivalent. The LEB's own preface presents a more careful goal: transparency to the original text combined with intelligible English. That balance is central to its usefulness.

Formal correspondence can preserve repeated vocabulary, clause structure, connective words, verbal relationships, and other features that are important in close study. Yet a translation that reproduces source-language syntax too rigidly can become misleading precisely because English readers may assign a different meaning to the resulting sentence. The LEB therefore sometimes smooths a construction while signaling what happened in a note. This allows the main text to remain functional English without pretending that no interpretive choice occurred.

The translation is especially valuable when comparing versions. A reader may place the LEB beside the KJV, ESV, NASB, NIV, NRSV, NET, or another translation and quickly notice where a more idiomatic version has reorganized a clause or where a traditional English rendering has obscured a repeated source-language expression. Such comparison does not mean the LEB is always “more correct.” Rather, its design exposes a different layer of the translation process.

That distinction is important for Christian readers. Faithful translation involves both accuracy and communication. At times, the most faithful English wording is not the one that looks most like Greek or Hebrew on the page. The LEB's strength lies in allowing readers to observe this tension rather than concealing it. The notes often make clear when a smoother English expression stands over against a more wooden rendering.

Old Testament Text, Ancient Witnesses & the Divine Name

The LEB Old Testament works primarily from the traditional Hebrew text represented by Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia (BHS), a standard scholarly edition of the Masoretic Text. The Masoretic tradition is the principal textual base for most modern translations of the Hebrew Bible, but translators also compare ancient witnesses when the Hebrew is difficult, when manuscripts preserve competing readings, or when an older version appears to reflect a different Hebrew form.

Accordingly, the LEB's notes can draw attention to readings associated with the Dead Sea Scrolls, the Septuagint, the Samaritan Pentateuch, and other ancient witnesses. These are not alternative “Bibles” competing with the Hebrew text so much as evidence for the long history of textual transmission. Their value is especially apparent in passages where an ancient reading may clarify an obscure or damaged form in the medieval Masoretic manuscripts.

One immediately visible characteristic of the LEB Old Testament is its use of Yahweh where many English Bibles traditionally print LORD for the divine name represented by the Hebrew consonants YHWH. This choice makes the personal name visible in the English text rather than replacing it with a title. Readers accustomed to the KJV, ESV, NIV, or similar traditions may notice the difference immediately in the Psalms, prophets, Pentateuch, and historical books.

The choice is both translational and pedagogical. It reminds readers that the Hebrew text distinguishes the divine name from titles such as Adonai (“Lord”) and from the general word elohim (“God/god”). It also makes certain patterns of repetition and wordplay easier to see. At the same time, readers should recognize that “Yahweh” represents a scholarly reconstruction of the pronunciation of the Tetragrammaton and that Jewish reading tradition generally avoids pronouncing the name as written.

The New Testament & the SBL Greek New Testament

The LEB New Testament is closely associated with the Greek New Testament: SBL Edition (SBLGNT), edited by Michael W. Holmes and published in 2010 by the Society of Biblical Literature in association with Logos Bible Software. The SBL describes the SBLGNT as a critically edited Greek New Testament made available in both electronic and print formats. Its production fits naturally with the LEB's digital scholarly setting.

A critical Greek New Testament is not a single ancient manuscript reproduced without alteration. It is an editorial text constructed through comparison of surviving manuscript evidence and earlier critical editions. Holmes evaluated readings using a broad range of scholarly resources. The SBLGNT therefore sometimes differs from the Nestle-Aland/United Bible Societies text that forms the main New Testament base of many other contemporary English translations.

Those differences are normally small and should not be exaggerated. New Testament textual criticism deals with a very large manuscript tradition, and most variation involves spelling, word order, small omissions or additions, and other details that do not overturn Christian doctrine. Where a textual decision matters for translation, the LEB's study-oriented design allows notes to identify alternatives rather than hiding the existence of textual questions.

This relationship to the SBLGNT gives the LEB an especially interesting place among modern English Bibles. It is not merely translated from “the Greek” in a generic sense; its New Testament is connected to a specific modern critical edition developed in the same digital and scholarly ecosystem. Students using the SBLGNT, the Lexham interlinear, and the LEB can therefore move among related resources with unusually tight correspondence.

Transparency: Idioms, Supplied Words, Notes & Textual Decisions

Perhaps the LEB's most distinctive visible convention is its treatment of idioms. The translation uses corner-bracket style markers to identify expressions where the source language employs an idiom that cannot be transferred naturally into English without interpretation. A note can then preserve a more direct representation of the underlying expression. This gives readers both levels: the sense appropriate for the main English sentence and the linguistic form that helps explain how that sense arose.

The LEB also uses italics to mark words supplied in English where no directly corresponding word stands in the source text. Supplying words is unavoidable in translation because Hebrew and Greek frequently omit information that English grammar requires or express relationships through forms English does not possess. Marking such words does not mean they are speculative additions; it means the editors want the reader to know where English syntax required an explicit element.

Footnotes extend the same philosophy. They address translation alternatives, textual variants, Old Testament quotations in the New Testament, and other issues that affect interpretation. In many modern Bibles, footnotes are intentionally sparse so as not to interrupt reading. The LEB takes the opposite approach when transparency is useful. Its notes turn the translation into a compact study resource.

This is particularly valuable for readers beginning Greek or Hebrew. A first-year language student can easily assume that one source-language word must always equal one English word or that an English tense label maps neatly onto Greek verbal categories. The LEB's structure helps undermine those oversimplifications. It shows, repeatedly, that meaning arises from syntax, context, discourse, lexical range, genre, and textual evidence working together.

Reading, Study & Use in the Church

The LEB is at its strongest when used as both a Bible and a study bridge. It can be read devotionally from Genesis to Revelation, but its design particularly rewards readers who compare translations, follow cross-references, inspect notes, or use original-language tools. Pastors and Bible teachers may find it useful when preparing lessons because its phrasing often exposes features that are less obvious in smoother translations.

For public worship, some congregations may prefer a translation with a more established liturgical cadence or broader congregational familiarity. The LEB can occasionally sound more angular because it chooses transparency over literary smoothing. That is not necessarily a weakness; it is a consequence of purpose. A translation optimized for close correspondence will sometimes read differently from one optimized for oral elegance.

Readers should therefore resist ranking Bible translations on a single scale from “literal” to “inaccurate.” Different translations make different responsible tradeoffs. The LEB's contribution is to make those tradeoffs inspectable. It works especially well beside another translation: a reader can hear the flow of a familiar version, consult the LEB for structural transparency, and then examine notes or original-language resources when the two diverge.

Its digital distribution also serves Christians who need a translation that can be incorporated into software, websites, educational projects, and ministry tools under comparatively generous terms. That openness helped the LEB circulate well beyond the Logos application itself and reflects the project’s original digital orientation.

Copyright, Distribution & Licensing

The LEB is copyrighted, not public domain, but its license is unusually permissive. Current published license language allows the text to be given away, while prohibiting sale of the LEB by itself. A larger work may include the LEB under specified conditions, and use beyond particular thresholds can trigger attribution and annual reporting requirements.

Published license summaries state that quotations must be attributed. Fewer than 100 verses in a single work may generally be identified with “(LEB)” after the quotation; longer use requires the fuller attribution notice specified by the license. Works containing more than 1,000 verses, or certain commercial distributions, are subject to additional reporting provisions. Electronic uses are also instructed to link references to the LEB and Logos to the appropriate official sites.

Because licensing terms can change, publishers should consult the current official LEB license or Logos/Lexham permissions information before distributing substantial portions of the text. The important historical point is that the LEB was deliberately licensed for wide circulation while retaining copyright protection. That model matches the translation's broader identity: digitally native, scholarship-oriented, and designed to move readily through modern Bible-study platforms.
