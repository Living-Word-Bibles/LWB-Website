Romans

Read Romans Now

Overview and Historical Occasion

Paul’s Letter to the Romans is the longest and most sustained theological argument among his surviving letters, but it is still an occasional letter written to real communities rather than a detached systematic theology. Paul wrote to believers in Rome whom he had not founded, probably in the mid-to-late 50s CE, as he prepared to carry a collection for Jerusalem and hoped eventually to travel west toward Spain. Romans therefore introduces Paul and his gospel to a strategically important network of house churches. The letter also addresses tensions between Jewish and Gentile believers in the aftermath of imperial disruptions to Jewish life in Rome. Paul’s argument moves from the universal need for God’s righteousness to justification, life in Christ and the Spirit, Israel’s place in God’s purposes, and practical exhortation about communal unity. Its scale and depth have made Romans extraordinarily influential in Christian theology.

Authorship, Delivery, and the Roman Churches

Romans is one of the letters almost universally accepted as authentically Pauline. Paul identifies himself at the beginning, and the closing chapter names a remarkable network of coworkers and acquaintances. Phoebe, a deacon or minister of the church at Cenchreae and a benefactor of many, is commended to the Roman believers and was likely the letter’s carrier, a role that may also have involved publicly reading and explaining it. Priscilla and Aquila appear among the greetings, alongside numerous women and men who hosted or served house churches. This social network reminds readers that Romans was not first encountered as a private theological book but as a performed letter within communities. Emperor Claudius had earlier expelled many Jews from Rome; after his death, returning Jewish believers and predominantly Gentile congregations likely faced practical questions about identity, law, food, sacred days, and authority. These circumstances illuminate Paul’s insistence on mutual welcome.

The Gospel, Sin, and the Righteousness of God

Romans 1:16–17 states Paul’s thesis: the gospel is God’s power for salvation to everyone who believes, to Jew first and also to Greek, because in it the righteousness of God is revealed. Interpreters debate whether “righteousness of God” primarily means God’s own covenant faithfulness, God’s saving action, a righteous status granted to believers, or some combination. Paul’s opening argument then indicts Gentile idolatry and moral disorder before turning the critique against self-confident judges and emphasizing that possession of Torah does not exempt anyone from accountability. The conclusion is universal: all are under sin and cannot boast before God. This argument has sometimes been misused to caricature Judaism as a religion of works opposed to Christian grace. Modern scholarship emphasizes that Paul’s debate takes place within Jewish covenantal categories and asks how God’s promises to Israel relate to the new reality Paul believes has arrived in Christ.

Justification, Abraham, and Grace

Romans 3–5 develops Paul’s language of justification by faith apart from “works of the law.” The phrase has generated centuries of debate. At minimum, Paul argues that God’s saving action in Christ establishes a basis for belonging that does not depend on Gentiles becoming Jews through circumcision and other boundary-defining practices. Abraham becomes the crucial scriptural example because his faith is reckoned as righteousness before circumcision, making him father of both circumcised and uncircumcised believers. The result excludes boasting and emphasizes grace. In Western Christianity, Augustine, Luther, Calvin, Wesley, and many others drew major doctrines of sin, grace, and justification from these chapters. Contemporary interpreters continue to debate how forensic, participatory, covenantal, and apocalyptic dimensions fit together. Romans itself refuses a thin account: justification reconciles enemies, creates hope, and introduces believers into a new sphere of life shaped by Christ.

Adam, Baptism, the Spirit, and New Creation

Romans 5–8 expands salvation beyond acquittal into participation and transformation. Paul contrasts Adam and Christ as representative figures whose actions shape humanity’s condition. Where sin and death reign through Adam, grace and life reign through Christ. Romans 6 uses baptismal imagery to describe believers as united with Christ’s death and resurrection, freed from sin’s dominion, and called to present themselves to God. Romans 7 dramatizes the divided human condition under sin and law, a passage interpreted variously as Paul’s autobiography, Israel’s story, Adamic humanity, or the experience of a person confronted by Torah. Romans 8 then reaches a soaring climax in life through the Spirit. Creation itself groans for liberation; believers groan while awaiting adoption and redemption; the Spirit intercedes; and nothing can separate God’s people from divine love in Christ. Salvation is therefore personal, communal, cosmic, and future-oriented.

Israel, Election, and Romans 9–11

Romans 9–11 confronts a theological crisis created by Paul’s own gospel: if Israel received covenants, Torah, worship, promises, and the Messiah, how can widespread Jewish nonacceptance of Paul’s Christ proclamation be understood without making God unfaithful? Paul wrestles with election through scriptural stories of Isaac and Ishmael, Jacob and Esau, Moses and Pharaoh, the potter and clay, prophetic remnants, and the olive tree. These chapters have sometimes been used to support Christian supersessionism, but Paul’s climax resists the idea that Israel has simply been discarded. Gentile believers are wild branches grafted into Israel’s olive tree and are warned against arrogance. Paul insists that the gifts and calling of God are irrevocable and anticipates a mystery in which “all Israel will be saved.” The precise meaning remains debated, but the ethical demand is clear: Gentile faith gives no warrant for contempt toward Jews.

Christian Ethics: Worship, Government, and the Weak and Strong

Romans 12 marks a shift from extended theological argument to exhortation, though Paul presents ethics as the embodied consequence of mercy. Believers offer their bodies as living sacrifices, resist conformity to the present age, exercise diverse gifts, practice sincere love, bless persecutors, and overcome evil with good. Romans 13’s instruction to be subject to governing authorities has played a complicated role in Christian political thought. It cannot be read apart from Paul’s wider conviction that God alone is ultimate Lord, nor used automatically to sanctify unjust regimes; throughout Christian history it has been interpreted alongside prophetic resistance and apostolic disobedience to commands that contradict God. Romans 14–15 addresses disputes over food and sacred days. Paul refuses to let either “strong” or “weak” believers despise one another. Christlike community requires restraint, welcome, and willingness to surrender legitimate freedom for another’s good.

Interpretive History and Theological Debates

Romans has repeatedly stood at turning points in Christian history. Augustine heard Romans 13 during his conversion; Martin Luther’s reading of righteousness and faith became central to the Protestant Reformation; John Wesley described a transformed assurance while hearing Luther’s preface to Romans; Karl Barth’s commentary helped reshape twentieth-century theology. Modern scholarship has added major debates about Paul’s relationship to Judaism. The “New Perspective on Paul” emphasized covenant, ethnic boundary markers, and the distortion involved in treating Second Temple Judaism as crude legalism. Apocalyptic readings stress God’s invasive deliverance from enslaving powers, while “Paul within Judaism” approaches ask whether Paul envisioned continued Torah observance for Jews alongside Gentile inclusion. These debates show the richness of Romans and the danger of assuming that one historical interpretation exhausts the letter. Careful reading holds together grace, Israel, Christ, Spirit, ethics, and communal reconciliation.

Why Romans Matters

Romans matters because it refuses to separate theology from the creation of a reconciled people. Paul’s grand language about sin, grace, justification, Spirit, election, and hope serves the practical aim of enabling Jewish and Gentile believers to glorify God together. The letter confronts human boasting, insists that salvation is gift, and describes new life as participation in Christ rather than private religious improvement. It also preserves unresolved tensions that keep the church humble: divine sovereignty and human response, Israel’s election and Gentile mission, obedience to authorities and nonconformity to the age, individual conscience and communal responsibility. For Christian readers, Romans offers a vast map of the gospel while warning that doctrine without love contradicts its own message. Its final vision is missionary and doxological: diverse peoples, gathered by mercy, becoming a community whose life displays the faithfulness of God.

Annotated Bibliography (Chicago Style)

  • Jewett, Robert. Romans: A Commentary. Hermeneia. Minneapolis: Fortress Press, 2007. Extensive historical and rhetorical commentary on Paul’s Roman mission, house churches, theology, and social context.
  • Moo, Douglas J. The Letter to the Romans. 2nd ed. New International Commentary on the New Testament. Grand Rapids: Eerdmans, 2018. Major evangelical exegetical commentary on Greek text, justification, law, Spirit, Israel, and ethics.
  • Dunn, James D. G. Romans. 2 vols. Word Biblical Commentary 38A–38B. Dallas: Word Books, 1988. Influential interpretation associated with the New Perspective, emphasizing covenant, law, and Jewish-Gentile relations.
  • Cranfield, C. E. B. A Critical and Exegetical Commentary on the Epistle to the Romans. 2 vols. International Critical Commentary. Edinburgh: T&T Clark, 1975–1979. Classic technical commentary on Greek text and Pauline theology.
  • Gaventa, Beverly Roberts. When in Romans: An Invitation to Linger with the Gospel according to Paul. Grand Rapids: Baker Academic, 2016. Theological reading emphasizing God’s action, cosmic powers, Israel, and the letter’s larger argument.
  • Longenecker, Richard N. The Epistle to the Romans. New International Greek Testament Commentary. Grand Rapids: Eerdmans, 2016. Comprehensive exegetical study of Paul’s argument, Scripture use, and first-century context.
  • Yale Bible Study. “Romans.” yalebiblestudy.org. Accessed August 28, 2026. Academic course on Paul’s occasion, salvation theology, Jewish-Gentile relations, Israel, ethics, and Christian practice.
  • Campbell, Douglas A. The Deliverance of God: An Apocalyptic Rereading of Justification in Paul. Grand Rapids: Eerdmans, 2009. Major and controversial reinterpretation of Romans emphasizing God’s liberating action in Christ.
